ALEX — RED FLAG LIBRARY
CATEGORY 6: GOVERNANCE RED FLAGS
Version 1.0. Built: April 2026.
10 flags. Apply mechanically to every deal submission.
These flags are observable from a submission without a meeting.
Two analysts applying these rules reach the same conclusion.
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION RULE:
For every deal submission, Alex checks all 10 flags before completing
the screening note. Flags are not discretionary — if the signal is
present, the flag is raised. Flags are then weighed in aggregate.
One CRITICAL flag = automatic deep investigation required.
Two or more HIGH flags = note this in the Risk Assessment section.
Governance flags are frequently underweighted by early-stage investors
because they feel like admin problems rather than business problems.
They are not. Governance failures destroy otherwise-good investments:
they prevent exits, trigger legal disputes, reduce acquirer interest,
and give founders leverage they should not have. A governance flag
caught early can be remedied in a week. Caught at Series B, it can
unwind an entire syndicate or block an acquisition. Apply every flag.

FLAG G01: NO VESTING CLIFF ON FOUNDER EQUITY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: CRITICAL

SIGNAL:
The cap table or shareholder agreement shows founders hold fully vested
equity — or the deal materials do not mention vesting schedules at all.
Observable from any cap table or shareholders' agreement excerpt.
Detectable from absence: if a deck presents a cap table with founders
holding large equity stakes and no reference to vesting, cliff, or
good-leaver / bad-leaver provisions, the default assumption is that
equity is fully vested or on non-standard terms.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Unverted or cliff-free founder equity creates the most predictable
governance disaster in early-stage investing: a co-founder departs
after six months, retains 25–40% of the company, contributes nothing
further, and either (a) blocks or complicates future fundraising by
appearing on the cap table as an unengaged holder, (b) demands a
buyout that the company cannot afford, or (c) simply sits on the cap
table as dead weight that dilutes the incentive pool available for
new hires. Without a cliff, even a founder who departs on day one
retains their full equity allocation in many jurisdictions unless
the shareholders' agreement has explicit bad-leaver provisions.
The second-order problem: acquirers run cap table due diligence.
A departed co-founder with 30% equity on non-standard vesting is
a deal-stopper at exit — acquirers will not proceed until the
structure is resolved, and resolving it at exit, under time pressure,
costs the company significantly more than resolving it at founding.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founders have been working together for three or more years, both
remain fully committed and active in day-to-day operations, AND the
shareholders' agreement contains detailed good-leaver / bad-leaver
provisions that effectively replicate the economic outcome of vesting
— i.e., a departing founder's equity is subject to compulsory
transfer at a defined price. Even in this case, the absence of formal
vesting is a flag requiring explicit confirmation that the
good-leaver / bad-leaver provisions are investor-drafted, not
founder-drafted.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Founders acknowledge the gap and agree to implement a standard
4-year vest with 1-year cliff as a condition of the investment round.
(b) Company is a sole founder with no co-founder equity split risk —
vesting still matters for key-person replacement value but the
co-founder departure scenario does not apply.
(c) All founders have been working together for more than five years
with an established track record of the relationship under stress.

VERIFICATION:
Request: shareholders' agreement or term sheet showing vesting schedule
for all founders and senior team equity holders. Specifically look for:
(1) total vesting period (standard: 4 years), (2) cliff length
(standard: 12 months), (3) good-leaver / bad-leaver definitions and
the price at which equity transfers on bad-leaver event, (4) whether
reverse vesting applies to existing shares or only new grants.
Ask: "Can you share the vesting schedule and good-leaver provisions
for all founders? What happens to a co-founder's equity if they leave
within 12 months of our investment?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When founders hold fully vested or cliff-free equity with no disclosed
vesting terms, request the shareholders' agreement before advancing
to due diligence — a co-founder departure on these terms has ended
multiple otherwise-fundable deals and is a structural flaw that cannot
be remediated retroactively without co-founder agreement.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G02: CAP TABLE COMPLEXITY AT EARLY STAGE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
A pre-Series A company's cap table shows more than 15 individual
shareholders, more than 2 share classes, convertible notes from more
than 3 separate investors on differing terms, or any shareholder holding
more than 15% who is unidentified or described only as a "family office"
or "strategic investor" with no further detail. Observable from any
cap table extract, data room summary, or shareholder list in the deck.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Cap table complexity at early stage is almost always the result of
one of four scenarios: (1) the founders raised a seed round via a
friends-and-family round with many small cheques and no professional
lead — common, but creates a shareholder consent management problem
for every future round; (2) the company previously attempted a raise
that partially closed, leaving a messy stub of convertible notes
on non-standard terms — common in companies that struggled to close
their previous round; (3) there is a historic employee or contractor
equity grant that was not properly documented as options — these
create ghost equity holders with uncertain entitlements; (4) a
strategic investor or early customer holds equity as part of a
commercial arrangement — which creates a conflict of interest at exit
if that strategic investor is also a potential acquirer or a competitor
of the most likely acquirer. Complex cap tables increase legal costs
at every subsequent round (each holder may need separate consent),
can create blocking minorities at inconvenient moments, and reduce
acquirer appetite by adding friction to exit structuring.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
There is a shareholder holding more than 10% of the company whose
identity or ultimate beneficial ownership cannot be confirmed — this
moves from a structural governance flag to a potential AML / KYC risk
that requires immediate escalation before any further engagement.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has already appointed a company secretary or law firm
to manage shareholder communications and the cap table is administered
on a recognised platform (Carta, Capdesk, or equivalent) — complexity
is manageable when it is professionally administered.
(b) The complexity is from convertible notes that all convert on the
same event and on substantially the same terms — complexity that
resolves at the next funding event is manageable complexity.
(c) The company has already engaged a law firm to run a cap table
clean-up and the timeline and cost are known.

VERIFICATION:
Request a full cap table showing: each shareholder name, equity class,
percentage holding on a fully diluted basis (including all option pools,
warrants, and convertible instruments), and the vesting status of each
holding. For any convertible notes, request: principal amount, interest
rate, conversion discount, valuation cap, and maturity date. Ask:
"Are there any shareholders who are not on this cap table — including
anyone with a verbal agreement, a promised equity allocation, or a
right of first refusal?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a pre-Series A company has more than 15 shareholders or more than
two share classes, request the full cap table and all convertible
note terms before advancing — the cost to restructure a messy cap table
rises exponentially with each subsequent funding round and is cheapest
to fix before the current round closes.

FLAG G03: MISSING IP ASSIGNMENT AGREEMENTS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: CRITICAL

SIGNAL:
Any of the following are present or absent: (1) the company was founded
by a team where one or more founders previously worked at a large
technology company, a university, or a research institution — and the
deal materials do not mention IP assignment or Freedom to Operate
confirmation; (2) the deck or submission mentions that the technology
was developed while a founder was at a previous employer without
explicitly stating that IP rights have been legally confirmed as
belonging to the company; (3) the company relies on open-source
components but does not mention a licence audit or confirm that
all OSS components are permissively licensed (MIT, Apache 2.0)
rather than copyleft (GPL, LGPL, AGPL).

WHAT IT MEANS:
IP ownership is the foundational asset of a technology company.
If the IP does not unambiguously belong to the company — not to a
founder personally, not to a previous employer, not to a university
technology transfer office, and not encumbered by copyleft open-source
obligations — the company has no legally defensible moat and cannot
be sold. The three failure modes are distinct:
(1) Founder-developed IP: most employment contracts at technology
companies contain IP assignment clauses covering work done in the
employee's own time if it relates to the employer's business. A founder
who left Google and built a product adjacent to Google's business
may have inadvertently assigned their IP to Google. This is not a
theoretical risk — it has destroyed real companies (Mattel v. MGA,
Blizzard v. Bossland, multiple university spin-out disputes).
(2) University spin-outs: universities typically retain rights to
IP developed using university resources. The technology transfer
office must have formally assigned rights to the spin-out vehicle
and that assignment must be documented. TTOs have blocked exits
by asserting rights mid-process.
(3) Copyleft contamination: a single GPL-licensed component used
in a commercial product can — under strict reading of the GPL —
require the entire product to be open-sourced. This is a latent
liability that surfaces during acquirer due diligence.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The primary technology of the company was developed while a founder
was at a previous employer, no IP assignment opinion has been obtained,
and the previous employer is a technology company that operates in
the same or adjacent space — in this scenario the IP risk is not
theoretical and the deal cannot proceed without a clean IP opinion
from external counsel.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has already obtained a written IP opinion from external
counsel confirming that (i) all founder IP created pre-incorporation
has been assigned to the company, (ii) no previous employer has a
plausible claim, and (iii) all OSS components are permissively
licensed or properly ring-fenced.
(b) The technology was developed entirely post-incorporation by
employees under standard employment contracts containing IP
assignment clauses — and all such contracts are in place.
(c) The company is a university spin-out with a formal licence
agreement or IP assignment from the TTO already executed.

VERIFICATION:
Request: (1) IP assignment agreements for all founders — confirm
they cover work done prior to incorporation; (2) employment contracts
for all technical staff — confirm IP assignment clause is present;
(3) open-source audit or software bill of materials confirming
no copyleft-licensed components in the commercial product stack;
(4) any correspondence with previous employers or university TTOs
regarding IP claims. Ask: "Has any previous employer or university
made any claim, inquiry, or communication regarding ownership of
any technology used in the product?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder team includes anyone from a major technology company,
university, or research institution and the submission does not
explicitly confirm IP assignment, flag this as CRITICAL and request
the IP opinion before advancing — IP ownership disputes are the
most common cause of acquirer withdrawal at the exclusivity stage.

FLAG G04: BOARD WITHOUT INDEPENDENT DIRECTOR
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
A Series A or later-stage company whose board composition consists
entirely of founders and investor representatives, with no independent
director — or a company that has completed more than £5M in cumulative
funding with no independent director on the board. Observable from
any board composition disclosure in the deck, shareholder agreement
summary, or Companies House filing showing current directors.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A board with only founders and investor representatives has a structural
conflict of interest baked into every major decision: founders want
operational autonomy and are reluctant to acknowledge when the business
model is not working; investors want returns and may push for decisions
(secondary sales, M&A, down-round recapitalisations) that benefit
their fund economics over the company's long-term health. An
independent director — a non-executive with no equity stake in the
fund and no employment relationship with the company — provides the
neutral arbiter function that prevents governance paralysis in founder-
investor disputes, provides a credible sounding board for the CEO
without the conflict that investor board members carry, and signals
to the next round of investors that the company is professionally
governed. The absence of an independent director at Series A and
beyond is a signal that the founders are resisting accountability
structures — which is a leading indicator of governance breakdown
when the business hits its first serious operating challenge.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The board has previously voted to remove a CEO or independent director
without cause or due process and the deal materials do not explain
the circumstances — this is not a missing-director flag but a
governance conduct flag that is disqualifying on its own.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is pre-Series A and the board is still in the
informal advisory phase — independent directors are standard at
Series A, optional pre-Series A.
(b) The company has identified a specific independent director
candidate and the appointment is a condition of the current round
closing — the gap exists because the round has not yet closed,
not because management is resisting governance.
(c) The company has an active advisory board that includes one or
more senior independent operators with relevant domain experience,
and a formal independent director appointment is part of the
Series A governance terms being negotiated.

VERIFICATION:
Request: current board composition showing name, role (founder /
investor representative / independent), and date of appointment.
For any independent directors, confirm they hold no equity in the
investing fund and are not currently employed by the company.
Ask: "What is your current board composition and do you have
an independent director? If not, is this something you are
planning to address in this round?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a post-Series A company has no independent director and no
documented plan to appoint one, raise this in the Risk Assessment
and include it as a condition of investment — governance structures
installed at Series A cost almost nothing; the same structures
installed during a crisis cost relationships, legal fees, and time.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G05: OPTION POOL INSUFFICIENT FOR HIRING PLAN
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The company's hiring plan includes 3 or more senior roles (VP level
or above) or a CTO / CPO / CFO hire, but the remaining unallocated
option pool represents less than 10% of the fully diluted cap table.
Observable when a deck includes a hiring plan alongside a cap table —
the mismatch between the two is the signal. Also observable when the
deck describes needing to hire a leadership team but the cap table
shows the option pool is already 80% or more allocated.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Option pool insufficiency forces the company into one of three
sub-optimal outcomes, all of which are avoidable: (1) the company
offers insufficient equity to attract the calibre of hire the plan
requires, settles for a weaker candidate, and the weaker hire becomes
the reason the business does not scale — the classic scaling failure
where the seed-stage team cannot operate at Series B speed; (2) the
company goes back to existing shareholders to expand the option pool
before the hire — which is dilutive to everyone on a timeline that
is rarely controlled; (3) the company uses cash compensation to
substitute for equity, which increases burn at precisely the stage
where burn discipline matters most. The second-order problem is that
option pool top-ups at later stages are typically done pre-money,
meaning the dilution falls on existing shareholders in a way that
is often not properly modelled in the financial plan. A company
that claims it needs £5M to hit its next milestone but has not
modelled the dilutive cost of expanding the option pool to hire the
team the plan requires is presenting an incomplete financial picture.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The option pool is less than 5% unallocated and the company's hiring
plan requires 5 or more senior hires — at this level the option pool
expansion required before hires can be made will cause meaningful
dilution to current shareholders and the financial model does not
reflect this cost. This is not automatically a pass but it is a
material financial modelling error that must be corrected before
a term sheet is issued.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The current round includes an explicit option pool top-up as
part of the round structure and the post-money cap table reflects
the expanded pool — meaning the dilution has been modelled and
disclosed.
(b) The company is hiring below VP level for the next 18 months
and the existing pool is sufficient for those grants at standard
percentages (0.1–0.5% for senior individual contributors).
(c) The company has already made the senior hires it needs and
the plan is for individual contributor growth — the option pool
is tight but the critical positions are already filled.

VERIFICATION:
Request: (1) the full option pool on a fully diluted basis showing
allocated, unallocated, and vested/unvested breakdown; (2) the
hiring plan with titles, expected start dates, and expected equity
grant size for each planned hire; (3) the financial model to confirm
whether option pool expansion cost has been modelled into the dilution
waterfall. Ask: "Is the proposed option pool in this round large
enough to cover your full hiring plan through the next funding round?
If not, how are you planning to address the gap?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a company presents a senior hiring plan alongside a cap table
with less than 10% unallocated option pool, model the dilution from
the required pool expansion before issuing a term sheet — option pool
shortfalls discovered during closing add cost and delay that could
have been addressed in term sheet negotiation.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G06: RELATED PARTY LOANS TO FOUNDERS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The company's balance sheet, financial statements, or notes to accounts
show loans from the company to one or more founders, directors, or
their connected parties — or the cap table or shareholder agreement
reveals a shareholder loan structure where founders have extracted
capital from the company as loans rather than salary or dividends.
Observable in any financial statement showing a debtor balance owed
by a connected party, or in any note describing a "director's loan"
or "shareholder loan" on terms that differ from arm's-length.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Related party loans to founders are one of the clearest signals of
poor financial governance and are illegal in certain forms under the
Companies Act 2006 (for loans above £10,000 to directors of public
companies without shareholder approval — and the equivalent provisions
for private companies require disclosure). The business failure modes
are distinct: (1) the loan is in substance a disguised dividend —
the founder has extracted company capital in a form that avoids tax,
reduces the assets available to investors, and signals that the
founder treats the company's cash as personally accessible — a
fundamental governance breach; (2) the loan is a working capital
mechanism — the founder lent money to the company, the company is
obliged to repay it, and that repayment obligation represents a
debt senior to equity that the investor's money may effectively
service; (3) the loan exists on paper only and represents a prior
undisclosed financial arrangement with a connected party. None of
these three scenarios is acceptable without full disclosure and
investor-approved terms. The reputational implication is also
significant: a founder who has used the company as a personal
lending vehicle before raising institutional capital is demonstrating
the governance instincts that cause problems at scale.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A loan from the company to a founder is outstanding at the time of
investment, has no documented repayment terms, is at below-market
interest rates, and exceeds £25,000 — this is a Companies Act
breach risk, a governance red flag, and a signal of financial
conduct that disqualifies the deal until the loan is fully repaid
and the accounts are restated.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) A founder previously lent money to the company to cover operating
expenses before external funding was available — a founder loan to
the company (not from the company to the founder) is common and
acceptable if on documented arm's-length terms and the repayment
plan does not create a cash flow drain during the investment period.
(b) The related party loan has been fully disclosed in the company's
accounts, was approved by independent directors or shareholders,
is at market interest rates, and has a defined repayment schedule
that does not materially impact the company's working capital.
(c) The loan is a historical artefact of an earlier period that
has already been repaid and restated in the current accounts.

VERIFICATION:
Request: (1) the most recent annual accounts and management accounts
showing any debtor or creditor balances with connected parties;
(2) any shareholder loan agreements currently outstanding;
(3) the Companies House filing to cross-reference director loans
disclosed in the statutory accounts. Ask: "Are there any outstanding
loans between the company and any founder, director, or connected
party? If yes, what are the terms and when will they be repaid?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When the balance sheet shows a debtor balance associated with a
director or connected party, request the loan documentation and
Companies House statutory accounts before advancing — founder loans
from company funds discovered post-term-sheet create a retrading
risk and are the most common governance issue requiring legal
remediation before close.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G07: MULTIPLE SHARE CLASSES WITH EXTREME PREFERENCE STACKS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The cap table shows three or more distinct series of preferred shares
(Series A, Series B, Series C or equivalent), with participating
preferred provisions in two or more series, or a total liquidation
preference stack that exceeds 1.5× the last round valuation — meaning
investors would need to realise more than 1.5× the current round
price before common shareholders (founders and employees) receive
any proceeds. Observable from any cap table disclosure, term sheet
summary, or investor rights agreement excerpt that shows liquidation
preference multiples by series.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Extreme liquidation preference stacks are the most reliable predictor
of a waterfall problem at exit — and the waterfall problem is what
turns a moderate exit into a zero for everyone other than the most
senior preferred holders. The mechanics: if a company has raised
£20M across three rounds with participating preferred at 1× liquidation
preference in each series, and the company sells for £30M, the first
£20M goes to preferred shareholders in order of seniority, leaving
£10M split between common holders — but because preferred also
participates in the common pool (participating preferred), preferred
holders take their pro-rata share of the remaining £10M as well.
In this structure, founders and employees with common stock receive
approximately 20–30% of the headline number — and the option pool
holders (typically employees) receive a number that may not cover
their strike price. The practical consequence: employees understand
their options are worthless, the best ones leave, and the company
cannot hire replacements at a competitive cost of capital. The
acquirer recognises the misaligned incentive structure and adjusts
its offer. The preference stack is the primary reason that companies
with £30M exit headlines produce £2M founder proceeds.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The liquidation preference stack exceeds 2× the current round
valuation AND the participating preferred provisions mean that common
holders receive less than 10% of a plausible exit scenario AND the
company's most recent 409A or share valuation places the common
share value at less than 20% of the preferred price — indicating
that the market already prices the common as near-worthless. This
is not a governance flag — it is a structural indicator that the
company is an effective zombie from an equity incentive perspective
and the deal economics for any buyer will require a full
recapitalisation as a condition of acquisition.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Preferences are non-participating (investors receive either
their liquidation preference or their pro-rata common share,
whichever is higher, but not both) — non-participating preferred
is standard market practice and is not a flag.
(b) The preference stack is from a single funding round with a
1× non-participating preference — this is the cleanest structure
and creates no waterfall problem.
(c) The company is preparing a preference stack clean-up as part
of the current round, converting prior preferred to common or
to a simplified single-class preference structure, and the
current round investors are leading this recapitalisation.

VERIFICATION:
Request: the complete waterfall model showing proceeds to each
share class at three exit scenarios: 1× current round valuation,
2× current round valuation, and 5× current round valuation.
The waterfall model must show the exact break-even exit value
at which common shareholders begin to participate. Ask:
"Can you share the full liquidation preference stack and a
waterfall analysis? At what exit value do founders and
employees begin to receive proceeds?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a cap table shows three or more preferred series with
participating provisions, model the waterfall at three exit
scenarios before advancing — preference stacks that destroy
employee incentives are the primary reason why technically
strong companies fail to retain the team required to achieve
the exit value the model assumes.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G08: NO DATA ROOM OR INCOMPLETE DATA ROOM
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
A company raising a Series A or later-stage round (or any round above
£2M) has not provided — and does not appear to have prepared — a
formal data room containing: (1) audited or management accounts for
the last 2 years, (2) cap table on fully diluted basis, (3) all
shareholder and investor agreements, (4) key customer contracts,
(5) IP assignment and employment agreements, and (6) corporate
structure documents. Observable when the entire submission consists
of a pitch deck and verbal financial claims with no supporting
documentation, or when the company describes itself as "in process
of preparing" the data room after more than one investor meeting.

WHAT IT MEANS:
The absence of a data room at Series A is not a neutral administrative
fact — it is a signal about how the company is run. Professional
operators preparing for an institutional raise begin data room
assembly 60–90 days before they begin investor conversations.
A company that begins investor conversations without a data room
is either: (1) raising opportunistically before being ready —
common in founders who mistake deck quality for deal-readiness;
(2) hiding something — a gap in the documentation that would
be apparent in due diligence (an unsigned contract, an IP dispute,
an unresolved tax liability); or (3) operationally unprepared for
the governance requirements that come with institutional investment.
The second-order implication: an investor who completes due
diligence on a company with an incomplete data room will spend
more time, incur higher legal costs, and discover more issues
late in the process — each of which increases the probability
of a retrade or a failed close.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A company that has previously represented to investors that a data
room is "ready" or "available on request" and then provides a folder
of incomplete documents when due diligence begins — this is a
misrepresentation that changes the nature of the deal and requires
a direct conversation about what else may have been misrepresented.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is at pre-seed or seed stage raising less than
£1M — data rooms are not standard at this stage and the flag
does not apply below this threshold.
(b) The company is at a very early stage of the process (first
investor conversation) and has a documented timeline for data
room completion within 4 weeks.
(c) The data room exists but has not been shared pending NDA
execution — this is standard practice and is not a flag.

VERIFICATION:
Request: data room access or a data room index showing what is
available and what is still in preparation. The index itself
is diagnostic — a company that can produce a data room index
with clear completion status for each section is operationally
ready for due diligence. Ask: "Do you have a data room prepared?
Can you share the index so we can confirm we have everything
we need before proceeding to due diligence?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a Series A company has no data room and no clear timeline
to produce one, delay advancing to due diligence until the core
documents are available — due diligence commenced without a
data room routinely extends closing timelines by 4–8 weeks and
increases the probability of a retrade on financial terms.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G09: AUDITED ACCOUNTS NOT AVAILABLE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
A company raising Series A or later-stage funding (or any round
above £3M) that has been trading for more than 18 months cannot
produce audited accounts for its most recently completed financial
year — and provides only management accounts, which have not been
independently verified. Observable from deal materials that rely
exclusively on unaudited management accounts, or from a Companies
House filing showing the company has filed dormant accounts or
abbreviated accounts without an audit opinion despite trading activity.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Audited accounts are the most basic form of independent financial
verification available to an investor. A company with 18 or more
months of trading history that cannot produce audited accounts has
one of four explanations, none of which is neutral: (1) the company
has not engaged auditors because it believed it could raise on
management accounts alone — which signals inexperience with
institutional fundraising standards; (2) the audit is in process
but has not been completed — which raises the question of what
issues the auditors have raised that are delaying completion;
(3) the company failed to file accounts on time with Companies
House — which is a legal compliance failure that signals
administrative disorder and can result in directors' personal
liability; (4) the audit revealed issues (revenue recognition,
related party transactions, going concern qualifications) that
the founders have not disclosed to investors. The going concern
qualification scenario is the highest-risk: an auditor who has
qualified the accounts is expressing a professional opinion that
the company may not be able to continue as a going concern —
which, if undisclosed, constitutes a material misrepresentation
to investors.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has audited accounts from a prior period but has not
filed them with Companies House on the required timeline, and
the filing is more than 6 months overdue — this is a Companies
Act compliance failure that can result in automatic strike-off
of the company and personal liability for directors. A company
in this position has a legal problem, not an administrative one.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is less than 18 months old and the first financial
year has not yet been completed — the flag does not apply to
companies that have not yet reached their first audit deadline.
(b) The audit is in process with a named firm and an expected
completion date within 8 weeks — the gap is an administrative
timing issue, not a concealment issue.
(c) The company is below the Companies Act audit threshold
(turnover <£10.2M, balance sheet total <£5.1M, fewer than
50 employees) and has chosen to use the small company exemption,
but has provided a full set of independently reviewed management
accounts prepared by a qualified accountant.

VERIFICATION:
Request: (1) Companies House filing history showing all accounts
filed to date; (2) most recent audited accounts or, if not yet
audited, the management accounts and the name of the audit firm
engaged; (3) confirmation of whether the audit opinion is clean,
qualified, or contains a going concern note. Ask: "Have your
accounts been audited? If not, why not — and are there any
issues your auditors have raised that we should be aware of?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a Series A company has no audited accounts and has been
trading for more than 18 months, request a Companies House
filing check and the audit engagement letter before advancing —
accounts that are overdue or qualify going concern are the
most common financial disclosure issue discovered after term
sheets are issued and are the primary cause of retrades on
financial representations.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG G10: GDPR OR DATA COMPLIANCE NOT IMPLEMENTED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Governance
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
A company that processes personal data of EU or UK individuals —
which includes almost every B2C company, every B2B company with
employee data, and every company using any analytics platform —
cannot demonstrate: (1) a registered ICO data controller number
(UK); (2) a documented lawful basis for processing for each data
category; (3) a privacy notice on the product; (4) a data processing
agreement with each third-party processor (including analytics, cloud,
CRM providers); or (5) a documented process for handling data subject
access requests (DSARs). Observable from product screenshots with
no visible privacy notice, from the absence of ICO registration
in the data room, or from deal materials that do not mention data
compliance despite a clearly data-intensive product.

WHAT IT MEANS:
GDPR non-compliance is not a theoretical legal risk — it is a live
ICO enforcement risk with penalties of up to 4% of global annual
turnover or £17.5M (UK GDPR), whichever is higher. The practical
business failure modes are: (1) an ICO investigation or enforcement
action triggered by a data subject complaint or a breach — the
ICO has been actively enforcing since 2020 and enforcement against
SMEs has accelerated since 2023; (2) acquirer due diligence uncovers
GDPR non-compliance and either reduces the acquisition price to
account for the remediation cost and liability tail, or uses the
compliance gap as grounds for price chipping or deal withdrawal;
(3) enterprise customers discover non-compliance during their own
vendor due diligence — a significant and growing proportion of
enterprise procurement processes include a data compliance review,
and a failed vendor assessment from a key customer can trigger
contract termination under standard data processing terms.
The EU dimension adds DORA (Digital Operational Resilience Act,
January 2025) for financial services companies operating in the EU —
DORA requires ICT risk management frameworks, third-party vendor
risk management, and incident reporting that go beyond standard
GDPR compliance and are frequently overlooked by UK-incorporated
companies that have EU customers.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has already received an ICO enforcement notice or is
currently under active ICO investigation — this is a live regulatory
enforcement action that creates a material liability on the balance
sheet and must be fully disclosed. A company under active ICO
investigation cannot provide clean regulatory representations in
an investment agreement.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has a documented GDPR compliance programme in
progress — a named DPO or privacy counsel, a data mapping exercise
completed, and a remediation timeline of 60 days or fewer to close
the remaining gaps.
(b) The company processes only B2B data under contracts where
GDPR compliance is the primary responsibility of the data controller
(the customer) and the company's role is limited to data processor
with appropriate DPAs in place.
(c) The company is at an early pre-launch stage and has not yet
processed any personal data — the flag applies from the point
of first personal data processing, not from incorporation.

VERIFICATION:
Request: (1) ICO registration certificate (UK) or lead supervisory
authority registration (EU); (2) data processing register or ROPA
(Record of Processing Activities); (3) DPAs with all third-party
processors; (4) privacy notice from the live product; (5) for
financial services companies with EU customers, DORA compliance
assessment or confirmation that DORA has been assessed as
not applicable. Ask: "Are you registered with the ICO? Do you
have a data processing register and DPAs with all your third-party
processors? Has anyone in the team reviewed your GDPR obligations
formally?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a data-intensive company cannot demonstrate ICO registration
and a basic GDPR compliance framework, escalate to legal review
before advancing — GDPR non-compliance discovered post-investment
is remediated at the company's cost and represents an unpriced
liability that should have been flagged at screening.
