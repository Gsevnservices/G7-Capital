ALEX ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK — VERSION 4
FRAMEWORK 4: COMPETITIVE MOAT DURABILITY ASSESSMENT
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PURPOSE: Most analysts assess whether a moat exists today. The best
analysts assess how long the moat will hold and what will erode it.
A moat that lasts 2 years requires a different investment thesis than
a moat that lasts 10 years. Alex assesses both moat existence AND moat
durability — and states a specific durability timeline with evidence.

RULE ZERO: Identify the moat type before scoring durability. Do not
accept a founder's moat claim at face value. Decompose it into one of
the seven types below. If it does not fit a type, it is not a moat —
it is a competitive advantage, which is a different and weaker thing.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION A — THE 7 MOAT TYPES
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 1 — NETWORK EFFECTS

DEFINITION: The product becomes more valuable to each user as more
users join. Value is non-linear — it compounds with scale.

SUB-TYPES (Alex identifies which applies):
— Direct (same-side): each new user directly benefits all existing
  users. Every message sent benefits all recipients.
— Indirect (cross-side/marketplace): more supply attracts more
  demand; more demand attracts more supply. Each side benefits
  the other.
— Data network effect: more users generate more data which improves
  the product for all users. Value increase is mediated through
  the product layer, not direct user-to-user interaction.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Deck or submission states that engagement, retention, or utility
  metrics improve as user count grows — with data.
— Product is a marketplace, platform, or communication tool where
  both sides of the network are named.
— Founder cites data showing that cohort retention improves as
  the network grows — not just that retention is good.

INHERENT DURABILITY: Long (5–10 years) for direct and indirect
network effects when the network has reached critical mass.
Medium (2–5 years) for data network effects — data advantages
erode if the underlying model can be retrained by a competitor
with different data.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: Network fragmentation or multi-homing.
Users join multiple competing networks simultaneously, reducing
the exclusivity value of any single network. A new entrant that
successfully seeds a sub-population can fragment the network
faster than expected.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Continuously increase switching cost
alongside network growth. Standalone product value must be high
enough that users remain even during network fragmentation events.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH defensibility when network effects are
direct and the network has reached critical mass. MEDIUM when
data-mediated. LOW when the founder asserts network effects but
the product is pre-network.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 2 — SWITCHING COSTS

DEFINITION: Customers cannot leave without significant cost —
financial, operational, or time cost — that makes switching
economically irrational.

SUB-TYPES (Alex identifies which applies):
— Data lock-in: proprietary data format or accumulated historical
  data that cannot be exported cleanly or that loses context
  when moved.
— Workflow integration: product is embedded in daily operations
  across multiple teams — switching requires retraining, reprocess
  design, and a transition period with productivity loss.
— Financial lock-in: multi-year contracts, exit penalties, or
  sunk costs in implementation and configuration.
— Regulatory lock-in: product holds a compliance certification or
  audit trail that regulators have approved — switching resets
  the compliance clock.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Customer contracts exceed 12 months with stated exit penalties.
— Product has API integrations into multiple third-party systems
  the customer already uses.
— Implementation requires professional services, training, or
  a stated onboarding period exceeding 4 weeks.
— Product stores customer data in a format that is not
  standardised or easily portable.

INHERENT DURABILITY: Medium (2–5 years) for financial and data
lock-in. Long (5–10 years) for deep workflow integration embedded
across multiple teams and systems. Structural (10+) for regulatory
lock-in in highly regulated sectors where switching requires
re-approval by a regulator.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: A competitor subsidises the switching
cost — offers free migration, free implementation, or pays the
exit penalty. When switching cost can be purchased away, it is
not structural.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Deepen integration over time. Add new
integrations with each contract renewal. Make the product the
system of record for a process the customer cannot afford to
disrupt.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH for regulatory lock-in and deep
multi-system workflow integration. MEDIUM for data and financial
lock-in. LOW for switching costs that a well-funded competitor
could purchase away.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 3 — PROPRIETARY DATA

DEFINITION: The company possesses data that competitors cannot
acquire or replicate. The data improves the product in ways that
more capital alone cannot replicate.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Data was collected through a proprietary process (exclusive
  partnerships, first-mover accumulation, unique sensor or
  transaction access) rather than purchased or scraped.
— Data accumulation is ongoing and compounds — more data directly
  improves the product in a measurable way.
— The data has an exclusivity period or structural barrier: a
  legal agreement, a physical access advantage, or a regulatory
  approval required to collect it.

CRITICAL DISTINCTION: Data that is proprietary today may become
available tomorrow if a competitor signs the same partnerships
or if the regulatory access is opened. Alex must assess exclusivity
period and whether the advantage compounds or degrades over time.

INHERENT DURABILITY: Medium (2–5 years) when exclusivity depends
on partnerships that can be replicated. Long (5–10 years) when
data is accumulated through first-mover network effects and
the dataset's size itself is the barrier. Structural (10+) when
data collection requires physical infrastructure or regulatory
approval that competitors cannot obtain.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: Data commoditisation. A large
incumbent, a regulatory change mandating data sharing, or a
new data source that approximates the proprietary dataset
can eliminate the advantage faster than expected.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Continuously expand data collection.
Build the product so that the dataset's value is demonstrated
in outputs — customers and partners see the product improvement
from data accumulation and are motivated to contribute more data.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH when data is truly exclusive with
a structural barrier to replication. MEDIUM when exclusive
by first-mover position but replicable with capital and time.
LOW when data claimed as proprietary is available from
alternative sources.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 4 — REGULATORY / LICENSING

DEFINITION: A licence, certification, or regulatory approval is
required to operate in this market. The company holds it.
Competitors must spend significant time and capital to obtain
the same approval, and may be rejected.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— A named licence is cited (FCA authorisation, FDA clearance,
  banking licence, legal aid certification) that the company
  has obtained and that competitors are still pursuing.
— The approval process is stated to take 12+ months and is
  subject to rejection, not simply a filing process.
— The regulatory approval provides a demonstrable commercial
  advantage: the company can offer a product or service that
  unlicensed competitors cannot.

NOTE: Regulatory moats can disappear if regulation changes.
Alex must assess regulatory stability — is the regulatory
regime being tightened (strengthening the moat) or liberalised
(weakening it)?

INHERENT DURABILITY: Long (5–10 years) in stable regulatory
environments where new licences are rarely granted. Structural
(10+) when the licence category is closed to new entrants by
statute. Short (1–2 years) when the regulatory regime is under
active review or when the approval process is being streamlined.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: Regulatory change that opens the
category to new entrants, reduces licensing requirements, or
eliminates the need for the licence entirely.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Monitor regulatory developments actively.
Use the licenced period to build switching costs and brand moats
so that the regulatory moat is not the sole defence.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH in stable or tightening regulatory
environments. MEDIUM when the regulatory regime is uncertain.
LOW when the licence is likely to be granted to new entrants
within 24 months.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 5 — BRAND / TRUST

DEFINITION: Customers choose this product because they trust the
brand, not because of a rational product comparison. Trust is
particularly durable in sectors where the cost of a wrong choice
is high: healthcare, financial services, legal, security, and
critical infrastructure.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— NPS scores materially above category average with customers
  citing trust and reliability rather than features.
— Customer testimonials reference the brand explicitly, not the
  product functionality.
— The company operates in a sector where reputational failure
  has measurable consequences for the buyer (regulatory sanction,
  patient harm, financial loss).
— Churn rate is below category average and customer tenure
  is extended — consistent with trust-based retention.

INHERENT DURABILITY: Long (5–10 years) when trust is established
in a high-stakes sector. Medium (2–5 years) in lower-stakes
categories where brand switching is lower cost.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: A single publicised failure event —
a data breach, a regulatory sanction, a product recall, or a
public customer dispute — can destroy years of brand equity
in weeks. Brand moats are asymmetric: slow to build, fast to destroy.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Invest continuously in reliability and
customer success. Have a crisis response protocol — a brand
moat without crisis management is fragile.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH in sectors with high cost of wrong
choice and demonstrated multi-year customer relationships.
MEDIUM in sectors with moderate switching cost. LOW when brand
is early-stage and not yet proven under stress.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 6 — SCALE ECONOMICS

DEFINITION: The company's cost structure improves as it scales
in a way that smaller competitors cannot replicate. The larger
they get, the cheaper it is to serve each additional customer.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Gross margin increases demonstrably with revenue scale — not
  simply because fixed costs are spread across more customers,
  but because variable costs per unit fall with volume.
— The company can name the specific cost component that falls
  with scale: supplier volume discounts, infrastructure amortisation,
  data centre efficiency, or proprietary manufacturing process.
— Unit economics at current scale are materially better than
  unit economics 12 months ago on a like-for-like basis.

CRITICAL DISTINCTION: A company that is simply larger than
competitors is not a scale economics moat. The moat exists only
when scale DIRECTLY reduces unit costs in a structural way —
not when it merely spreads fixed costs.

INHERENT DURABILITY: Medium (2–5 years) when scale advantages
derive from purchasing power or operational efficiency that
competitors can replicate by raising capital. Long (5–10 years)
when scale advantages derive from proprietary infrastructure,
data accumulation at scale, or supplier relationships that
require years of volume to unlock.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: A well-capitalised competitor or
an incumbent with an existing cost base subsidises entry at
a loss — buying scale faster than the moat can be extended.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Continuously reinvest scale savings into
product and price rather than margin — maintain the cost advantage
as an offensive tool, not just a profitability metric.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH when scale advantage is structural
and margin improvement is demonstrated in historical financials.
MEDIUM when scale advantage is logical but not yet demonstrated.
LOW when the company cites scale economics without evidence of
margin improvement.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MOAT TYPE 7 — UNIQUE RESOURCE / IP

DEFINITION: The company owns a patent, proprietary technology,
exclusive relationship, or physical resource that competitors
cannot access or replicate regardless of capital.

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Named patents with filing dates and jurisdictions, covering
  the core product functionality.
— An exclusive contract or partnership that provides a resource
  competitors cannot independently obtain.
— A physical or geographic resource (a specific dataset origin,
  a manufacturing facility with unique capability, an exclusive
  distribution right) that is not replicable.

CRITICAL DISTINCTION: IP moats require active defence. A patent
is only a moat if the company can enforce it against infringers —
which requires capital, legal resources, and a willingness to
litigate. Alex must assess enforceability alongside ownership.

INHERENT DURABILITY: Medium (2–5 years) for patents in fast-moving
technology categories where design-arounds are achievable. Long
(5–10 years) for patents covering fundamental processes in
slower-moving sectors. Structural (10+) for exclusive physical
resources or relationships that are legally protected and
cannot be circumvented.

PRIMARY EROSION MECHANISM: Patent expiry, successful design-around
by a competitor, or failure to enforce — which sets a legal
precedent that weakens the IP position.

DEFENCE REQUIREMENTS: Maintain an active IP portfolio — file
continuation patents as the product evolves. Monitor competitors
for infringement. Budget for enforcement.

ALEX'S MOAT SCORE: HIGH when patents are broad, recently granted,
in a jurisdiction where enforcement is practical, and the company
has demonstrated willingness to defend them. MEDIUM when patents
are narrow or design-arounds are feasible. LOW when IP is cited
but no filing details are provided.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION B — MOAT DURABILITY SCORING
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

After identifying the moat type(s), score durability 0–10.

9–10: STRUCTURAL MOAT.
Durable 10+ years without active defence. Requires: network effects
AND switching costs combined, OR regulatory licensing in a stable
environment, OR truly proprietary data that compounds structurally.
Output: "Structural moat identified: [type(s)]. Durability: 10+ years.
This position will be very difficult for a competitor to replicate
or erode without a fundamental change in the market or regulatory
environment."

7–8: STRONG MOAT.
Durable 5–10 years with active defence. Single strong moat type,
well-executed with clear evidence.
Output: "Strong moat identified: [type]. Durability: 5–10 years.
Active defence required — specifically [defence requirement].
Without it, erosion risk increases materially after [period]."

5–6: MODERATE MOAT.
Durable 2–5 years. Requires consistent active defence. A well-funded
competitor could erode with sufficient capital and time.
Output: "Moderate moat identified: [type]. Durability: 2–5 years.
[Primary erosion mechanism] is the primary threat. Recommend
evaluating [specific defence requirement] as a priority."

3–4: WEAK MOAT.
Durable 1–2 years. Can be replicated by a well-funded competitor
within 18 months.
Output: "Weak moat identified: [type]. Durability: 12–24 months.
[Erosion mechanism] presents near-term risk. This investment thesis
depends on the company building additional moat layers before
the current advantage erodes."

1–2: MOAT CLAIMED, NOT DEMONSTRATED.
Founder claims a moat but evidence does not support it. Any
competitor with equivalent capital can replicate the position.
Output: "Moat claimed ([founder's claim]) but not demonstrated.
Evidence provided does not establish structural defensibility.
Treating competitive position as undefended for scoring purposes."

0: NO MOAT IDENTIFIED.
Output: "No structural competitive moat identified. The business
has competitive advantages [list if any] but these are not
structural moats — they can be replicated with capital. This is
not automatically disqualifying but is a significant risk factor
that must be addressed in the investment thesis."

SECTION C — THE AI MOAT DECOMPOSITION RULE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

"AI" is never accepted as a moat description. When a company claims
AI as its moat, Alex decomposes it into the underlying moat type —
or classifies it as no moat. Apply these rules to every AI moat claim:

CLAIM: "Proprietary AI model."
DECOMPOSITION: Ask — what data was this model trained on? Is that
  data exclusive? If the training data is public, purchasable, or
  available to any competitor with capital, the model is not
  proprietary in any durable sense. The moat, if any, is
  PROPRIETARY DATA (Type 3). Score the data exclusivity, not the
  model's current performance.
OUTPUT: "AI model claim decomposed: the underlying moat is the
  training data, not the model. Scoring as Proprietary Data (Type 3)."

CLAIM: "AI that gets smarter with use."
DECOMPOSITION: This is a data network effect claim. Test it:
  does the product demonstrably improve as more data is collected,
  with evidence that improvement is compound not linear? If yes:
  classify as DATA NETWORK EFFECT (a combination of Type 1 and
  Type 3) and score accordingly. If no — if the improvement is
  asserted but not evidenced — this is a marketing claim, not a moat.
OUTPUT: "AI improvement claim tested: [evidence present / not present].
  [Classifying as Data Network Effect and scoring as Type 1+3 /
  Classifying as undemonstrated claim — no moat awarded]."

CLAIM: "First mover in AI for [sector]."
DECOMPOSITION: First mover is not a moat. Speed is not a moat.
  Ask — what does first-mover advantage translate to structurally?
  If it translates to proprietary training data accumulated during
  the first-mover period that competitors cannot now replicate:
  PROPRIETARY DATA moat (Type 3) — assess exclusivity period.
  If it translates to brand trust from early enterprise customers:
  BRAND moat (Type 5) — assess fragility. If it translates to
  nothing structural: first-mover advantage only — time-limited,
  not a moat.
OUTPUT: "First-mover claim decomposed: structural translation is
  [data / brand / none]. Scoring accordingly as [Type 3 / Type 5 /
  no moat]."

CLAIM: "Proprietary algorithm."
DECOMPOSITION: Algorithms can be replicated or improved. Ask —
  is this algorithm patented with a named filing? Is it protected
  by trade secret with appropriate legal structures in place?
  If patented: score as UNIQUE IP (Type 7) — assess enforceability.
  If trade secret with legal protection: score as WEAK moat —
  trade secrets erode when employees depart. If neither: this is
  a technical lead that will erode. Score as 1–2 (Claimed, not
  demonstrated) at best.
OUTPUT: "Algorithm claim decomposed: [patented — scoring as Type 7 /
  trade secret — scoring as weak moat / unprotected — scoring as
  claimed not demonstrated]."

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION D — MOAT COMBINATION RULES
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Companies with two or more independent moat types are significantly
more defensible. Apply these rules when multiple moats are identified.

SINGLE MOAT: Score as per Section B. State: "Single moat identified:
[type]. Durability: [period]."

TWO INDEPENDENT MOATS: Add 1.5 points to the base durability score
from Section B (maximum 10). State: "Two independent moats identified:
[type 1] and [type 2]. Combined durability increases to [period].
Each moat reinforces the other — erosion of one does not automatically
erode both."

THREE OR MORE INDEPENDENT MOATS: Add 3 points to the base durability
score. Classify as STRUCTURAL MOAT if total reaches 9+, regardless of
individual moat scores. State: "Multiple moat stack: [list all types].
This is a structurally defensible position. A competitor must
simultaneously overcome [list each moat] to displace this company —
which requires capital, time, and market access that few competitors
can assemble concurrently."

MOAT DEPENDENCY RULE: When two apparent moats are the same underlying
moat described differently, count as one. Common dependency pairs:
— "Proprietary data" and "AI gets smarter with use" → same moat:
  Proprietary Data (Type 3).
— "Network effects" and "data gets better with scale" → same moat:
  Data Network Effect (Type 1).
— "Scale economics" and "purchasing power advantage" → same moat:
  Scale Economics (Type 6).
State: "[Moat claim A] and [moat claim B] are the same underlying
moat — [type]. Scoring as a single moat."

SECTION E — MOAT EROSION TRIGGERS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 1 — NETWORK EFFECTS:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: A competing network successfully seeds a
  sub-population of the existing network — colleagues, a geography,
  or a professional segment — and offers a feature that the incumbent
  does not, causing localised defection that fragments the whole.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: Multi-homing becomes costless. Users join the
  competing network without leaving the original, diluting the
  exclusivity value until neither network has a meaningful advantage.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Engagement metrics declining in a specific
  user cohort or geography; a competing product launching with
  a subsidised free tier targeting the same user population.

TYPE 2 — SWITCHING COSTS:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: A competitor builds and funds a migration team
  that absorbs the switching cost on behalf of defecting customers —
  offering free implementation, free data migration, and a paid
  transition period.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: A new infrastructure standard emerges that makes
  the proprietary data format or integration obsolete — portability
  increases and lock-in dissolves.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: A funded competitor advertising a migration
  guarantee or "we pay your exit fee" offer; a new API standard or
  open-source alternative gaining adoption in the customer base.

TYPE 3 — PROPRIETARY DATA:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: A regulatory change mandates data sharing or
  portability, opening the dataset to all market participants and
  eliminating exclusivity.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: A large incumbent or new entrant acquires a
  different but functionally equivalent dataset through an exclusive
  partnership, reducing the proprietary advantage to a head start
  rather than a structural barrier.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Regulatory consultations on data sharing in
  the sector; news of a large competitor signing exclusive data
  partnerships with key data sources.

TYPE 4 — REGULATORY / LICENSING:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: The regulatory body opens a new licensing round,
  or simplifies the application process, enabling a cohort of
  competitors to obtain equivalent licences within 12–18 months.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: A legislative or policy change eliminates the
  requirement for the licence in the company's primary market —
  the moat disappears without warning.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Government consultations or white papers
  proposing regulatory reform in the licensed category; a competitor
  publicly announcing a licence application or approval.

TYPE 5 — BRAND / TRUST:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: A publicised failure event — data breach,
  regulatory sanction, product recall, high-profile customer dispute —
  triggers trust collapse faster than the brand was built. Brand
  moats are asymmetric in erosion speed.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: A new entrant in the category obtains a third-party
  endorsement (regulatory approval, institutional partnership, or
  major media coverage) that confers trust credibility without the
  investment of time, neutralising the brand advantage.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Any increase in customer complaint volume or
  regulatory enquiry; a competitor announcing a major institutional
  partnership or certification that confers equivalent trust signals.

TYPE 6 — SCALE ECONOMICS:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: A well-capitalised incumbent or new entrant
  enters the market and subsidises pricing at a loss, buying scale
  quickly enough to reach cost parity before the company can extend
  its advantage.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: A technology shift changes the cost curve
  fundamentally — a new infrastructure or delivery method makes
  the existing scale advantage irrelevant because the new technology
  has a different and more favourable cost structure from day one.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: A funded competitor announcing aggressive
  pricing; a new technology entering the category with materially
  different infrastructure costs.

TYPE 7 — UNIQUE RESOURCE / IP:
EROSION TRIGGER 1: A competitor successfully designs around the
  patent — achieves the same functional outcome through a different
  technical method — or challenges the patent's validity and wins.
EROSION TRIGGER 2: The exclusive relationship or partnership expires,
  is not renewed, or the partner is acquired by a competitor who
  terminates the exclusivity.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Competitor patent filings in adjacent
  technical space; news of a key partner entering discussions
  with a competitor or being acquired.

SECTION F — BUSINESS MODEL SCORE IMPACT
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Business Model dimension is scored out of 10. Apply the moat
durability score as the primary determinant of the business model
score range. Other business model factors (unit economics, margin
structure, revenue predictability) determine where within the range
the score lands.

MOAT DURABILITY 9–10 (STRUCTURAL):
Business Model score range: 8–10/10.
Add note: "Structural competitive moat identified — [type(s)].
Business model is highly defensible. Score in this range reflects
both moat durability and unit economics quality."

MOAT DURABILITY 7–8 (STRONG):
Business Model score range: 7–8/10.
Add note: "Strong moat — [type] — with [period] durability.
Active defence required: [specific defence requirement].
Business model score reflects current strength with a
medium-term durability horizon."

MOAT DURABILITY 5–6 (MODERATE):
Business Model score range: 5–6/10.
Add note: "Moderate moat. 2–5 year window before erosion risk
increases materially. Business model score reflects the need
to build additional defensibility within this window."

MOAT DURABILITY 3–4 (WEAK):
Business Model score range: 3–5/10.
Add note: "Weak moat. Current competitive advantage is replicable
within 18 months by a well-funded competitor. Recommend assessing
the company's specific plan to build structural defensibility
before the current lead erodes."

MOAT DURABILITY 1–2 (CLAIMED, NOT DEMONSTRATED):
Business Model score range: 2–4/10.
Add note: "Moat claimed but not evidenced. Business model is
treated as currently undefended for scoring purposes. The investment
thesis requires a specific moat-building pathway to be credible."

MOAT DURABILITY 0 (NONE IDENTIFIED):
Business Model score range: 1–3/10.
Add note: "No moat identified. Business model is currently undefended.
This is a significant risk factor. Note: absence of a moat today
is not an automatic PASS — some businesses build moats through
scale. State this explicitly: 'No moat today. Investment thesis
depends on [specific moat type] developing as the company scales
— specifically [named mechanism].'"
