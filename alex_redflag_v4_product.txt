ALEX — RED FLAG LIBRARY
CATEGORY 4: PRODUCT RED FLAGS
Version 1.0. Built: April 2026.
10 flags. Apply mechanically to every deal submission.
These flags are observable from a submission without a meeting.
Two analysts applying these rules reach the same conclusion.
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION RULE:
For every deal submission, Alex checks all 10 flags before completing
the screening note. Flags are not discretionary — if the signal is
present, the flag is raised. Flags are then weighed in aggregate.
One CRITICAL flag = automatic deep investigation required.
Two or more HIGH flags = note this in the Risk Assessment section.
Product flags interact with financial flags — a product with HIGH
implementation risk combined with a burn multiple above 3x is a
compounding risk, not two independent concerns.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P01: NO WORKING PRODUCT DEMO AT SERIES A OR BEYOND
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
A company at Series A or beyond — defined as £1M+ ARR or a disclosed Series A
funding round — is unable to provide a live product demo on request, offering instead
screenshots, mockups, pre-recorded video, or a staged environment that does not
reflect the current production product. Detectable from: submission describes
features that are not yet built; demo request is met with scheduling delays or
a staged walkthrough; the product screenshots in the submission do not correspond
to a live URL; or the company describes its product in future tense for core features
that should be live at this stage.

WHAT IT MEANS:
At Series A, the product exists. Customers are using it. The inability to demo
a live product to a potential investor is explicable in a small number of legitimate
scenarios (security-sensitive enterprise product, regulated environment with strict
data handling requirements) — but in most cases it signals one of the following:
(1) Core features described in the pitch are not yet built — the product is
presented as more complete than it is. The submission is describing the roadmap,
not the current state.
(2) The live product is unstable, slow, or contains significant UX problems that
the founder does not want an investor to see — the demo environment is curated.
(3) The company does not have a demo environment set up and has never been asked
for a live demo — which raises questions about the maturity of the sales process.
A product that cannot be demoed live at Series A has not been used extensively
enough by enough customers to have driven the ARR claimed.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has stated ARR above £500K AND cannot provide any form of live product
access or demo within 5 business days of request AND the submission contains
feature descriptions that appear to be future-state rather than current-state —
the ARR claim is not consistent with a product that cannot be demonstrated.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The product operates in a regulated environment where data security requirements
prevent a generic demo environment — the product exists and is used, but live demos
require signed NDAs and specific data environments. The company can provide a
reference customer who will confirm usage in a call.
(b) The product is a hardware-software combination where a live software demo is
possible but the hardware component cannot be demoed remotely — the software can
be demonstrated and the hardware component can be described with customer testimonials.
(c) The product is genuinely in closed beta with a small number of design partners
and is not yet at Series A stage — the flag applies to the stage claim, not the demo.

VERIFICATION:
Request a live product demo or sandbox access as a standard part of screening.
Ask: "Can you give us a live walkthrough of the product as it works today for
a current customer?" If this request generates delay, caveats, or an offer of
a pre-recorded video instead, flag the inconsistency with the claimed ARR and
request a direct customer reference call to confirm active usage.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a company at Series A cannot provide a live product demo within a standard
timeframe, verify that the ARR claim is consistent with the product's actual
state before proceeding — the inability to demo a live product at this stage
is a product completeness signal, not a scheduling issue.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P02: CORE FEATURE BUILT ON A SINGLE THIRD-PARTY API
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The primary value-creating feature of the product — the capability that customers
pay for — is entirely dependent on a single third-party API whose pricing, availability,
or terms of service could change unilaterally. Detectable from: the submission
describes the product's AI capability as powered exclusively by one foundation model
provider; the core data feed comes from a single licensed source; the primary workflow
integration is with one platform (Salesforce, Stripe, Plaid) where the vendor's
API terms explicitly restrict competitive use cases.

COMMON INSTANCES IN 2025–2026:
— AI-native SaaS product where all inference is via OpenAI API with no multi-model
  architecture or proprietary model component
— Fintech product where all bank data is accessed via a single open banking
  aggregator (TrueLayer, Plaid) with no alternative data source
— Productivity tool built as a single plugin to one platform (Salesforce, HubSpot,
  Notion) where the platform's developer terms restrict the product's monetisation
— Healthcare data product where all NHS data access is through a single integration
  partner whose contract is up for renewal

WHAT IT MEANS:
Third-party API dependency creates four specific risk vectors:
(1) Price risk: the API provider raises prices. The vendor's gross margin compresses
immediately and proportionally to the API's share of COGS. In AI inference, this
has materialised in both directions — OpenAI reduced prices 94% between 2022 and 2025,
but has also changed model capabilities, pricing tiers, and rate limits multiple times.
(2) Terms risk: the API provider changes its terms of service to restrict use cases,
require revenue sharing, or prohibit competitive products. The vendor's product may
become non-compliant with terms overnight.
(3) Availability risk: the API provider experiences downtime, exits the market, or
is acquired. In each case, the vendor's product fails alongside the provider.
(4) Platform displacement risk: the API provider launches a competing product that
uses the same underlying API — the vendor is building distribution for a future competitor.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
More than 70% of the core product's primary value-creating feature is dependent on
a single third-party API AND that provider has launched or announced a competing
product in the last 24 months AND the vendor has no documented roadmap for building
a proprietary alternative or adding a second provider — the platform displacement
risk is no longer theoretical.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has a documented multi-provider architecture — it uses multiple
foundation models, data sources, or integration points and can switch between them.
Even if one provider is currently primary, the ability to switch within 30 days
materially reduces the risk.
(b) The dependency is on a commodity infrastructure provider (AWS, GCP, Azure)
where pricing is stable, alternatives are equally capable, and the switching cost
is high but achievable — this is infrastructure dependency, not core feature dependency.
(c) The company has a signed API agreement with pricing and availability guarantees
that cover the investment horizon — the contractual protection addresses the price
and availability risk even if the platform displacement risk remains.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "If [primary API provider] doubled their prices or changed their terms tomorrow,
what would happen to your gross margin and your product? And how long would it take
you to migrate to an alternative?" The answer reveals both the financial exposure
and the engineering optionality. Also ask: "Has [primary API provider] launched or
announced any product that competes with yours?" If yes and the vendor is unaware,
the competitive intelligence gap is as concerning as the dependency itself.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the core product value is delivered through a single third-party API, model
the gross margin impact of a 50% price increase from that provider and ask whether
the provider has launched a competing product — the two questions together determine
the severity of the dependency.

FLAG P03: TECHNICAL DEBT — FULL REBUILD ACKNOWLEDGED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The submission, CTO note, or technical architecture description explicitly
acknowledges that the current product requires a complete rewrite or rebuild
before it can scale to enterprise use or handle the next order of magnitude
of usage — or the description of the technical architecture uses language that
implies a rebuild requirement ("we're currently on a monolith that won't scale,"
"we're in the process of moving to a microservices architecture," "v2 of the
product is being built from scratch").

WHAT IT MEANS:
A technical rebuild is one of the most capital-intensive and timeline-risky events
in a startup's life. The specific failure modes:
(1) The rebuild takes 2–3x longer than planned. During the rebuild, new feature
development for existing customers slows or stops. Customers request features that
cannot be delivered on the old architecture. Churn increases while the team is
focused inward on the rebuild rather than outward on customers.
(2) The rebuild consumes a disproportionate share of engineering capacity — typically
50–70% of the engineering team for 12–18 months. This is capital that was raised
for product development and market expansion but is consumed by technical remediation.
(3) The rebuild creates a period of instability where the old architecture is no
longer invested in and the new architecture is not yet production-ready — a trough
of unreliability that damages customer confidence at exactly the wrong time.
(4) The rebuild itself may encounter new architectural problems — the team that
built the first version is often not the team with the skills to architect the second.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A complete rebuild is acknowledged as necessary AND the rebuild timeline is estimated
at more than 12 months AND the company's current burn will be exhausted before the
rebuild is complete AND the company is using this fundraise to fund the rebuild rather
than to accelerate commercial growth — the capital deployment rationale is remediation,
not expansion.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The rebuild is a partial technical modernisation — specific components being
refactored while the core product remains operational — not a full rebuild that
leaves customers on an unsupported legacy system.
(b) The company has sufficient runway (18+ months) to complete the rebuild without
racing against capital depletion — the engineering risk is real but the timeline
pressure does not compound it.
(c) The rebuild is driven by a strategic expansion requirement (adding enterprise-grade
SSO, RBAC, audit logs to a previously SMB product) rather than by instability —
this is a planned investment in enterprise readiness, not emergency remediation.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "How much of the current product will be reused in the v2 architecture? What
is the estimated timeline for the rebuild? And how will you maintain feature delivery
for existing customers while the rebuild is underway?" A team that has planned the
rebuild rigorously will have specific answers. A team that has not planned it will
give optimistic estimates that do not account for the overhead of running parallel
systems.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a technical rebuild is acknowledged, estimate the engineering capacity it will
consume and the timeline before the new architecture is production-ready — if the
rebuild will consume more than 50% of engineering capacity for more than 9 months,
model it as a growth pause, not a growth investment.

FLAG P04: NO PROPRIETARY DATA ACCUMULATION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The product does not accumulate proprietary data through customer usage — every
new customer starts from zero and gains no benefit from the prior usage of other
customers or from their own historical data within the platform. The product is
functionally equivalent on day 1 and day 365 from a data perspective. Detectable
from: no mention of proprietary datasets, training data, or data network effects
in the submission; the product is described purely as a workflow tool with no
data accumulation component; competitive moat claims rely entirely on features
or UX rather than data.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Proprietary data accumulation is one of the strongest moats in software — it creates
compounding switching costs (the customer's historical data is within the product),
improves the product over time without additional development investment (the model
gets better as more customers use it), and makes the product defensible against
feature-parity competitors (they can copy the features but not the data). Without
data accumulation: (1) switching costs are low — a competitor that matches the
feature set can win a customer's renewal; (2) the product does not improve over
time through usage — improvements require deliberate engineering investment rather
than emergent from data; (3) the competitive moat is purely execution-based —
a better-funded or larger competitor can replicate the product.
This flag does not disqualify on its own — many excellent businesses have low
data accumulation and win through distribution, brand, or integration depth.
But it requires that the moat claim be reassessed.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The submission explicitly claims data moat, proprietary data advantage, or AI
capability improvement through usage as a key competitive differentiator AND the
product architecture reveals no data accumulation mechanism — the claimed moat
does not exist. The mismatch between the moat claim and the product reality is
more concerning than the absence of the moat itself.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The product's moat is explicitly and credibly attributed to something other
than data — switching costs through workflow integration, network effects between
users, distribution advantage, or regulatory expertise embedded in the product.
(b) The product is early enough that data accumulation is a planned future capability
with a specific architecture described — the current version does not accumulate
data but v2 will, and the plan is credible.
(c) Data accumulation within a single customer account (personalisation, historical
analytics) exists even if cross-customer data network effects do not — intra-customer
data creates meaningful switching costs even without inter-customer data sharing.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "Does your product improve for a specific customer the longer they use it?
And does it improve for new customers because of what existing customers have done?"
The first question tests intra-customer data accumulation. The second tests
cross-customer data network effects. If both answers are no, the moat question
must be answered on a different dimension.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the submission claims a data moat but the product architecture contains no
data accumulation mechanism, test the moat claim against the actual product design —
a moat claim that is inconsistent with the product architecture is more concerning
than the absence of a data moat.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P05: FEATURE PARITY WITH FREE ALTERNATIVES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
Core product features are materially equivalent to free capabilities provided by
established platforms — Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Notion, Slack, HubSpot
free tier, Canva, GitHub Copilot, or similar — and the differentiation claim is
based primarily on UX improvement, design quality, or marginal feature additions
rather than a category-defining capability that free alternatives cannot provide.
Detectable from: competitive analysis that acknowledges free alternatives but
dismisses them based on UX ("our product is much more intuitive"); pricing that
implies willingness-to-pay significantly above zero for a product category that
is available free; or a demo that replicates functionality already present in a
widely distributed free product.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Free alternatives create the most structurally difficult competitive position in
software — the customer has no financial incentive to switch and the vendor must
justify a price premium based on a benefit differential that the customer may not
value sufficiently to pay for. The specific failure modes:
(1) Enterprise buyers use the free alternative for the same use case and the startup
cannot displace it because IT already supports the free product.
(2) The startup's users are power users who adopted it for marginal UX reasons —
a feature update to the free alternative eliminates the differential and triggers churn.
(3) The switching cost argument does not hold — customers can export data and
migrate to the free alternative in a day.
UX alone is not a durable competitive moat at the prices required for venture returns.
Microsoft, Google, and Notion invest billions in UX — a startup's UX advantage
has a measured half-life.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Core features are directly replicable using a free tier of a product the company's
target customers already use AND the pricing is above £100/month per user AND
the differentiation claim is entirely UX-based AND there is no evidence of
integrations, data accumulation, or workflow lock-in that the free alternative
cannot provide.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The product solves a specific workflow problem that the free alternatives address
generically — the specificity of the solution creates value that the generic free
alternative cannot replicate with the same time investment.
(b) The product has compliance, security, or audit capabilities that the free
alternatives do not — which is a genuine capability gap in regulated industries,
not a marginal UX improvement.
(c) The product is sold to enterprises where the free alternative is not approved
by IT security (consumer-grade products with weaker security profiles) — the
enterprise-grade version of the capability is the product.

VERIFICATION:
Test the core value proposition against the free alternative. Ask: "Could a technically
competent user replicate your core workflow in [free alternative] in less than a day?
If not, what specifically prevents them?" A product that cannot answer this question
with a specific, technical barrier to replication has a free alternative problem that
will surface at renewal conversations.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the competitive differentiation relies on UX superiority over a free alternative,
verify whether the differential is quantifiably meaningful to the target buyer —
a 10% UX improvement over a free product does not sustain a £10K/year price premium
in a downturn budget review.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P06: PRODUCT REQUIRES SIGNIFICANT WORKFLOW CHANGE FOR VALUE DELIVERY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: MEDIUM (LOW for enterprise ACV above £200K | HIGH for SMB ACV below £10K)

SIGNAL:
The product's primary value proposition requires the customer to substantially change
an existing workflow — replacing a process they have used for years with a new one —
rather than slotting into an existing workflow as a direct replacement or augmentation.
Detectable from: onboarding is described as requiring "training" or "change management";
time-to-value is stated as 3+ months; the submission describes a "transformation"
rather than a "replacement"; or early churn in the customer base is attributed to
"adoption challenges."

WHAT IT MEANS:
Behaviour change at the individual level is expensive to achieve and expensive to sustain.
Enterprise buyers who must run change management programmes for a new software product
face a cost and timeline that reduces willingness-to-pay and increases churn risk.
SMB buyers who must change their workflow simply do not — they revert to the familiar
process or abandon the product before reaching the value threshold.
The severity of this flag is highly dependent on ACV:
— At £200K+ ACV enterprise: behaviour change is tolerable because the ROI justification
  is large, dedicated implementation support is included, and the customer has a change
  management function. This is LOW severity.
— At £10K ACV SMB: behaviour change is FATAL. SMB customers will not invest 3 months
  to change their workflow for a £10K/year product. They will try it for 30 days,
  not change their process, and cancel.
The classic signal of this problem in the metrics: high trial-to-paid conversion
combined with very high 90-day churn — the product attracts customers but cannot
retain them through the behaviour change barrier.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The product requires significant workflow change AND the ACV is below £15K AND the
90-day churn rate (or trial cancellation rate) is above 25% AND the submission
attributes early churn to "customer education" or "adoption" rather than to a
product problem — the product is asking SMB customers to bear a change management
cost that is out of proportion to the value.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The product is sold into enterprise at ACV above £150K where behaviour change
is expected, professional services are included to manage it, and the ROI justification
is sufficiently large to drive internal change management investment by the buyer.
(b) The product delivers measurable value within the first 14 days — even if the
full value requires workflow change, there is a quick win that demonstrates the
benefit before the full change investment is required.
(c) The submission demonstrates that the 90-day retention rate is high (above 85%)
despite the workflow change requirement — customers are changing their workflows
because the value is compelling enough to justify it.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "What does a customer's first 90 days look like? What do they have to change
about how they work to get value from your product? And what is your 90-day
retention rate?" The combination of the workflow change description and the 90-day
retention rate tells you whether customers are making the change and finding it
worth it — or abandoning the product before completing the transition.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the product requires significant workflow change, check the ACV against the
severity threshold — a £200K enterprise product with workflow change is LOW risk;
a £5K SMB product with workflow change is HIGH risk — and verify the 90-day
retention rate before accepting the adoption story.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P07: AI CAPABILITY NOT DEFENSIBLE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The product is described as AI-powered and the AI capability is presented as a
core differentiator — but all described AI features could be replicated within
30 days by a developer with access to the same public foundation model APIs, with
no proprietary training data, no fine-tuned model, no proprietary architecture,
and no customer-specific data accumulation that improves the output. Detectable from:
AI capability is described purely in terms of the output quality without any description
of what makes the underlying model or data proprietary; the competitive moat is claimed
to be "AI" generically rather than a specific proprietary AI component; the submission
describes the product as "built on GPT-4" or "powered by Claude" as if the API access
itself is a differentiator.

WHAT IT MEANS:
In 2025–2026, wrapping a public foundation model API is not a defensible AI product —
it is a thin integration layer over a commodity API. Any competitor, including the
API provider itself, can build the same output with the same inputs. The specific
failure mode is the Foundation Model Displacement Risk from the AI-Native SaaS section
of the B2B SaaS Sector Library: the API provider improves its base model to achieve
the same output the vendor is providing as a premium feature, and the vendor's product
becomes a more expensive version of the base API.
Genuine AI defensibility requires at least one of:
(1) Proprietary training data that is not available to competitors and improves
    model performance on the specific task above the base model level.
(2) A proprietary fine-tuned model trained on accumulated customer data that gets
    better with each customer use.
(3) A workflow integration architecture so deep that switching away from the AI
    component requires rebuilding the surrounding workflow — the AI is embedded,
    not a standalone feature.
(4) Proprietary evaluation infrastructure — the ability to measure and improve
    AI output quality faster than competitors in the specific domain.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The submission describes AI as the primary competitive moat AND the AI capability
is entirely via public API calls with no proprietary data, training, or architecture
AND the API provider has already announced or released a feature that directly
competes with the vendor's primary use case — the moat has already been eroded.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The proprietary element is the workflow and data integration surrounding the AI —
the AI output itself is commodity, but the structured data inputs and workflow
embedding create a product that is substantially more useful than a raw API call
in the specific use case.
(b) The company is accumulating proprietary data with each customer interaction that
is being used to improve output quality — the current output may not be differentiated
but the data flywheel will create differentiation within 12–18 months.
(c) The company's differentiation is the domain expertise embedded in the prompting
architecture, evaluation framework, and output structure — which is genuinely
proprietary and non-obvious, even if the base model is shared.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "If OpenAI or Anthropic released a GPT-5 or Claude 4 that natively performed
your core use case at your quality level, how long would it take for your customers
to switch to the raw API? What would they lose?" The answer reveals whether the value
is in the AI output (commoditisable) or in the surrounding product architecture (durable).

PATTERN LESSON:
When AI is described as the primary moat, test whether the moat is in the model
(usually not defensible) or in the data, workflow, and architecture surrounding the
model (potentially defensible) — a product whose AI can be replicated by a developer
in 30 days has a distribution or workflow moat, not an AI moat.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P08: IMPLEMENTATION TIME EXCEEDS 6 MONTHS BEFORE FIRST VALUE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The submission states or implies that enterprise customers require more than 6 months
of implementation before experiencing any measurable value from the product — detectable
from stated implementation timelines, professional services descriptions that reference
multi-phase implementations spanning multiple quarters, or customer case studies where
the go-live date is described as 9–18 months after contract signing.

NOTE: This flag overlaps with Enterprise Red Flag F02 (Implementation Failure Rate)
but addresses a different risk dimension — not whether implementations fail, but
whether the time-to-first-value is structurally too long for customers to remain
committed through the process. Both flags can apply simultaneously.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A 6-month implementation before first value creates three compounding risks:
(1) Buying champion turnover. Enterprise buying champions change roles, companies,
or priorities on an 18–24 month cycle. A 6-month implementation to first value
means the champion who signed the contract has a 25–30% probability of having
changed roles before the customer has received any return on the investment.
When the champion leaves mid-implementation, the new executive inherits a project
with 6 months of cost and no visible value — and the first question is whether to
continue or cut the programme.
(2) Budget re-evaluation. Enterprise budgets are reviewed quarterly and annually.
A product that has consumed 6 months of budget without visible value output is
at risk of being cut in the next budget cycle, regardless of the contracted term.
(3) Competitor displacement window. A competitor has 6 months to enter the account,
demonstrate quick-win value, and present itself as the alternative to the incumbent
who has not yet delivered.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Average implementation time before first value is more than 9 months AND the ACV
is below £200K AND the company has a documented case of a customer churning or
failing to renew after a long implementation — the implementation risk has already
converted into a commercial outcome.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The implementation delivers documented interim value at defined milestones
before the 6-month mark — the final go-live may take 12 months but the customer
has a working component generating measurable output by month 2.
(b) The implementation is for an ERP or core systems replacement where 6–12 month
timelines are industry standard and customers are experienced in managing the process.
(c) The ACV is above £500K — at this price point, customers have an internal project
team, executive sponsorship, and formal programme management that reduces champion
turnover risk and budget re-evaluation risk during implementation.

VERIFICATION:
Request the implementation timeline for each of the last 5 go-lives: contract
signing date, first measurable value delivery date, and full go-live date. Ask:
"What value does the customer receive in the first 90 days after signing? And
what does the 90-day experience look like from the customer's perspective?" A company
that can describe specific 90-day value milestones has engineered the implementation
for retention; one that describes only the end-state has not.

PATTERN LESSON:
When implementation time before first value exceeds 6 months, assess whether there
are documented 90-day value milestones within the implementation — the presence or
absence of early value milestones is the primary predictor of whether customers
survive the long implementation without churning or de-prioritising the project.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P09: NO PRODUCT USAGE DATA PROVIDED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The submission contains ARR and customer count data but no product usage metrics —
no daily or monthly active users, no feature adoption rates, no session frequency,
no engagement depth indicators — despite the company being at a stage where product
instrumentation should be standard. Detectable from: the submission discusses
customer satisfaction in qualitative terms only ("customers love the product,"
"strong NPS") without any usage data; or product usage data is absent in a submission
that otherwise contains detailed financial metrics.

WHAT IT MEANS:
ARR is a measure of contracted revenue — it tells Alex how much customers have agreed
to pay. Usage data tells Alex how much customers are actually using the product they
paid for. The gap between the two is the retention risk indicator. A company with
£2M ARR and zero usage data has told Alex the first half of the story and withheld
the second. Three possible reasons for the omission:
(1) Usage data is unflattering. Active usage is low relative to the customer count
or ARR — customers have paid but are not using the product. This is a leading indicator
of churn: customers who do not use a product do not renew it.
(2) Usage is not tracked. The company has not instrumented its product. This is a
product management maturity problem at seed stage and a serious gap at Series A —
a company that does not measure usage does not know where its product is creating
value and where it is not.
(3) The usage data is not considered relevant by the founder. This is a signal about
the founder's product instincts — a founder who does not think about usage metrics
is not running a product-led company, which may or may not be appropriate for the
business model.
In all three cases, the absence of usage data reduces Alex's ability to assess
the true retention outlook.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company is at Series A with more than £1M ARR AND has no product usage tracking
infrastructure in place AND the submission cannot provide any usage metric for any
customer — the product intelligence gap means the company cannot optimise for
retention or identify at-risk customers before they churn.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The product is enterprise software with a small number of large accounts where
usage is tracked through customer success activity logs rather than automated
instrumentation — the company can describe specific usage behaviour per account.
(b) The product is genuinely new (less than 6 months post-launch) and instrumentation
is being built as part of the current engineering sprint — the omission is a
timing gap, not a structural gap.
(c) The product operates in a regulated environment where automated usage tracking
requires customer consent and data governance that has not yet been established —
the absence is a compliance consideration, not an oversight.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "What is your DAU/MAU ratio? What percentage of your customers logged in
in the last 30 days? What is your most-used feature?" A product team that is
tracking usage will answer immediately with specifics. A team that is not tracking
usage will give qualitative answers ("customers use it regularly") without numbers.
The quality of the answer tells Alex more than the metric itself.

PATTERN LESSON:
When no product usage data is provided alongside ARR, ask for DAU/MAU and 30-day
active rate for the last 3 months — if the answer is qualitative rather than
numeric, treat the retention outlook as unverified and note this in the risk section.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG P10: PRODUCT ROADMAP DRIVEN ENTIRELY BY CUSTOMER REQUESTS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Product
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The product roadmap described in the submission consists entirely of features
requested by existing customers, with no evidence of proactive category development,
founder-initiated product vision, or investment in capabilities that customers have
not yet asked for. Detectable from: the roadmap is described as "driven by customer
feedback"; every roadmap item has a named customer attached; the product's past
release notes list features that were each requested by specific accounts; or the
founder cannot describe a product bet they made that customers did not anticipate needing.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A product roadmap that is 100% customer-request-driven has a specific failure mode —
the Feature Factory (Training Library, PASS Deal pattern). The product becomes a
custom software shop rather than a category-defining product. The specific risks:
(1) The product becomes increasingly specific to the needs of existing customers
and increasingly irrelevant to the next generation of target customers — who have
different needs that the product does not address.
(2) Core product experience deteriorates. No engineering time is allocated to
improving the fundamental value proposition — only to adding features on the margins.
DAU/MAU declines 6–9 months before the team notices because engagement with the
core product is weakening even as the feature count grows.
(3) The product loses coherence. A roadmap assembled from individual customer requests
does not have an underlying vision — features conflict, the UX becomes inconsistent,
and onboarding new customers becomes harder as the product adds complexity.
(4) Expansion sales become harder. A customer who bought the product for a specific
use case finds that the roadmap does not extend the product into adjacent use cases
they care about — NRR stalls because the product is not growing in the directions
the customers want to expand into.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
More than 80% of the last 12 months of shipped features were customer-requested AND
DAU/MAU or core feature engagement has declined in the same period AND the founding
team cannot describe a single proactive product bet they have made in the last 12
months — the product is in Feature Factory mode and the NRR impact is already beginning.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Customer-driven features represent less than 60% of the roadmap and the remaining
40% is founder-initiated product vision — customer input informs but does not entirely
determine the roadmap.
(b) The company is in a customer discovery phase at seed stage where the primary goal
is to learn from early customers — a 100% customer-driven roadmap at seed is appropriate;
at Series A it is a concern.
(c) The customer-requested features are clustered around a coherent product thesis —
the requests are pointing in the same direction and the founder is synthesising them
into a category-defining product strategy, not just building each request independently.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "Tell me about the last significant product bet you made that customers did not
ask for. What was the hypothesis and how did it play out?" A product-led founder
will have a clear answer — a product vision bet, a category-creating feature, a
pricing innovation. A founder in Feature Factory mode will struggle to answer because
every product decision has been customer-reactive rather than vision-driven.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the product roadmap is described as customer-driven, ask the founder to describe
the last proactive product bet they made that no customer asked for — the ability
to answer this question distinguishes a product vision from a feature backlog.
