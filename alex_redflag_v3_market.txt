ALEX — RED FLAG LIBRARY
CATEGORY 3: MARKET RED FLAGS
Version 1.0. Built: April 2026.
10 flags. Apply mechanically to every deal submission.
These flags are observable from a submission without a meeting.
Two analysts applying these rules reach the same conclusion.
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION RULE:
For every deal submission, Alex checks all 10 flags before completing
the screening note. Flags are not discretionary — if the signal is
present, the flag is raised. Flags are then weighed in aggregate.
One CRITICAL flag = automatic deep investigation required.
Two or more HIGH flags = note this in the Risk Assessment section.
Market flags often interact with financial flags — a shrinking market
combined with declining NRR is more serious than either alone.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M01: NO CREDIBLE WHY NOW
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The submission cannot articulate a specific enabling event — a regulatory change,
a technology inflection, a cost threshold crossed, a behaviour shift — that occurred
in the last 36 months and created this market opportunity now, where it did not
exist or was not viable 5 years ago. The "Why Now" section of the pitch, if present,
describes the market opportunity in general terms (e.g. "digital transformation,"
"the rise of AI," "growing demand for X") rather than a specific, dateable event.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Market timing is one of the most significant factors in venture outcomes — a correct
idea entered at the wrong time produces the same financial return as a wrong idea.
The Why Now test identifies whether the company has identified a genuine timing
inflection or is simply entering an established market hoping to take share through
execution alone. Without a credible Why Now, three risks materialise:
(1) The company is entering a market that is already maturing — the early-mover
advantage has been captured by incumbents and the startup is competing on features
against well-resourced players without structural advantage.
(2) The technology or infrastructure that makes the product viable has not yet arrived —
the company is ahead of the market, which is economically equivalent to being wrong.
(3) The market timing rationale has not been thought through — which raises questions
about the depth of the founder's understanding of their own market.
The best Why Now statements are specific and verifiable: "The FDA approved AI-assisted
diagnostic software in 2023, creating the first reimbursable pathway for our product";
"GPT-4 reduced inference cost 94% since 2022, making our unit economics viable for
the first time"; "The FCA's Consumer Duty requirement (July 2023) made our compliance
product mandatory for every UK retail financial firm."

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The submission contains no Why Now rationale AND the market has existed in recognisable
form for more than 7 years AND at least two well-funded companies have already attempted
the same product category — the combination suggests not a timing inflection but
a repeated entry into a market that has not been cracked, with no new structural advantage.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The founder articulates a specific Why Now in the meeting that was absent from
the written submission — the omission is a presentation gap, not a thinking gap.
(b) The Why Now is implied by verifiable public events (a specific regulation,
a technology release date, a market event) that are in the submission even if not
explicitly framed as the timing rationale.
(c) The company is a market share play in an established, growing market where
timing is less critical than execution — in which case Alex should note the absence
of a timing inflection and assess the competitive differentiation instead.

VERIFICATION:
Ask directly: "What happened in the last 3 years that makes this the right moment
to build this company? What would have prevented you from building this 5 years ago?"
A founder who has genuinely thought about market timing answers immediately with
a specific event. A founder who has not gives a general answer about market growth
or customer demand that could apply to any point in the last decade.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the Why Now is absent or described in general terms, ask the founder to name
the specific event in the last 36 months that created the window — the specificity
and confidence of the answer determines whether timing is a structural advantage
or an assumption.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M02: MARKET DEFINED TOO BROADLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The TAM figure in the submission is sourced from an industry report that measures
the total size of a broad industry category rather than the specific segment this
product addresses at this price point for this buyer profile. Common indicators:
TAM is described as "the global [industry] market" rather than "the addressable
market for [specific product type] sold to [specific buyer type]"; the TAM figure
is in the trillions or tens of billions for a product with an ACV below £20K;
the TAM figure comes from a research firm (Gartner, IDC, McKinsey) measuring
an industry category that is 5–10x broader than the company's actual serviceable market.

SPECIFIC PATTERNS THAT TRIGGER THIS FLAG:
— "The global HR software market is £18B" for a product that serves only UK SMBs
— "The healthcare market is £3T" for a specific NHS workflow tool with a £30K ACV
— "The cybersecurity market is £200B" for a point solution addressing one specific threat vector
— TAM figure does not change materially if the company's product is removed from
  the market — which means the TAM does not measure the company's specific opportunity

WHAT IT MEANS:
An inflated TAM does not change the underlying business — but it does reveal one
of two things: (a) the founder has not done the analytical work to calculate their
actual serviceable addressable market (SAM), which raises questions about the rigour
applied to financial planning and market sizing generally; or (b) the founder has
done the work and the real SAM is too small to present to investors, so the broader
market figure is used as a substitute. The second case is more common and more
concerning. The true market size matters for one practical reason: a company that
cannot build a £50M+ ARR business in its primary market cannot return a fund-moving
result at venture scale, regardless of execution quality.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The stated TAM is more than 20x the implied SAM (calculated as: ACV × realistic
buyer universe in the company's primary market) AND the company is seeking Series A
capital at a valuation that implies a 10x return requiring £500M+ ARR — the
return requirement cannot be achieved within the realistic addressable market.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The submission distinguishes between TAM (total industry), SAM (addressable
with current product), and SOM (realistically capturable in 5 years) — the TAM
figure is context, not the investment case.
(b) The company has a credible multi-product or multi-market expansion path
that converts the broader TAM into a reachable addressable market over a
10-year horizon, with stage-gated milestones for each market entry.
(c) The SAM is large enough on its own to support the investment thesis — the
TAM figure is irrelevant if the SAM calculation is sound.

VERIFICATION:
Calculate the implied SAM: identify the company's specific buyer profile (title,
company size, geography), estimate the realistic universe of such buyers, multiply
by ACV. Ask: "How many companies of the type you target exist in the UK/EU market?
What percentage do you think you could realistically capture at full penetration?"
The arithmetic either supports the investment thesis or does not — the reported
TAM from a research firm does not change the calculation.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the TAM figure comes from a broad industry report, calculate the implied SAM
from buyer universe × ACV and test whether the investment thesis survives the
smaller number before accepting the reported TAM as the addressable opportunity.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M03: PRIOR FAILED ATTEMPTS UNADDRESSED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Two or more companies have raised venture capital, built a product in this exact
category, and subsequently failed (shut down, pivoted away entirely, or been acquired
at distressed valuation) — and the submission does not acknowledge these prior attempts
or explain what structural difference makes this attempt different. The prior failures
are identifiable through a basic Crunchbase or press search for the product category.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A category with prior failed funded attempts is not automatically uninvestable —
many great companies succeeded where predecessors failed because a specific enabling
condition changed. But the founder's treatment of prior failures is diagnostic:
(a) A founder who proactively acknowledges prior failures and explains the structural
difference demonstrates market knowledge and intellectual honesty. This is a positive signal.
(b) A founder who is unaware of prior failures has not done the basic competitive
and market research expected of a company in this space. This is a negative signal
about preparation.
(c) A founder who is aware of prior failures and omits them from the pitch is hoping
the investor does not find them — a transparency failure that is more serious than
the failures themselves.
The specific question the prior failures answer: was this market not viable because
of the wrong technology, the wrong timing, the wrong team, the wrong business model —
or because the market simply does not want this solution at any price?

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Three or more funded companies have failed in this exact category within the last
7 years AND the current submission does not acknowledge any of them AND the current
company cannot articulate a structural difference from the prior attempts beyond
"better execution" or "we have a better team" — because execution quality alone
is not a structural market insight.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The prior failures are proactively disclosed in the submission with a specific
account of why each failed and why the current company is structurally different —
the Why Now (M01) answers the prior failure question simultaneously.
(b) The prior failures were in an adjacent but distinct category — a different
buyer type, a different technology approach, or a different price point — and the
current company's approach genuinely differs.
(c) The prior failures occurred more than 10 years ago and the technology, regulatory,
or economic context has changed materially since then.

VERIFICATION:
Search Crunchbase for the product category + "shutdown" or "acquired." Search
TechCrunch and press archives for the category name from 3–10 years ago. Identify
any company that raised more than £2M in this category and is no longer operating.
Present the findings to the founder: "We found [X] and [Y] who attempted this between
[years]. What happened and what is different about your approach?" The quality of
the answer is the primary signal.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the product category has prior funded failures, present the specific companies
and ask the founder to explain the structural difference — a founder who cannot name
or explain the prior failures has not done the market research that underpins the investment thesis.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M04: MARKET CONSOLIDATING AROUND INCUMBENTS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The addressable market is in an active consolidation phase — 2 or more large players
have made acquisitions of the category's earlier startups within the last 24 months,
the number of independent funded competitors has declined, and press coverage of
the category has shifted from "emerging market" to "dominated by X and Y." Detectable
from Crunchbase M&A data, press coverage of the category, and the competitive landscape
described in the submission.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Market consolidation creates a closing window for new entrants — specifically, it means:
(1) The best-performing startups in the category have already been acquired, which
means the most credible exit pathway for a new entrant now requires out-competing
entrenched acquiree products with the resources of a large company behind them.
(2) The remaining independent competitors are either the category leaders who have
not yet exited or the laggards who could not attract an acquisition offer — neither
profile is a good signal for a new entrant.
(3) Enterprise buyers in a consolidating market begin standardising on the dominant
platforms, making it progressively harder for a new entrant to get procurement
consideration — the buyer says "we're going all-in on Salesforce / ServiceNow /
Microsoft" and the new entrant has no answer.
The window for a new entrant to achieve venture-scale traction in a consolidating
market is typically 24–36 months from the point of consolidation onset — after which
the structural headwinds become prohibitive without a true category-defining differentiation.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The category has seen 3 or more M&A transactions in the last 24 months AND the
acquirers include at least 2 of the company's potential exit counterparties AND
the company does not have a differentiation that is materially distinct from the
acquired products — because the exit pathway has narrowed to a small number of
buyers who have already made their strategic bets.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company occupies a specific sub-segment of the consolidating market that
has not yet been acquired and where the large acquirers have demonstrated no interest —
a wedge strategy that targets the underserved segment the incumbents cannot or will
not address.
(b) The consolidation is creating customer dissatisfaction — acquiree products are
being under-invested, pricing is rising, and customers are actively looking for
alternatives — creating a counter-cycle opportunity.
(c) The company has already established a relationship with one of the consolidating
incumbents as a strategic partner — which makes it a potential acquisition target
rather than a new entrant competing against them.

VERIFICATION:
Search Crunchbase M&A for the product category in the last 24 months. Identify
acquirers and acquired companies. Ask: "Which companies in your category have been
acquired in the last 2 years, and who acquired them? What does that mean for your
exit strategy?" A founder who sees the consolidation as an opportunity (potential
acquirer list) rather than only a threat has thought through the competitive dynamics.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a category shows 2 or more M&A transactions among potential exit counterparties
in the last 24 months, assess whether the consolidation is creating a closing window
or a counter-cycle opportunity — the two scenarios require opposite investment decisions.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M05: REGULATORY DEPENDENCY FOR MARKET EXISTENCE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: CRITICAL

SIGNAL:
The entire commercial opportunity depends on a specific regulation, government
mandate, or subsidy regime remaining in place — AND that regulation or mandate
is currently under active review, facing political challenge, or subject to a
consultation process that could result in its withdrawal or significant modification
within the investment horizon. Detectable from: the product's value proposition
explicitly citing a specific regulation as the source of customer obligation;
a statutory review or consultation documented in public record; or a change of
government or regulatory body leadership that signals policy direction.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A regulatory tailwind is a legitimate market timing advantage — but a business
whose existence depends on a single regulation is not a technology business,
it is a regulatory arbitrage. When the regulation changes, the market disappears.
Three specific risk profiles:
(1) The regulation is removed: the customer no longer has an obligation to buy the
product. Revenue drops to zero in the next contract cycle. There is no alternative
use case — the product was built to solve a compliance requirement, not a business problem.
(2) The regulation is modified: the product's compliance applicability is reduced.
Revenue from affected use cases declines. The company must rebuild for the new
regulatory perimeter.
(3) The regulation is delayed in implementation: customers defer purchase decisions
while waiting for enforcement clarity. Revenue that was imminent moves by 12–24 months.
See also Climate Sector Library (Subsidy Cliff failure pattern) and Fintech Sector
Library (Regulatory Licence Trap) for sector-specific instantiations of this flag.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
More than 60% of the company's current or projected ARR comes from a compliance
obligation created by a single specific regulation AND that regulation is currently
subject to an active government consultation, statutory review, or legal challenge
that could result in its removal or significant modification within 36 months.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The regulation creates the initial market entry but the product solves an
underlying business problem that exists independently of the regulation — compliance
is the wedge, not the entire value proposition. Customers would continue using
the product even if the compliance obligation was removed because it delivers
operational value.
(b) The regulation is well-established, bipartisan in political support, and has
survived multiple government changes without substantive challenge — the regulatory
risk is theoretical rather than live.
(c) The company has already diversified beyond the single regulatory driver —
other revenue streams represent more than 40% of ARR and are growing faster than
the compliance-driven revenue.

VERIFICATION:
Identify the specific regulation cited as the market driver. Search the relevant
government body (FCA, MHRA, OFGEM, DESNZ, EU Commission) for any open consultation,
statutory review, or reform proposal affecting that regulation. Ask: "If this
regulation was withdrawn or substantially modified tomorrow, what percentage of
your current customers would still use your product? For what reason?" The answer
reveals whether the product has standalone value or is purely compliance-driven.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the entire market opportunity is attributed to a specific regulation, verify
whether the regulation is under active review and whether the product delivers
value independently of the compliance obligation — the answers determine whether
the company is building a business or a regulatory arbitrage.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M06: GEOGRAPHY TOO SMALL FOR VENTURE RETURNS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The company's primary addressable market — applying the correct buyer universe
and ACV — is insufficient to produce a venture-scale return (£100M+ ARR or
£500M+ enterprise value) even at full market penetration within the company's
stated geography, and the submission contains no credible path to market expansion
beyond the primary geography.

CALCULATION METHOD:
Realistic buyer universe in stated geography × ACV = Maximum SAM at 100% penetration.
Venture-grade threshold: SAM must support £50M+ ARR at 20–30% realistic penetration.
A company with 500 potential buyers at £50K ACV has a SAM of £25M — maximum ARR
at 100% penetration is £25M. At a realistic 25% penetration, ARR is £6.25M.
This is not venture-scale in isolation.
Geography-specific ceilings that frequently trigger this flag:
— UK-only B2B products with fewer than 2,000 realistic buyers
— Single EU country products with fewer than 5,000 realistic buyers
— Vertical SaaS with a UK buyer universe below 500 companies
— Gov-tech products limited to a single government department

WHAT IT MEANS:
A geography-constrained market is not inherently a bad business — it can be an
excellent private equity, growth equity, or bootstrapped business. But venture
capital requires returns that come from a fund-moving exit: a company must achieve
£500M+ enterprise value to return a meaningful portion of a £50M+ fund. A company
that achieves 100% of its UK market and generates £20M ARR at 8x multiple produces
£160M enterprise value — which is a reasonable outcome but not a venture fund-returner.
The question is not whether the business is good — it is whether the market structure
allows the company to grow to venture-scale.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The realistic SAM (buyer universe × ACV) in the primary geography is below £30M
AND the submission contains no evidence of a credible expansion plan beyond the
primary geography AND the company is seeking venture capital at a valuation implying
a £100M+ exit.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The submission includes a staged expansion plan with specific market entry dates,
GTM strategy, and evidence of demand in secondary markets — UK is the proof of
concept for a global rollout.
(b) The ACV expansion path is credible — the company will sell more modules or expand
from one buyer type to adjacent buyer types, increasing the SAM without geographic expansion.
(c) The business is raising growth equity or PE-track capital, not traditional VC —
in which case the geography constraint is not a disqualifier because the return
expectation is different.

VERIFICATION:
Calculate the realistic buyer universe from first principles: company size filter ×
industry filter × geography filter = number of potential buyers. Multiply by ACV.
Ask: "At 25% market penetration in the UK alone — which would be exceptional —
what would your ARR be? And what is your plan for the markets beyond the UK?"
The arithmetic and the answer reveal whether the company has a viable venture path.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the stated geography is UK-only or a single country, calculate the realistic
SAM from buyer universe × ACV before assessing growth rate — a company growing
at 100% annually in a market that caps at £15M ARR is not a venture investment
regardless of the growth trajectory.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M07: MARKET TIMING PEAK ALREADY PASSED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Industry-level data — VC investment into the category, media coverage volume, search
trend data, or disclosed revenue growth rates of the category's public comparables —
shows a clear peak 2–3 years ago followed by a sustained decline, while the company
in the submission is entering the category for the first time or has only been
operating for 12–18 months. The category's early winners have already consolidated
market share and the growth rates of public comparables in the space have declined
materially from their peak.

DETECTABLE FROM:
— Category VC investment peaked in 2021–2022 (common for many SaaS categories that
  benefited from COVID tailwinds) and has declined 40–60% annually since
— Public company peers in the space are trading at 5–8x ARR vs 30–50x at peak
— Press coverage of the category has shifted from "the future of X" to "X faces
  headwinds" or "consolidation in the X market"
— Growth rates of the category's best performers have declined from 80–100% YoY
  to 20–40% YoY

WHAT IT MEANS:
Entering a market after the timing peak is not impossible — late entrants can win
with genuine differentiation. But the structural conditions at peak and post-peak
are different in ways that matter for venture returns:
(1) Customer willingness to adopt new solutions is lower post-peak — the early
adopters have already adopted something, and the mainstream buyers are now waiting
for the market to stabilise before switching.
(2) The available pool of equity capital for the category is contracting —
investors who funded the category at peak are managing existing portfolio
companies through a down-cycle, not deploying into new ones.
(3) Exit multiples have compressed — a company that exits at 8x ARR in a category
that peaked at 30x ARR requires much higher ARR to produce the same absolute return.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Industry-level VC investment into the category has declined more than 50% from its
3-year peak AND the company is entering the category with less than £500K ARR
AND has no structural differentiation from the companies that built the category
during its growth phase — a late entrant with no differentiation in a declining
category faces two headwinds simultaneously: weak market and strong competition.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has identified a sub-segment of the category that has not yet
been served by the peak-era companies — the market timing is late for the
category overall but early for this specific application.
(b) The company has a genuinely disruptive cost position or technology advantage
that allows it to serve customers the peak-era companies could not reach —
creating new demand rather than competing for existing demand.
(c) The category declined due to macro conditions (interest rate shock, COVID
normalisation) rather than structural market failure — and the underlying demand
has persisted, creating a re-entry opportunity.

VERIFICATION:
Search Crunchbase for VC investment into the category by year. Search Google Trends
for the category's primary keywords. Look at the revenue growth rates disclosed by
the category's leading public companies or late-stage private companies over the last
3 years. Ask: "How do you view the timing of your entry into this market relative to
when the category was established? What advantages does late entry give you?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When VC investment into the category peaked more than 2 years ago and has since
declined, assess whether the company has a structural advantage that compensates
for adverse timing — late entry without differentiation in a declining category
is one of the most common patterns in deals that fail to reach Series B.

FLAG M08: TARGET CUSTOMER DOES NOT HAVE BUDGET FOR THIS CATEGORY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The stated target customer type does not typically have an existing budget allocated
to the product category — meaning every sale requires the customer to create a new
budget line rather than redirecting spend from an existing category. Detectable when:
the target customer is a small or micro-business (fewer than 10 employees) being
asked to pay above £3K/year for a B2B SaaS product; or the target buyer is a
department or function that does not conventionally control discretionary software
spend (e.g. a clinical team being asked to approve a £50K software purchase,
or a manufacturing floor manager being asked to approve a £30K data analytics tool).

WHAT IT MEANS:
B2B sales are fastest and most capital-efficient when the product replaces an
existing budget line — the customer is already spending money on this problem,
and the vendor is simply redirecting that spend. When no budget line exists, every
sale is a budget creation event: the founder must convince the customer that the
problem is worth funding, get that funding approved through a budget process that
may run on an annual cycle, and then compete for approval alongside other new budget
requests. This extends sales cycles, increases churn risk (unbudgeted spend is the
first thing cut in a downturn), and makes the revenue structurally less durable than
spend that comes from an established budget category.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The target customer is a company with fewer than 20 employees AND the ACV is above
£10K AND the product category does not have an established budget precedent in
companies of that size AND there is no evidence in the submission that customers
have created new budget lines to pay for the product — the sales story is theoretical.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The product demonstrably displaces an existing spend — it replaces a headcount,
eliminates a third-party service cost, or reduces a measurable operational expense
by an amount that creates a clear ROI case that justifies the new budget creation.
(b) The company sells at a price point low enough to be approved from discretionary
spend without a formal budget process (below £500/month for most SMB buyers).
(c) The company's evidence shows that customers have in fact created new budget lines —
the theoretical concern is contradicted by actual commercial behaviour.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "When a customer decides to buy your product, what budget does it come from?
Is that budget already allocated before they meet you, or do they have to create a
new budget line?" The answer tells you whether the company is capturing existing
spend or creating new spend — and whether the sales cycle reflects budget cycle
dependency or product willingness-to-pay.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the target customer type does not have an established budget category for the
product, verify whether existing customers are paying from a pre-existing budget line
or from a new discretionary allocation — the difference determines the structural
durability of the revenue and the length of the sales cycle.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M09: MARKET REQUIRES SIMULTANEOUS BEHAVIOUR CHANGE AT SCALE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The product's core value proposition only materialises when a critical mass of users,
suppliers, or buyers adopts it simultaneously — before this critical mass is reached,
the product delivers little or no value to individual early adopters, creating a cold
start problem that the company has not yet solved. Detectable from language like
"once enough companies use our platform," "the value increases as more suppliers
join," or from a network-effects business model description where the early-adopter
value proposition is weak.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Products that require simultaneous adoption face the most difficult GTM challenge
in technology — the chicken-and-egg problem. Every individual early adopter has
less incentive to adopt because the network is not yet valuable, which means the
network does not become valuable, which means the next early adopter has even
less incentive. The companies that have solved this problem historically did so
through one of three strategies: (1) subsidising one side of the market heavily
until critical mass is reached (Uber, Airbnb); (2) targeting a highly concentrated
geography or community where critical mass is achievable with limited capital (dating
apps, local marketplaces); (3) building standalone value that does not require
the network to be useful in its early form (Slack — useful as a team communication
tool for 2 people before it became a platform). Without one of these three strategies
explicitly described in the submission, the behaviour change problem is unsolved.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The product delivers zero standalone value to a single early adopter AND the company
has not subsidised one side of the market AND critical mass has not been achieved in
any defined geography or customer segment AND the company is at Series A or beyond
— meaning the cold start problem has persisted through seed without resolution.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has a documented critical mass strategy — a specific geography,
community, or customer segment where they will achieve minimum viable density before
expanding — and has early evidence that the strategy is working in the target segment.
(b) The product delivers standalone value to individual early adopters that is
independent of the network — the network effect is an enhancement, not the minimum
viable value proposition.
(c) The company has already achieved critical mass in one market segment and is
using that as the reference case for subsequent expansions.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "What is the value of your product to the first customer who adopts it, before
any other customers are using it?" If the answer is "limited" or "it's better with
more users," ask: "What is your strategy for reaching the critical mass required
for your core value proposition to work? And where are you in that journey?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When the core value proposition requires simultaneous adoption by multiple parties,
verify whether the company has achieved critical mass in any segment and has a
documented strategy for each subsequent segment — the absence of a specific critical
mass milestone is a cold start problem that does not resolve through additional capital alone.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG M10: COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE OMITS FUNDED COMPETITORS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Market
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The submission's competitive analysis — whether a 2x2 matrix, a list, or a narrative
description — omits one or more competitors that are (a) verifiably operating in the
same product category, (b) have raised more than £2M in disclosed funding, and (c)
are identifiable within 5 minutes of a basic Crunchbase or Google search for the
product category. The omission may be of direct competitors, well-funded near-competitors
whose expansion trajectory brings them into direct competition, or large incumbents
who have announced a competing product in the last 12 months.

WHAT IT MEANS:
The competitive landscape slide is one of the most systematically distorted elements
of a pitch deck. Founders have a structural incentive to minimise the competition —
a crowded competitive landscape is harder to fund. The result is competitive slides
that are technically accurate but strategically incomplete: they show the weakest
competitors (legacy on-premise systems, expensive consultants, Excel spreadsheets)
and omit the most dangerous ones (well-funded startups with similar approaches
that are 18 months ahead). This creates three problems:
(1) Alex and the firm make investment decisions based on an incomplete competitive
picture — materially affecting the market position assessment.
(2) The omission signals that the founder either does not know about the competitor
(lack of market research) or knows and omitted them deliberately (lack of transparency).
Neither is reassuring.
(3) The undisclosed competitor is likely to be the first thing due diligence surfaces —
which damages the firm's confidence in the accuracy of other submission claims.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A funded competitor with more than £5M raised and materially similar product is
omitted from the competitive analysis AND when the omission is identified, the founder
either cannot explain the competitor's approach or provides an explanation that reveals
they were unaware of a major competitive development (e.g. a product launch, a major
customer win) in the last 12 months.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The omitted company is in an adjacent category and the founder has a clear and
credible explanation for why it is not a direct competitor — the omission was a
deliberate categorisation decision, not an oversight.
(b) The omission is of a large incumbent whose product the founder has analysed in
detail and can speak to — the incumbent appears in their analysis but not on the
competitive matrix because it operates on a different competitive dimension.
(c) The omitted company is very recently funded (within the last 90 days) and the
submission predates the news — a timing gap, not a research failure.

VERIFICATION:
Before every meeting, run a 5-minute Crunchbase search for the product category
and identify any company with more than £2M raised operating in the space. Cross-
reference against the submission's competitive slide. If a funded company is not
mentioned, ask: "We noticed [Company X] is operating in a similar space and has
raised [£Y] — how do you view their approach and where do you see the main
differences between your product and theirs?" The depth of the answer reveals
the quality of the founder's competitive intelligence.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the competitive landscape appears incomplete, run a 5-minute Crunchbase search
before the first meeting and identify any funded company omitted from the analysis —
the founder's response to the question reveals whether the omission was research
failure or deliberate framing, and both are material signals.
