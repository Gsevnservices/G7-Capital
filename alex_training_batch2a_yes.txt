YES DEAL 011 — PEER-TO-PEER CURRENCY EXCHANGE
Reconstructed from: Index Ventures / TransferWise Series A review, early 2013
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
COMPANY:         International money transfer platform using real exchange rate
STAGE:           Series A
AMOUNT RAISING:  $6M at ~$30M pre-money
STATUS:          PRIORITY REVIEW

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:
Two Estonian expats in London who solved their own FX problem have built
a money transfer business growing 25% month-on-month with £30M in monthly
transfer volume and an NPS above 75 — the highest recorded score in UK
financial services at this stage. The insight is elegant and non-obvious:
banks charge a 3–5% hidden margin by using a degraded exchange rate.
TransferWise uses the real mid-market rate and charges a small transparent
fee. Customers save 60–80% on the cost of international transfers. The
founders are a genuinely complementary pair — one built Skype from inside,
one is a management consultant who experienced the problem personally and
understands the regulatory landscape. The business is pre-profitability
but the unit economics are clearly superior to every incumbent in the space.
This is a Priority Review.

G7 SCORE: 79/100  |  VALUATION: FAIR
Thesis Fit:       18/20  — B2B/B2C fintech infrastructure. Strong fit
Market:           17/20  — Global FX transfer market. Large. Underserved
Team:             21/25  — Complementary pair. Type 8. Strong archetype signals
Traction:         14/20  — £30M/month volume, 80K users, NPS 75+
Business Model:   7/10   — Transaction fee model. Clear. Scalable. Regulatory risk
Risk:             2/5    — Bank partner dependency. Regulatory. Incumbent response

01 — THESIS FIT
Fit: STRONG FIT
Consumer and SMB fintech with potential to expand into B2B payments.
Solves a problem affecting hundreds of millions of people. First time
we have seen this exact model at this quality of execution.

02 — MARKET
TAM: Global consumer FX transfer market ~$700B annually (2013).
     Bank-mediated cross-border transfers: $400B.
     Fee pool at 3–5% margin: $12–20B annually.
SAM: UK to EU/US/emerging market corridors: ~$80B (TransferWise's
     initial geography). Fee pool: ~$3B.
SOM: 1% market share in 3 years = $800M transfer volume → ~$4M ARR.
     Achievable given current 25% MoM growth trajectory.

Market quality: STRONG. Three independent timing catalysts converging:
— UK expat population growing (1.5M Poles, 500K+ Estonians/Latvians in UK)
— Smartphone banking normalising — users comfortable managing money via app
— Public anger at bank FX practices growing (BBC coverage, consumer press)
Why now vs 5 years ago: Smartphone penetration + API banking infrastructure
(Stripe, Twilio demonstrating what's possible) + growing distrust of banks
post-2008. The window opened approximately 2011. We are in it now.
Key market risk: Banks respond with competing product. Regulatory clampdown
on e-money institutions. Transfer volume is not the same as profit.

03 — COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE
Direct: World First, Moneycorp, Western Union (legacy), OFX
Indirect: High street banks (NatWest, Barclays, HSBC international transfer)
Failed attempts: Multiple prior FX startups failed on customer acquisition
cost — could not compete with banks' branch distribution.
Incumbent risk: MEDIUM. Banks are structurally unable to offer real
exchange rate without destroying their own FX revenue. This is their
business model conflict — they cannot compete without losing a major
profit centre. TransferWise's right to win is not technology — it is
the pricing model the incumbents cannot match.
Competitive risk: OFX and World First are real — but they serve B2B
primarily and have legacy cost structures. None have NPS above 50.

04 — TEAM
Founder-market fit: STRONG. Type 8 — Complementary Co-founder Pair.
Taavet Hinrikus: First employee at Skype. Scaled a consumer internet
product from zero to global. Understands viral growth, press, and
consumer product design. Network includes every major EU tech investor.
Kristo Käärmann: Former PwC and Deloitte management consultant. Experienced
the FX problem personally — moved money between Estonia and UK monthly.
Understands the regulatory environment. Commercial and analytical.
Key test applied: Remove one founder. Without Taavet: no growth engine,
no brand, no investor network. Without Kristo: no regulatory navigation,
no commercial discipline. Genuinely complementary — the pair fails the
removal test correctly.
Team gaps: No dedicated regulatory counsel in-house. No CFO at this size.
Both acceptable for Series A — flag for Series B.
Verification signal: Skype alumni network is confirming Taavet's
operational reputation. Consumer press is covering TransferWise positively.

05 — TRACTION
Stage: Series A. 18 months post-launch.
Metrics provided:
— Transfer volume: £30M/month [Strong for 18 months]
— Active customers: ~80,000 [Strong organic growth]
— Month-on-month growth: 25% [Top decile for B2C fintech]
— NPS: 75+ [Extraordinary. Category-defining signal]
— Customer acquisition: predominantly word of mouth [Zero paid spend]
Missing metrics: Gross profit per transfer (estimated 0.3–0.5%), annual
revenue, cohort retention by corridor.
Public signal verification: Consumer press coverage strongly positive.
Martin Lewis (MoneySavingExpert) has recommended the product unprompted.
Media coverage drives organic acquisition. This is a meaningful signal.
Traction quality: The NPS of 75+ in financial services is the single
most important metric in this submission. This does not happen without
genuine product-market fit. Combined with 25% MoM growth and zero
paid acquisition — this is a retention pattern matching Pattern 1
(Ideal Curve) by proxy.

06 — BUSINESS MODEL
Revenue type: Transaction fee (percentage of transfer amount)
Current fee: ~0.5% + small fixed fee per transfer
Gross margin: Estimated 40–50% (FX spread + payment processing cost)
Moat type: Network effects (Type 1, emerging) + Brand/Trust (Type 5, early)
Burn multiple: Not provided. Estimated high at current growth.
Runway: 12–18 months with this raise.
Business model quality: The fee model is simple and communicable.
Customers understand why they save money — this drives the word of mouth.
The challenge is that the business requires regulatory licences in every
new market, which slows expansion. This is a medium-term cost that becomes
a moat (regulatory licence = Type 4 moat) once the licences are obtained.

07 — VALUATION ASSESSMENT
Asking: $6M for ~17% post-money at $30M pre-money
Implied multiple: ~10x run-rate ARR (estimated $3M ARR)
Market benchmark: Aggressive for 18-month-old B2C fintech — but justified
by NPS and growth rate. Index Ventures has pricing power here.
Precedent: Comparable consumer fintech at this stage in EU: none directly.
Verdict: FAIR. The NPS alone justifies a premium to standard multiples.

Return scenario (base case):
Entry ownership: ~17% → Diluted to ~8.5% at exit (50% dilution)
Base case exit: $2B acquisition or IPO in 5–7 years
Return: $170M → 28x on $6M investment.
Bull case: Global transfer platform, IPO at $8B+ → $680M → 113x.
Fund contribution test at base case: Fund-moving on any reasonable fund size.

08 — EXIT THESIS
Most likely exit: IPO (London or US dual listing) given regulatory
complexity making acquisition difficult
Natural acquirers: Visa, Mastercard, PayPal, major banks (Citi, HSBC)
Why they buy: Customer base, regulatory licences, brand trust
Exit timeline: 5–8 years
Exit achievability: HIGH if geographic expansion executes

09 — RED FLAGS
— Bank partner dependency: HIGH. Processing requires banking relationships.
  If a banking partner terminates (regulatory pressure, competitive threat),
  transfers halt. Mitigation: diversify banking partners. Watch closely.
— Regulatory: MEDIUM. E-money licence in each jurisdiction. Slower
  expansion than pure software. Cost of compliance grows with geography.
— Incumbent response: MEDIUM. Banks cannot match the price but can
  increase brand marketing to confuse customers on fee comparison.
Overall risk level: MEDIUM
Primary risk: Banking partner loss or regulatory action in key corridor
before the company reaches sufficient scale to have leverage.

10 — RECOMMENDATION
STATUS: PRIORITY REVIEW

Investment hypothesis: A complementary founding pair with demonstrated
execution credentials has built the first genuinely customer-beloved
money transfer product. The pricing model exploits a structural conflict
the banks cannot resolve. NPS of 75+ with zero paid acquisition is a
signal that rarely appears at this stage in any category. The regulatory
expansion path is slow but becomes a moat once licences are held.
The business will be worth multiples of this valuation in 5 years if
the growth rate sustains for 18 more months.

Questions for founder meeting:
1. Who are your top 3 banking partners by volume and what is the status
   of each relationship — specifically, have any indicated concerns?
2. What is the expansion priority order and regulatory timeline for
   the next 3 markets?
3. What does CAC look like now vs 6 months ago — is word-of-mouth
   sustainable as you scale?

ALEX'S NOTE:
The score is 79. The score undersells the deal. The NPS of 75+ in
financial services — a category where most companies score 10–20 —
is the most important data point in this submission. When financial
services customers actively refer a product to friends, something
genuinely important is happening. The product is better, measurably
better, and customers can prove it to their peers with a live comparison.
This is a Priority Review without hesitation.

WHAT HAPPENED:
TransferWise (renamed Wise in 2021) went public on the London Stock
Exchange in July 2021 via a direct listing at a £8.75bn ($11.7bn)
valuation — the largest ever tech listing on the LSE at the time.
The $6M Series A investment, diluted through multiple subsequent rounds,
returned approximately 40–60x on invested capital. By 2021, Wise was
processing £5.4B in cross-border transfers monthly and had 10M+ customers.
LESSON: When NPS exceeds 60 in a financial services product at Series A,
the market is telling you something the score cannot capture — move to
Priority Review regardless of revenue scale.

YES DEAL 012 — MOBILE-FIRST CHALLENGER BANK
Reconstructed from: Passion Capital / Monzo Series A review, October 2016
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
COMPANY:         Mobile-first bank for UK consumers. No branches.
STAGE:           Series A
AMOUNT RAISING:  £5M at ~£30M pre-money
STATUS:          PRIORITY REVIEW

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:
A five-person founding team led by a second-time founder (Tom Blomfield,
GoCardless co-founder) has built a mobile banking product with 500,000
people on a waiting list and the fastest-growing banking app in UK history.
No revenue. Banking licence application filed with the FCA but not yet
granted. The product today is a prepaid Mastercard with real-time spending
notifications — a fraction of what a full bank account will be. The
investment thesis is entirely about the team, the waiting list signal,
and the structural shift in how younger UK consumers want to interact with
financial services. The business is pre-revenue and pre-licence. Almost
every quantitative screen says Watch. The qualitative signal says Priority Review.

G7 SCORE: 70/100  |  VALUATION: FAIR
Thesis Fit:       16/20  — Consumer fintech, UK domestic. Strong fit
Market:           17/20  — UK retail banking. Enormous. Uncontested by challengers
Team:             22/25  — Type 3. Second-time success. Strong founding team

01 — THESIS FIT
Fit: STRONG FIT
Consumer fintech at Series A with a founding team with demonstrated
prior execution in the same regulatory environment. No comparable
in current deal history.

02 — MARKET
TAM: UK retail banking current account market. 68M accounts.
     Approximately £14B in annual current account revenue (overdraft fees,
     interchange, interest margin). Average revenue per account: ~£200/year.
SAM: 18–35 demographic most susceptible to switching. ~15M accounts.
SOM: 3% of SAM in 5 years = 450,000 accounts. Achievable at current
     acquisition trajectory.

Market quality: STRONG. UK banking is an oligopoly (Barclays, Lloyds,
HSBC, NatWest, Santander) with low NPS (average 15–20) and minimal
product innovation. The switching rate is historically low — 3% annually —
driven by inertia not satisfaction. Monzo's job is to make switching
frictionless, which a mobile-first bank with instant account opening can do.
Why now: UK Current Account Switch Service (CASS) launched 2013 — made
switching genuinely easy for the first time. Combined with smartphone
penetration at 75%+ for under-35s. The infrastructure for disruption
now exists. It did not 5 years ago.

03 — COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE
Direct: Starling Bank (same year, same regulatory path)
Indirect: Traditional banks (weak mobile), Revolut (launching same period)
Failed attempts: No prior mobile-only UK bank has reached this scale.
                 Metro Bank (branch-based challenger) — different model.
Right to win: Monzo's brand and community is ahead of Starling's.
The waiting list culture — the orange coral card as a social signal —
has given Monzo a community moat its competitors do not have.
Competitive risk: HIGH. Starling, Revolut, and N26 are all in this space.
But the UK market will support 2–3 winners. Market size is sufficient.

04 — TEAM
Founder-market fit: STRONG. Type 3 — Second-Time Founder, Prior Success.
Tom Blomfield: Co-founded GoCardless in 2011 (still operating, Series B+).
Has navigated FCA licensing before. Knows the operational requirements of
building regulated fintech at scale. Left GoCardless to pursue this.
Jonas Huckestein, Jason Bates: Senior engineering and product from
relevant backgrounds.
Paul Rippon and Gary Dolman: Chief Compliance Officer and CFO — both
experienced in regulated financial services. This is not a team learning
compliance. They have done it before.
Key signal: This team raised £1M via crowdfunding in 96 seconds in 2016.
That is a social proof signal about team credibility and consumer demand
that cannot be manufactured.
Team gap: Tom Blomfield is CEO but GoCardless is still operating and
successful — is attention divided? Flag for founder meeting.

05 — TRACTION
Stage: Pre-revenue. Pre-banking licence. 18 months post-founding.
Metrics:
— Waiting list: 500,000 registered [Exceptional for UK fintech]
— Active prepaid cardholders: 50,000+
— Crowdfunding: £1M in 96 seconds (2016)
— App Store rating: 4.9/5 (highest-rated financial app in UK store)
— Social sharing: Coral card photographed and shared organically
Missing metrics: Revenue (none), retention cohorts, licence timeline.
Traction quality: The waiting list of 500,000 is not marketing data.
It is consumer intent data. 500,000 people gave their email address,
waited for a card, and shared the wait with friends. This pattern
matches the organic pre-product demand signal of Dropbox waitlist (2007).
The product has not launched and people are already evangelising it.

06 — BUSINESS MODEL
Revenue type: Not yet established. Planned: interchange (0.2–0.5%),
premium accounts, lending margin, overdraft fees.
Current revenue: Zero (prepaid, costs Monzo money per card issued)
Gross margin: Negative currently. Target: 60%+ at scale with banking licence.
Moat type: Brand/Trust (Type 5, building fast) + Network effect (Type 1,
community-driven referral loop)
Runway: 12–15 months with this raise. Will need Series B before banking

Note: the lack of a current business model is appropriate for this stage

07 — VALUATION ASSESSMENT
Asking: £5M at £30M pre-money
Implied multiple: N/A — pre-revenue
Benchmark: Starling Bank raised at similar valuation same period. Fair.
Verdict: FAIR. This is a team-and-signal bet, not a revenue multiple.

Return scenario (base case):
Entry ownership: ~14% → Diluted to ~7% at exit
Base case exit: £2B acquisition or IPO in 6–8 years (1M+ customers)
Return: £140M → 28x on £5M. Fund-moving.

Exit timeline: 6–10 years (banking licence timelines add delay)
Exit achievability: HIGH if banking licence granted within 12 months

09 — RED FLAGS

— Starling Bank: MEDIUM. Same regulatory path, same consumer proposition.

Overall risk level: HIGH (appropriate for stage and reward)

10 — RECOMMENDATION
STATUS: PRIORITY REVIEW

Investment hypothesis: The only scenario where this is not a Priority
Review is if you believe the FCA will deny the banking licence. If the
licence is granted — which the team's regulatory experience makes likely —
Monzo will be the first mobile-native current account in the UK with
500,000 pre-committed customers. That is an almost-impossible starting
position to replicate. The moat is being built before the product exists.

Questions for founder meeting:
1. What is the FCA timeline — specifically, have they given any informal

ALEX'S NOTE:
The quantitative score is 70. This would normally be Watch. I am

monthly profitability in 2023 and filed for a US banking licence.
LESSON: When a second-time founder with regulatory experience builds a
pre-revenue consumer product with 500,000 organic sign-ups, the waiting
list is the metric — not the revenue.

COMPANY:         Full-stack payments infrastructure for enterprise merchants
STAGE:           Series A (bootstrapped for 7 years prior)
AMOUNT RAISING:  $230M at ~$2B pre-money
STATUS:          PRIORITY REVIEW

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:

external capital, grown to $80M+ ARR at 150%+ year-on-year, reached
profitability, and is now raising its first external round at a $2B

10+ years in the global payments industry who identified a structural gap
between legacy acquirers (Worldpay, Adyen) and the enterprise merchants
who needed higher acceptance rates, global coverage, and direct bank
connections with no middleware. Checkout.com is not competing on price —
it is competing on technical capability and merchant conversion rates. The
customers include Grab, Adidas, BMW, Revolut, and Transferwise. This is a
Priority Review. The only question is whether the valuation is justified.

incumbents with legacy technology (Worldpay, Adyen, Stripe — which serves
smaller merchants). Incumbents have declining acceptance rates as the
complexity of global card processing increases. Every 1% improvement in
acceptance rate for an enterprise merchant processing $1B/year is worth
$10M annually — this is Checkout.com's primary sales lever.
Timing: E-commerce growing at 20%+ annually. Cross-border e-commerce
growing at 30%+. Every incumbent is constrained by legacy middleware

YES DEAL 020 — OPEN-SOURCE INFRASTRUCTURE SOFTWARE
Reconstructed from: GGV Capital / HashiCorp Series A review, December 2014
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
COMPANY:         Open-source infrastructure automation tools with enterprise commercial layer
STAGE:           Series A
AMOUNT RAISING:  $10.2M at ~$40M pre-money
STATUS:          PRIORITY REVIEW

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:
Mitchell Hashimoto, 24 years old, built Vagrant — an open-source tool for
creating reproducible development environments — as a side project at university.
It now has 2M+ downloads and is used by every major technology company. He
co-founded HashiCorp with Armon Dadgar to build a suite of infrastructure
automation tools on top of this community. Terraform (infrastructure as code),
Consul (service networking), and Vault (secrets management) are all in active
development or early release. The community is growing 300%+ year-on-year.
The commercial opportunity is the enterprise licensing of these tools — the
same model as Red Hat (Linux), Elastic (Elasticsearch), and MongoDB.
This is a young prodigy with non-replicable community capital and an open-source
business model that has produced three category-defining companies. Priority Review.

01 — THESIS FIT
Fit: MODERATE-STRONG. Open-source infrastructure tooling with a commercial
enterprise layer. The OSS commercial model is proven. The products are
in categories that will matter for the next decade. Fits infrastructure
software mandate.

02 — MARKET
TAM: DevOps and infrastructure automation software: ~$8B (2014), growing 25%/year.
     Infrastructure as code, secrets management, service networking combined: ~$3B.
SAM: Enterprise companies (>500 employees) adopting cloud-native practices: ~$1.5B.
SOM: 5% of SAM in 5 years = $75M ARR. Conservative given community size.

Market quality: STRONG. Cloud infrastructure adoption is creating a new
category of infrastructure tooling that did not exist before. Physical
servers did not need infrastructure-as-code — you racked them once.
Cloud servers are ephemeral — they must be defined in code, managed with
automation, and secured at scale. This is a new problem. HashiCorp's tools
are the reference implementations for solving it.
Timing: AWS, Azure, and GCP are crossing mainstream enterprise adoption.
The infrastructure automation problem scales with cloud adoption.
Every enterprise that moves to cloud eventually needs Terraform.

03 — COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE
Direct: Puppet, Chef (configuration management — adjacent but older approach),
        Ansible (Red Hat acquisition), SaltStack
Indirect: Cloud-native tools (AWS CloudFormation — AWS specific only)
Right to win: Hashimoto's community credibility is the right to win.
He is not a business founder who built a product for the community —
he is a community member who became a founder. The community trust is
genuine and precedes the company. Puppet and Chef have enterprise sales
teams but are building community. HashiCorp has community and is building
the enterprise sales team. That is the correct direction.
Competitive risk: MEDIUM. Puppet is well-established in enterprise.
Chef is well-funded. But neither has community momentum at HashiCorp's scale.

04 — TEAM
Founder-market fit: STRONG. Type 6 — Young Prodigy.
Mitchell Hashimoto (24): Built Vagrant at university as a side project.
It solved his own problem — reproducible development environments —
and became used by millions of developers globally. He did not research
the market. He built something he needed. That is the canonical Type 6
signal: product instinct unconstrained by conventional assumptions.
Armon Dadgar (24): CS degree, University of Washington. Early HashiCorp
engineer who became CTO. Provides technical depth and operational
support to Hashimoto's product vision.
Key risk — Type 6 archetype: Hashimoto has exceptional product instinct
and zero business management experience. He has never run a P&L, managed
an enterprise sales organisation, or navigated a board. This is the
primary operational risk.
Mitigation: GGV Capital and True Ventures have the operational playbook
for the OSS-to-enterprise transition. Todd Francis (GGV) has done this
with Zendesk and Pandora. The investor brings the management experience.
