Reconstructed from: Kleiner Perkins / Rippling Series A review, February 2019

COMPANY:         Single platform for employee HR and IT management
STAGE:           Series A
AMOUNT RAISING:  $45M at ~$270M pre-money
STATUS:          PRIORITY REVIEW

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:
Parker Conrad was removed as CEO of Zenefits by its board in February
2016 after the company sold insurance in states where it lacked licences,
generating up to $83M in unlicensed commissions and facing regulatory
action across 15 states. Zenefits reached a $4.5B valuation and then
collapsed into a regulated crisis. Three years later, Parker Conrad has
built Rippling — a company that addresses the exact root cause of Zenefits'
failure while doing something Zenefits never did: unifying HR and IT
management on a single employee record. At $10M ARR growing 200%+,
with compliance architecture built into the product from day one, and a
structural differentiation that Zenefits cannot replicate, this is the
clearest example of a Type 4 founder who processed the failure correctly
and returned with a fundamentally stronger product. Priority Review.

02 — MARKET
TAM: SMB and mid-market HR and IT software combined: ~$40B annually.
     HR software alone: ~$25B. IT management (MDM, app provisioning): ~$15B.
     The combination is Rippling's novel positioning.
SAM: Companies 10–500 employees in US: ~$10B.
SOM: 1% of SAM in 5 years = $100M ARR. Achievable at current trajectory.

Market quality: STRONG. The incumbent HR platforms (Workday, ADP) serve
enterprise. The SMB market is served by fragmented point solutions —
Gusto for payroll, Okta for SSO, Jamf for device management, Slack,
Google Workspace. Each new employee requires setup across 8–12 separate
tools. Rippling combines all of them under one employee record. When an
employee leaves, Rippling offboards them from every tool simultaneously.
This is a workflow pain with a clear and measurable ROI per HR action.
Timing: Remote work growth (pre-COVID trend accelerating) increasing the
number of tools per employee. The problem is getting worse, not better.

03 — COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

Indirect: Okta (identity), Jamf/Mosyle (device management)
Right to win: No competitor combines HR and IT management on a single
employee record. Gusto cannot provision an employee's MacBook. Jamf
cannot run payroll. Rippling can do both from one dashboard in minutes.
The architectural insight — that the employee record is the master data
object linking both HR and IT — is non-obvious and difficult to replicate
once customer data is in the system.
Competitive risk: MEDIUM. Rippling's architecture requires significant
engineering investment to replicate. Gusto and Workday are unlikely to
build IT management. Okta is unlikely to build payroll.

Type 4 scoring: Apply +3 upgrade for specific root cause + structural fix

STAGE:           Growth (post-pivot)
AMOUNT RAISING:  €27M at ~€80M pre-money
STATUS:          PRIORITY REVIEW

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:
Vinted was founded in 2008 in Kaunas, Lithuania and came within weeks of
shutting down at least twice before 2015. It had a conventional marketplace
fee model — sellers paid to list items — which suppressed supply growth
enough to kill liquidity on the demand side. In 2016, the company did
something deeply counterintuitive: it eliminated all seller fees entirely
and shifted all transaction revenue to buyers via a "buyer protection fee."
Supply exploded within 90 days. Transaction volume increased 10x within
12 months. The company is now profitable on a per-transaction basis and
growing 200%+ year-on-year. This is not a turnaround story — it is a
marketplace with correct unit economics that found its model after 7 years
of iteration. The submission before us is the post-pivot company.
This is the company we should evaluate. Priority Review.

COMPANY:         Deep learning autonomous driving — full-stack, no HD maps

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:
Amar Shah and Alex Kendall, both Cambridge PhDs in machine learning and
computer vision, have built an end-to-end autonomous driving system that
learns to drive the way humans do — from experience, not from rules.
Every competitor in autonomous driving (Waymo, Cruise, Mobileye) uses
hand-coded rules and HD maps. Wayve uses none. The vehicle's behaviour
is a single learned neural network: camera input → steering output.
If this approach works at scale, it will be 10–100x cheaper to deploy
than the rule-based approach. If it does not work, the company has nothing.
This is a binary deep tech bet with an extraordinary IP moat if it succeeds,
an uncertain timeline, and two technical co-founders who have no commercial
experience. Applying the deep tech modified framework: Priority Review
at a reduced position size reflective of the binary risk.
