SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Climate / Impact):
— Product or service directly reduces, avoids, removes, or measures greenhouse gas
  emissions — or addresses a related environmental or social challenge
— Revenue depends at least partially on carbon pricing, government energy subsidies,
  green procurement mandates, sustainability reporting regulations, or impact investment
— Company explicitly describes its primary mission in environmental or social terms
  alongside (or instead of) purely commercial terms
— Exit pathway includes infrastructure funds, energy majors, or mission-aligned
  investors (alongside or instead of traditional technology acquirers)

THE MOST IMPORTANT DISTINCTION ALEX MUST MAKE FIRST — TYPE A vs TYPE B:

TYPE A — TECHNOLOGY COMPANY IN CLIMATE:
The product is primarily software, data, or a technology platform that happens to
address a climate or sustainability problem. Unit economics are driven by software
economics: high gross margins, low marginal cost, scalable without proportional
capital increase. Appropriate analytical framework: SaaS Library or relevant sector
library alongside this Climate Library.
Examples: Corporate carbon accounting SaaS, climate risk data platform, grid
optimisation software, precision agriculture analytics, EV fleet management software.
Valuation framework: revenue multiple (8–25x ARR depending on growth and NRR).

TYPE B — INFRASTRUCTURE OR PROJECT COMPANY IN CLIMATE:
The product requires physical asset deployment — energy assets, carbon sequestration
land, hardware installations, manufacturing capacity. Unit economics are driven by
asset returns: lower gross margins, high upfront capital, long payback periods,
predictable long-term cash flows once deployed. Appropriate analytical framework:
project finance and infrastructure fund analysis alongside this Climate Library.
Examples: EV charging network operator, solar farm developer, battery storage project,
nature-based carbon credit developer, green hydrogen production facility.
Valuation framework: asset value, EBITDA multiple, or discounted cash flow on the
contracted revenue stream — NOT a revenue multiple.

CRITICAL MISTAKE TO AVOID: A Type B infrastructure company that has been pitched
to investors at a SaaS revenue multiple will experience severe multiple compression
at Series B when institutional infrastructure investors apply the correct framework.
A company raising at 15x revenue on the basis of "climate SaaS" that is actually an
infrastructure deployment business will be valued at 5–8x EBITDA (or 1–3x revenue)
by the infrastructure investor who eventually needs to fund it at scale. Identify this
mismatch at the earliest possible stage.

HOW TO DISTINGUISH TYPE A FROM TYPE B IN A SUBMISSION:
Type A signals: gross margin above 60%, revenue scales without proportional asset
  deployment, customers are enterprises paying a subscription or data fee, company
  does not own the physical asset generating the impact.
Type B signals: gross margin below 40%, revenue scales with physical deployment (more
  panels, more chargers, more land), company deploys or owns physical assets, payback
  period is measured in years not months, blended finance or project debt is in the
  capital structure.
Mixed: many climate companies have both elements. A building energy management SaaS
  (Type A) that also installs hardware under a managed service contract (Type B)
  should be assessed on a disaggregated basis: what are the software economics and what
  are the hardware deployment economics?

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A company selling ESG consulting services is a professional services firm, not a
  climate technology company — it does not belong in this library unless it has a
  proprietary technology or data platform
— A company primarily earning revenue from commodity carbon credit trading without
  originating or verifying the credits is a financial intermediary — apply Fintech
  Library for the trading business; apply this library for any origination or
  measurement technology
— A company whose "climate" positioning is purely marketing — without a measurable
  impact metric and a mechanism that generates it — is not a climate company

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. CLEAN ENERGY TECHNOLOGY
Signals: product generates, stores, distributes, or optimises energy; submission
references energy output (MWh), efficiency improvements, levelised cost of energy
(LCOE), or grid integration; revenue may include power purchase agreements (PPAs),
energy-as-a-service contracts, government Contracts for Difference (CfD), or hardware
sale; competitive set includes incumbent energy companies, grid operators, or clean
energy hardware manufacturers.
Type A examples: Grid optimisation software, virtual power plant platforms, energy
  trading algorithms, battery management software, demand response SaaS.
Type B examples: Battery storage deployment companies, solar farm developers, offshore
  wind project companies, hydrogen production facilities, grid infrastructure operators.
Key metric split: Type A — ARR, NRR, margin. Type B — installed capacity (MW/MWh),
  cost per kWh, LCOE, capacity factor, contracted revenue under PPA or CfD.

2. CARBON MARKETS AND MEASUREMENT
Signals: company generates, verifies, registers, trades, or tracks carbon credits or
carbon emissions; submission references tonnes of CO2 equivalent (tCO2e), carbon
credit price (£/tonne), or corporate net-zero commitment pipelines; key standards
referenced include Verra VCS, Gold Standard, ICVCM Core Carbon Principles, or EU ETS
/ UK ETS compliance credits.
Type A examples: Corporate carbon accounting SaaS, carbon credit registry platforms,
  emissions measurement and reporting tools, MRV (Measurement, Reporting, Verification)
  software.
Type B examples: Nature-based carbon credit developers (forestry, soil carbon, wetland
  restoration), engineered carbon removal projects (DACCS, BECCS), blue carbon projects.
Key risk: voluntary carbon credit quality is a systemic risk — Guardian/Financial Times
  investigations in 2023 challenged the additionality of a significant proportion of
  voluntary credits, triggering corporate buyer disengagement. Any carbon company must
  have independently verified additionality.

3. SUSTAINABLE FOOD AND AGRICULTURE
Signals: product reduces the environmental impact of food production or enables
sustainable agriculture; submission references carbon intensity per kg of food produced,
water usage reduction, land use efficiency, or alternative protein production cost;
competitive set includes agricultural technology companies, food manufacturers, or
upstream food suppliers.
Type A examples: Precision agriculture analytics, food waste reduction platforms, supply
  chain sustainability tracking, crop disease prediction.
Type B examples: Vertical farming facilities, alternative protein manufacturing,
  insect protein production, cultivated meat bioreactors.
Key risk: food production economics are notoriously difficult to improve — vertical
farming companies have consistently struggled to achieve gross margins that justify
the capital intensity relative to conventional agriculture. Apply extreme scrutiny to
any vertical farming deal claiming imminent unit economics parity with conventional methods.

4. CIRCULAR ECONOMY AND WASTE
Signals: company extends product lifecycles, enables material recovery, or reduces
waste generation; submission references materials diverted from landfill (tonnes),
recycling rate improvements, product-as-a-service economics, or circular supply chain
integration; competitive set includes waste management companies, recycling operators,
or materials recovery businesses.
Type A examples: Waste management optimisation software, reverse logistics platforms,
  materials marketplace for recycled inputs, product lifecycle extension SaaS.
Type B examples: Advanced recycling technology plants, chemical recycling facilities,
  take-back and refurbishment operations.
Key risk: circular economy companies face competition from the low cost of virgin
materials — the economics of recycling depend on a spread between the cost of recycled
material and the price of virgin materials, which compresses when oil and commodity
prices are low.

5. GREEN BUILDINGS AND CONSTRUCTION
Signals: product reduces energy consumption in buildings, enables sustainable
construction, or improves the environmental performance of built assets; submission
references energy use intensity (kWh/m²/year), carbon emissions per m² of construction,
heat pump installation rates, or building performance certificates; buyer is typically
a property owner, facilities manager, construction company, or local authority.
Type A examples: Building energy management SaaS, HVAC optimisation platforms, smart
  meter data analytics, retrofit planning software, embodied carbon measurement.
Type B examples: Heat pump installation operations, building retrofit services,
  onsite energy generation and storage assets.
Key risk: the UK building retrofit market is highly policy-dependent — the UK
Boiler Upgrade Scheme, EPCs (Energy Performance Certificates), and MEES (Minimum
Energy Efficiency Standards) for rental properties drive the market. Policy reversal
or underfunding of retrofit grants directly suppresses demand.

6. SUSTAINABLE MOBILITY
Signals: product electrifies transport, optimises logistics for lower emissions, or
enables sustainable mobility infrastructure; submission references CO2 per km, charge
point count or utilisation rate, EV fleet conversion rate, or electric vehicle range
and efficiency; buyer is a fleet operator, local authority, property developer, or
individual EV owner; competitive set includes established charge point operators,
fleet management software vendors, or automotive OEMs.
Type A examples: EV fleet management software, route optimisation for emissions reduction,
  EV charging network management software, logistics sustainability analytics.
Type B examples: EV charging network operators, hydrogen refuelling infrastructure,
  electric bus fleet operators.
Key risk: EV charging infrastructure economics depend on utilisation — a charging
network with below 20% utilisation is losing money on each charger. The UK public
charging infrastructure rollout is years behind government targets, creating both
opportunity and risk for companies dependent on public charging network density.

7. CLIMATE DATA AND ANALYTICS
Signals: company provides data, analytics, or intelligence on climate risk, emissions,
biodiversity, or sustainability performance; buyer is a financial institution, a
corporation with sustainability reporting obligations, a government body, or an
infrastructure owner; revenue is SaaS subscription or data licence; submission
references data sets covered (physical climate risk models, satellite imagery,
corporate emissions databases); competitive set includes Moody's ESG Solutions,
MSCI ESG, Sustainalytics, or specialist climate data providers.
Type A always: Climate data and analytics companies are Type A technology companies.
  Apply SaaS Library benchmarks alongside this Climate Library.
Key risk: regulatory tailwinds (CSRD, TCFD mandatory reporting, TNFD) drive demand
but also create a commoditisation risk — as reporting requirements standardise, the
data required becomes more uniform, and pricing power for niche data providers
is competed away by larger platforms with broader data sets.

8. IMPACT TECH (BROAD)
Signals: company addresses a social or environmental challenge beyond pure climate —
water scarcity, biodiversity loss, financial inclusion, access to education or
healthcare in underserved markets; submission explicitly describes impact metrics
alongside financial metrics; revenue may include blended finance (public capital +
private capital), development finance institution investment, or impact-first contracts.
Type A or B: impact tech companies may be either — water treatment technology with
  recurring subscription revenue (Type A) or water infrastructure deployment (Type B).
Key risk: the impact investor market is smaller and more concentrated than mainstream
  VC — exit options are limited to strategic acquirers with a genuine impact mandate
  or specialist impact PE funds. Ensure the exit pathway is credible before assessing
  the investment thesis.

SECTION B — STAGE-SPECIFIC METRIC BENCHMARKS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Format: STRONG (top quartile) | ACCEPTABLE (median) | CONCERN (flag) | FAIL (disqualifying)

NOTE: Climate benchmarks operate on a dual framework. Every climate company must be
assessed on both FINANCIAL metrics and IMPACT metrics simultaneously. A company with
excellent financial metrics and unverified impact claims is a greenwashing risk.
A company with verified impact but broken unit economics is a charity, not an investment.
Both frameworks must be satisfied for a PRIORITY REVIEW recommendation.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FINANCIAL METRICS — TYPE A (TECHNOLOGY / SOFTWARE):
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Apply the SaaS Library benchmarks (Section B of SaaS Library) for ARR, NRR, gross
margin, CAC payback, and burn multiple. Climate context adjustments:

Gross margin adjustment for climate SaaS:
  Climate data / analytics:    STRONG >75% | ACCEPTABLE 60–75% | CONCERN <60%
  Grid / energy SaaS:          STRONG >70% | ACCEPTABLE 55–70% | CONCERN <55%
  ESG reporting platforms:     STRONG >75% | ACCEPTABLE 65–75% | CONCERN <65%

Government dependency adjustment:
  If more than 30% of ARR is from government contracts (not private sector customers),
  apply a government dependency flag. Government contracts are valuable but represent
  a concentrated revenue risk — single procurement decisions can add or remove large
  ARR chunks in a single quarter.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FINANCIAL METRICS — TYPE B (INFRASTRUCTURE / PROJECT):
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

For Type B companies, replace standard SaaS metrics with:

Installed or contracted capacity:
  Energy storage / generation:
    Seed:     STRONG >5 MW deployed or contracted | ACCEPTABLE 1–5 MW | CONCERN <1 MW
    Series A: STRONG >20 MW deployed or contracted | ACCEPTABLE 5–20 MW | CONCERN <5 MW
    Series B: STRONG >100 MW deployed or contracted | ACCEPTABLE 30–100 MW | CONCERN <30 MW

Revenue per unit of output:
  Solar / wind (contracted):    Revenue per MWh under PPA: STRONG £40–80/MWh (CfD) | CONCERN: no contracted offtake
  Battery storage:              Revenue per MWh cycled: STRONG £50–120/MWh | CONCERN below grid arbitrage threshold
  EV charging (per charger):    Revenue per charger per day: STRONG >£25 | ACCEPTABLE £10–25 | CONCERN <£10

Gross margin (hardware deployment + installation):
  Clean energy hardware:        STRONG >35% | ACCEPTABLE 20–35% | CONCERN 10–20% | FAIL <10%
  EV charging (hardware + ops): STRONG >30% | ACCEPTABLE 15–30% | CONCERN <15%
  Note: Type B gross margins are structurally lower than Type A. The investment
  thesis for Type B is long-term contracted cash flows, not margin expansion.

Levelised Cost of Energy (LCOE) — for energy generation companies:
  STRONG: LCOE below grid parity (below £60/MWh in UK 2025–2026)
  ACCEPTABLE: LCOE approaching grid parity with a credible cost reduction path
  CONCERN: LCOE significantly above grid parity with no scale-driven improvement
  FAIL: LCOE above grid parity with subsidies included in the calculation

Payback period (project-level):
  STRONG <7 years | ACCEPTABLE 7–12 years | CONCERN 12–20 years | FAIL >20 years
  Note: infrastructure investors (Macquarie, Brookfield) will fund 20-year payback
  projects if the cash flow is contracted and the counterparty is creditworthy.
  Venture capital investors will not — the payback period is incompatible with a
  7–10 year fund life. Align the payback period with the investor type.

Subsidy dependency ratio (revenue from subsidies / total revenue):
  STRONG <20% | ACCEPTABLE 20–40% | CONCERN 40–60% | FAIL >60% with a subsidy cliff date
  Note: UK energy companies receiving Contracts for Difference (CfD) have contracted
  government revenue for 15 years — this is acceptable subsidy dependency because it is
  long-term and legally contracted. A company dependent on an annual discretionary
  government grant programme is a FAIL condition.

IMPACT METRICS — ALL CLIMATE COMPANIES:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

CO2 avoided or removed (tonnes per year):
  Define whether the metric is avoided (emissions not produced) or removed
  (emissions taken from atmosphere). These are different and should not be conflated.
  STRONG: independently verified by a recognised standard (Verra, Gold Standard, ICVCM,
    or equivalent for removal projects)
  ACCEPTABLE: third-party verification in progress with a credible verifier named
  CONCERN: self-reported without methodology disclosure
  FAIL: company cannot quantify its CO2 impact at all

Cost per tonne of CO2 avoided or removed:
  Nature-based removal:          STRONG <£20/tonne | ACCEPTABLE £20–50 | CONCERN £50–150 | FAIL >£150
  Engineered removal (DACCS):    STRONG <£150/tonne | ACCEPTABLE £150–400 | CONCERN >£400
    Note: DACCS is currently £300–500/tonne commercially; below £150 would represent
    a major breakthrough. Apply scepticism to any DACCS company claiming sub-£100/tonne
    without peer-reviewed cost verification.
  Technology deployment (avoided): STRONG <£10/tonne | ACCEPTABLE £10–30 | CONCERN >£30

Additionality test (would this emissions reduction have happened anyway?):
  STRONG: additionality verified by independent third party using approved methodology;
    counterfactual baseline documented and peer-reviewed
  ACCEPTABLE: additionality assessment in progress; methodology disclosed
  CONCERN: additionality claimed without methodology; "we would not exist without investment"
    is not an additionality argument
  FAIL: no additionality consideration disclosed

Permanence assessment (how long does the impact last?):
  Most relevant for: nature-based carbon credits, soil carbon, forest protection.
  STRONG: 100-year permanence buffer with insurance or equivalent protection against
    reversal (wildfire, disease, policy change in host country)
  ACCEPTABLE: 25–100 year permanence with documented risk buffer
  CONCERN: permanence below 25 years or no reversal buffer
  FAIL: permanence not addressed for a nature-based or sequestration product

Impact verification standard:
  STRONG: Verra VCS, Gold Standard, ICVCM Core Carbon Principles, or equivalent
    (for carbon markets); GRI, SASB, or TNFD (for broader impact reporting)
  ACCEPTABLE: ISO 14064 or equivalent emissions accounting standard
  CONCERN: proprietary measurement methodology with no external standard alignment
  FAIL: self-reported with no methodology disclosed

CARBON MARKET PRICE BENCHMARKS (April 2026 reference):
  EU ETS (compliance): approximately £55–65/tonne CO2 (subject to market volatility)
  UK ETS (compliance): approximately £35–45/tonne CO2 (subject to market volatility)
  Voluntary high-quality (ICVCM-aligned): £15–35/tonne CO2
  Voluntary standard quality: £3–12/tonne CO2
  Nature-based (REDD+/forest): £5–20/tonne CO2 (quality-dependent)
  Engineered removal (biochar, enhanced weathering): £50–150/tonne CO2
  DACCS (direct air capture): £300–500/tonne CO2 (commercially deployed)
  Note: voluntary carbon market prices fell significantly in 2023 following the
  quality crisis. A business plan assuming £25/tonne voluntary credits should be
  stress-tested at £8/tonne to assess viability under a market dislocation scenario.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. POLICY DEPENDENCY — BUSINESS MODEL FAILS WITHOUT A SPECIFIC SUBSIDY
SIGNAL: The company's financial model is viable only because of a specific government
subsidy, tax credit, or mandate — and that support either has a stated end date, is
subject to annual budget review, or is under active political discussion for reform.
The company has not modelled a scenario where the subsidy is reduced by 50% or removed.
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: A business model that requires a government subsidy to be economically
viable is not a business model — it is a beneficiary of government spending. When that
spending changes — as UK heat pump subsidies, solar feed-in tariffs, and EV purchase
grants all have within the last decade — the business case evaporates. Every climate
company built on a subsidy must demonstrate the point at which it becomes subsidy-
independent. If no such point exists in the financial model, this is a PASS.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "If the [specific subsidy] was reduced by 50% tomorrow, would your
business model remain viable? At what carbon or energy price does your business break
even without any government support? When does your model show subsidy independence?"

02. CARBON CREDIT QUALITY — ADDITIONALITY AND PERMANENCE NOT VERIFIED
SIGNAL: Company is originating, selling, or building a marketplace for voluntary carbon
credits, but the additionality of the credits (would the emissions reduction have
happened without the project?) and the permanence (how long does the sequestration last?)
have not been verified by an independent third party using a recognised standard.
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: The voluntary carbon market was significantly damaged in 2023 when
investigative journalism revealed that a substantial proportion of REDD+ forest credits
had no verified additionality — the forests being protected were not at risk of deforestation
without the credit programme. Corporate buyers who had retired these credits reversed their
net-zero claims, suffered reputational damage, and withdrew from the voluntary market.
A carbon credit without verified additionality is not worth its stated tonne price.
Any investor in a carbon company is exposed to the same credibility risk.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Which third-party verifier has assessed the additionality of your
credits? Which standard are you using (Verra VCS, Gold Standard, ICVCM Core Carbon
Principles)? What is the permanence period and reversal buffer for your project type?"

03. GREENWASHING RISK — CLAIMS NOT SUPPORTED BY VERIFIED DATA
SIGNAL: Company's marketing materials, website, or investor deck make specific
quantified impact claims ("removed 500,000 tonnes of CO2," "reduced customer
emissions by 40%") without disclosing the measurement methodology, the verifier,
or the boundary of what is included in the figure.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Greenwashing is an active enforcement target for the CMA (under DMCCA
2024), the FCA (for financial products), the EU (Corporate Sustainability Reporting
Directive), and the ASA (advertising claims). A company that makes unsubstantiated
impact claims risks: CMA enforcement action (fines up to 10% of global turnover),
reputational damage when claims are challenged, and corporate buyer withdrawal when
due diligence reveals the claims cannot be substantiated.
VERIFICATION: For each quantified impact claim in the submission, ask: "Who measured
this figure? What methodology was used? What is the system boundary — does it include
Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions? Has the figure been independently verified?"

04. HARDWARE GROSS MARGIN BELOW 20% — NO PATH TO IMPROVEMENT
SIGNAL: Hardware-heavy climate company has gross margin below 20% at current
deployment scale. The financial model does not demonstrate how gross margin will
exceed 35% at 10x deployment volume. The primary cost driver is a commodity material
(lithium, silicon, steel, copper) that the company cannot control or substitute.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A gross margin below 20% with no improvement path means the business
cannot generate sufficient profit to fund sales, R&D, and overhead while remaining
solvent. This is not a scale problem — it is a structural economics problem. Apply the
Deep Tech Library's BoM learning curve analysis: is cost declining with volume? If
not, why not, and what changes at 10x volume to change this?
VERIFICATION: Request the BoM cost breakdown. Calculate gross margin at current volume
and project at 10x volume. Ask: "What is the primary cost driver in your hardware?
What is your target gross margin at scale, and what specific manufacturing or design
changes will achieve it?"

05. SINGLE OFFTAKER — ONE BUYER FOR ALL OUTPUT
SIGNAL: The company's revenue is entirely or predominantly from a single corporate
offtake agreement, government contract, or energy purchase agreement. The submission
presents this as evidence of commercial validation without acknowledging the
concentration risk.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Single-offtaker concentration in a climate company is identical to
customer concentration in a SaaS business — except the consequences of loss are more
severe because the contracted revenue stream is often the basis for project financing.
If the offtaker defaults, changes its sustainability policy, or exercises a break clause,
the project finance structure may trigger early repayment obligations simultaneously.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your revenue comes from your largest offtake
agreement? What is the term and break clause structure? If that offtaker cancelled the
agreement today, what would happen to your ability to service your project debt?"

06. SUBSIDY CLIFF — CURRENT ECONOMICS DEPEND ON SUPPORT WITH A KNOWN END DATE
SIGNAL: A specific government subsidy, feed-in tariff, or tax credit that the company
depends on has a stated end date (e.g., UK Boiler Upgrade Scheme annual budget,
Contracts for Difference auction round, US IRA production tax credit sunset). The
company's financial model does not show viability beyond the subsidy end date, and
no concrete plan exists for the transition.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The Subsidy Cliff failure pattern (Section D) kills climate companies
at predictable intervals. Unlike policy dependency (Red Flag 01, which is about
whether the business works without subsidies at all), the Subsidy Cliff is specifically
about a known event horizon. The company is building value that will be partially or
fully destroyed on a specific date.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What subsidies is your business currently receiving? When do each
of those subsidies end or come up for renewal? What is your business plan for the 12
months following each subsidy end date?"

07. TECHNOLOGY NOT PROVEN AT SCALE — TRL TOO LOW FOR CAPITAL DEPLOYMENT
SIGNAL: Company is asking for Series A capital to deploy assets or scale production
of a technology that is at TRL 5–6 — demonstrated in laboratory or pilot conditions
but not yet proven in operational deployment at commercial scale. The capital is
intended for commercial deployment, not further technology development.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Deploying large amounts of capital into unproven technology is venture
risk masquerading as infrastructure risk. Infrastructure investors expect TRL 9 before
deployment capital is invested. Venture investors expect TRL 6–7 as a condition of
Series A. A climate company asking for Series A deployment capital against TRL 5–6
technology is asking investors to accept both technology risk and deployment risk
simultaneously — a combination that rarely ends well.
VERIFICATION: Apply TRL assessment (Deep Tech Library, Section B). Ask: "What is your
TRL? Has the technology been demonstrated in conditions representative of your target
deployment environment? What are the outstanding technical risks at your current TRL?"

08. LONG PAYBACK CREATES INVESTOR-MODEL MISMATCH
SIGNAL: Climate company's unit payback period exceeds 12 years but the company is
raising from venture capital funds with a 7–10 year fund life. The company is structured
as a growth-stage technology business but the underlying asset returns timeline is
incompatible with the investor base being targeted.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: This is the Infrastructure Company Mispositioned as a Tech Company
failure pattern. A venture fund cannot wait 15 years for its investment to generate
returns. A climate company that belongs in an infrastructure fund cannot raise the
necessary capital from VCs because the asset life exceeds the fund life. The misalignment
leads to a permanent underfunding problem — too early for infrastructure funds, wrong
structure for venture funds.
VERIFICATION: Calculate the payback period from the submission data. If it exceeds
10 years, ask: "Have you had conversations with infrastructure funds or development
finance institutions about your capital structure? Have you considered project debt or
blended finance to separate the infrastructure returns from the technology returns?"

09. IMPACT NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED
SIGNAL: Company claims a specific impact outcome (CO2 reduced, water treated,
biodiversity improved) in its submission and investor materials but the measurement
methodology has not been reviewed by any external party. The company self-reports
its impact using its own model.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Unverified impact claims are increasingly a commercial risk, not just
a reputational one. Institutional investors (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds,
development finance institutions) require independently verified impact metrics as a
condition of investment. Corporate buyers of carbon credits require third-party
verification as a condition of purchase. A climate company without verified impact
metrics is structurally excluded from the fastest-growing part of the climate capital
market — institutional money that is now flowing at scale.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Who has independently verified your impact metrics? Which
methodology or standard do they use? When was the last verification completed, and
when is the next scheduled?"

10. NO PATH TO SUBSIDY INDEPENDENCE
SIGNAL: The financial model shows the company depending on government support at every
stage of its development — current subsidies fund operations, future subsidies fund scale.
There is no point in the five-year model at which the company achieves viable unit
economics without government support.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: This is distinct from legitimate government contract revenue (a
SBRI contract or CfD is a contracted revenue stream, not a recurring subsidy). It
describes a company whose product cannot be sold at a price that covers its cost of
production without a third-party subsidy. If the technology has learning curves
(costs decline with scale), there may be a path to subsidy independence — but
the model must show it explicitly, at a specific volume, with specific cost drivers.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "At what deployment volume or unit cost does your technology
achieve viability without government subsidy? What is the specific cost driver that
unlocks subsidy independence, and what is the capital requirement to reach that point?"

11. REGULATORY RISK — CARBON MARKET STILL BEING DEFINED
SIGNAL: Company's business model depends on a carbon market regulation, policy,
or pricing mechanism that is under active government review, consultation, or reform.
Examples: UK ETS price reform, voluntary carbon market integrity framework changes,
CSRD scope changes for SMEs, or biodiversity credit market rules.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: A regulatory regime "under consultation" can change materially.
A company building its revenue model on the assumption that a current or proposed
regulatory framework will remain stable is exposed to the Policy Reversal failure
pattern. Carbon markets in particular have been subject to significant regulatory
evolution — UK ETS replaced EU ETS post-Brexit; voluntary carbon markets are being
restructured by ICVCM and VCMI; CSRD reporting requirements are being phased and may
be adjusted. Any revenue model dependent on current regulatory configuration should
be stress-tested against a scenario where the relevant regulation is revised.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Which specific regulatory frameworks does your revenue model
depend on? Have any of those frameworks been announced for review or consultation?
What is your revenue model if the regulation changes materially?"

12. COMPETITION FROM INCUMBENTS WITH LOWER COST OF CAPITAL
SIGNAL: Company is competing in a market where established energy companies, utilities,
or infrastructure operators have a structural cost-of-capital advantage for the same
assets. A £50M wind project funded by a startup at 15% cost of capital cannot compete
with the same project funded by Ørsted or National Grid at 5% cost of capital.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: In most infrastructure-style climate markets, the cost of capital is
the primary competitive advantage. A startup deploying infrastructure capital at
venture returns expectations is fundamentally uncompetitive against a utility or
infrastructure fund deploying the same assets at lower cost. The startup's sustainable
competitive position must be in the technology or platform layer — not in the
capital deployment of standard infrastructure.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Why can your company deploy this infrastructure more cost-effectively
than an incumbent utility or infrastructure fund? Is your competitive advantage
in the technology, the customer relationships, the operational model, or something
else? Could a utility replicate your model in 18 months if they chose to?"

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE SUBSIDY CLIFF
DESCRIPTION: A clean energy or cleantech company builds a successful business on the
back of a government subsidy programme — the UK Feed-in Tariff for solar, the Boiler
Upgrade Scheme for heat pumps, or EV purchase grants. Unit economics are positive
with the subsidy. The company grows rapidly, raises capital based on subsidy-supported
projections, and scales its team and operations accordingly. The government either
reduces the subsidy, caps its total budget, or closes the programme entirely. The
subsidy reduction may be sudden (the UK solar feed-in tariff was cut 64% in 2011 with
six weeks notice), partial, or announced 12 months in advance. In every case, the
company's revenue model is invalidated. Pipeline customers cancel. The company is
left with a cost base built for subsidy-era growth and a revenue opportunity that has
shrunk materially. The Series A or B investors who funded the growth phase are left
holding a business that needs to fundamentally rebuild its unit economics.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Subsidy dependency ratio above 60%; the company's five-year
model makes no reference to subsidy end dates or reduction scenarios; management
describes the subsidy as "long-term policy" without acknowledging that every subsidy
has a precedent of reform; competitor companies that received the same subsidy have
not yet demonstrated viability at below-subsidy pricing; the company has raised capital
specifically to accelerate growth before an anticipated subsidy end date rather than
to build subsidy-independent economics.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any climate company where the subsidy dependency ratio is above 40%
and the subsidy is not contracted for the full investment horizon (i.e., it is subject
to annual budget review or known end date).

2. THE CARBON CREDIT QUALITY CRISIS
DESCRIPTION: A company generates, sells, or intermediates voluntary carbon credits.
Its business model depends on corporate buyers purchasing and retiring credits to
offset their reported emissions. An investigative report, academic paper, or standard
body review finds that the methodology used to calculate the credits' impact was flawed —
either the additionality was not genuine (the forest was not at real risk of deforestation)
or the permanence was overstated (the sequestered carbon was released by wildfire or
pest). The credits are retrospectively invalidated by the standard body. Corporate
buyers who have publicly claimed to be "net zero" on the basis of those credits face
reputational damage, withdraw from the voluntary market, and demand refunds. The
company's revenue pipeline collapses simultaneously with its reputation. This is not
a hypothetical — it is the pattern of what happened to Verra REDD+ credits in 2023,
removing $1B+ of claimed impact from corporate net-zero accounts.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Credits use a high-discount-rate baseline methodology for
additionality without independent sensitivity analysis; the permanence buffer is self-
assessed rather than independently verified; the host country's regulatory environment
is unstable (increasing risk of policy reversal that exposes the "protected" forest);
the company has never had a sample of its projects independently audited at the field
level; the company's impact claims appear in marketing materials at a higher tonne count
than the total issuances registered with the standard body.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any voluntary carbon credit originator, aggregator, or marketplace where
the credit quality verification process has not been conducted by an independent third
party using ICVCM Core Carbon Principles or equivalent.

3. THE HARDWARE SCALE-UP COST SPIRAL
DESCRIPTION: A climate hardware company (battery storage, advanced recycling, green
hydrogen) demonstrates excellent economics at pilot scale — £0.08/kWh storage cost,
30% gross margin at 10 MWh deployed. It raises a Series A to scale to 100 MWh. At
scale, several cost assumptions prove incorrect: the contract manufacturer's volume
discount is 40% less than modelled; the installation labour cost in target geographies
is higher than expected; the primary raw material (lithium, platinum, rare earth)
price has increased 30% since the financial model was built. The 30% gross margin at
pilot scale does not materialise at commercial scale. The company needs a larger round
than planned to reach the volume where the economics actually improve. But it cannot
raise the larger round because it has not demonstrated the unit economics it promised
at Series A. It is stuck in a capital trap: the economics do not work at current scale,
require more capital to reach the next scale, but investors will not fund the next
scale without evidence that the economics work.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Financial model gross margin at scale has not been validated
against actual pilot production costs; bill of materials cost projection relies on
volume discounts not yet contracted with suppliers; the capital requirement to reach
target-economics scale is more than 3x the current fundraise amount; raw material
prices are modelled at current spot prices rather than stressed at +30%; the founding
team has not managed a manufacturing scale-up before and has not engaged a contract
manufacturer with experience at the target volume.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any climate hardware company raising Series A or B capital to scale
production where gross margin improvement is dependent on volume not yet achieved
and the capital required to reach that volume has not been fully committed.

4. THE POLICY REVERSAL
DESCRIPTION: A company builds its commercial model on a government policy that changes.
The UK government cancelled the Zero Carbon Homes standard in 2015 with no replacement.
The previous government's net-zero heat pump rollout targets were downgraded in 2023.
The EU revised CSRD scope in 2024, reducing the number of companies required to comply.
In each case, companies that had built commercial plans based on the regulatory certainty
of these policies found their addressable market materially smaller than projected.
Policy reversals are not rare in climate — the sector is inherently politically sensitive
because it involves costs to consumers and businesses, and political administrations
change their cost-benefit calculus on climate action in response to electoral pressures.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: The company's TAM calculation assumes a specific regulatory
mandate will remain in force without scenario analysis; the founding team cites
"government commitment" to a net-zero target as evidence that a specific policy
instrument will persist; the business model is only viable at current regulatory
requirements — a regulation rollback to the previous standard would eliminate 50%+
of the addressable market; the company is exposed to a change of government with
different climate priorities in the next 24 months.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any climate company whose primary growth lever is a specific government
regulation or mandate that is subject to democratic revision within the investment horizon.

5. THE IMPACT MEASUREMENT GAP
DESCRIPTION: A climate company raises capital from impact-oriented investors on the
strength of compelling impact metrics — CO2 avoided, hectares of biodiversity protected,
tonnes of waste diverted. As the company scales, it faces requests from institutional
investors, corporate customers, and standard bodies for independently verified impact
data. The company discovers that its proprietary measurement methodology does not
align with industry standards — it has been counting avoided emissions using a baseline
that is more conservative than accepted practice, double-counting credits that have
been partially sold to multiple buyers, or reporting Scope 1 impacts without Scope 3
context that would reduce the net figure. When verification is conducted, the independently
certified impact is 30–60% lower than the company has claimed. Institutional investors
withdraw. Corporate buyers reduce their purchases. The fundraise fails.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Impact metrics are self-reported without external verification;
the measurement methodology is proprietary and not aligned to any published standard;
the company has not sought alignment with Verra, Gold Standard, SBTi, or equivalent;
the founding team resists the cost and complexity of third-party verification because
"our methodology is superior"; the impact figures have been growing faster than the
operational activity that generates them (a mathematical impossibility).
WHEN TO APPLY: Any climate company making specific quantified impact claims that have
not been independently verified and where those claims are material to the investment
thesis.

6. THE INFRASTRUCTURE COMPANY MISPOSITIONED AS A TECH COMPANY
DESCRIPTION: A company deploys clean energy assets or carbon sequestration infrastructure.
It describes itself as a "technology company" because it uses software to manage
its assets. It raises seed and Series A from technology-focused venture capital firms
at 15–20x revenue multiples, arguing that its software layer makes it more like Stripe
than like a power company. At Series B, it approaches the institutional infrastructure
funds and energy majors who need to finance the scale-up. These investors conduct
their own analysis and value the company on EBITDA and asset returns — not revenue
multiples. The Series B valuation implied by infrastructure economics is 40–60% below
the Series A valuation implied by SaaS economics. Existing investors face significant
dilution to accept the infrastructure valuation. The management team, believing in the
tech narrative, refuses to accept the infrastructure valuation. The round fails. The
company runs out of capital before the scale required for infrastructure economics to
materialise.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: The company describes itself as a "technology company" in a
domain where revenue scales with physical asset deployment; gross margin is below 40%
but the company is presenting revenue multiple valuations without an EBITDA framework;
the founding team has not had conversations with infrastructure fund investors to
understand their valuation framework; the company's capital structure contains no
project debt (which is the appropriate instrument for infrastructure asset financing);
existing investors have no infrastructure investment experience.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any company where more than 50% of revenue scales with physical asset
deployment and the company has raised from venture capital at implied revenue multiples
above 8x. Identify the mismatch early and reframe the capital structure before the
Series B.

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. POLICY DEPENDENCY — DOES THE BUSINESS WORK WITHOUT SUBSIDIES?
QUESTION: "If every government subsidy, tax credit, and mandate that currently supports
your business was removed tomorrow, at what point in your five-year plan would you still
be commercially viable? What is the specific cost or price at which your technology
becomes subsidy-independent? How close are you to that point today?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names the specific point of subsidy independence (e.g., "at 50 MWh
deployed, our LCOE is below grid parity without subsidy"); has a financial model showing
the pathway; the timeline to subsidy independence is within the investment horizon.
WEAK ANSWER: "Subsidies accelerate our growth but aren't essential" without a specific
viability model; "the government is committed to net zero so subsidies will continue";
cannot model the subsidy-free scenario.
REVEALS: Whether the company is building a real business or a subsidy arbitrage vehicle
that cannot survive a policy change.

02. CARBON CREDIT ADDITIONALITY AND VERIFICATION METHODOLOGY
QUESTION: "For each project generating carbon credits: what is the additionality
methodology? Who conducted the additionality assessment — a third-party verifier or
an internal team? Which standard body (Verra, Gold Standard, ICVCM) has registered
the credits? What is the permanence period and what is the reversal buffer mechanism?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names the third-party verifier; states the specific standard and
methodology (e.g., "Verra VCS VM0007, verified by SCS Global Services"); permanence
buffer is a standard industry reversal buffer pool; all credits are registered on
the standard body's public registry.
WEAK ANSWER: "Our methodology is proprietary and more accurate than Verra's"; "we're
in the process of getting verified"; cannot name the verifier or the standard.
REVEALS: Whether the credits being sold have any defensible quality basis or whether
they will face the same challenge that destroyed Verra REDD+ credit credibility in 2023.

03. IMPACT MEASUREMENT — WHO VERIFIES THE IMPACT CLAIMS?
QUESTION: "For your headline impact claim [cite specific figure from submission]: what
is the measurement boundary, the methodology, and who has independently verified the
figure? Does it include Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions? Is it avoided or removed emissions?
How frequently is the measurement updated?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific methodology with boundary clearly defined; independent verifier
named with their standard; distinction between avoided and removed explicitly stated;
annual re-verification scheduled.
WEAK ANSWER: "We calculate this ourselves using industry-standard assumptions"; "it's
been reviewed by our auditors"; conflates avoided emissions with removed emissions;
cannot state the measurement boundary.
REVEALS: Whether the company's impact claims will survive due diligence from an
institutional investor or a corporate sustainability director — both of whom will ask
exactly these questions.

04. HARDWARE GROSS MARGIN PATH TO 30%+ AT SCALE
QUESTION: "What is your current gross margin on hardware deployment? What is your target
gross margin at 10x your current deployment volume? What are the specific cost drivers
that will enable that improvement — volume discounts on materials, manufacturing process
improvement, or design changes? And have you seen any margin improvement from your
first deployment to your most recent?"
STRONG ANSWER: Current gross margin with actual data; specific target at scale with
the cost driver identified; evidence of margin improvement in deployment to date
(even small improvement shows the learning curve is working).
WEAK ANSWER: "Margins will improve significantly at scale" without a specific driver;
"our bill of materials will benefit from volume discounts" without contracted prices;
no evidence of margin improvement in pilot deployments.
REVEALS: Whether the unit economics improvement story is a model or a demonstrated reality.

05. SUBSIDY DEPENDENCY RATIO AND CLIFF DATE
QUESTION: "What percentage of your total revenue in the last 12 months came from
government subsidies, grants, or mandated payments rather than direct commercial
customers choosing to pay for your product? Which specific subsidies? And for each
one — when does it end, how much notice would you get if it was reduced, and what is
your plan for the 12 months following that event?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific dependency ratio; each subsidy named with its end date
and renewal mechanism; a scenario model for the largest subsidy ending shows the
business remains viable.
WEAK ANSWER: "Subsidies are a small part of our revenue" without a percentage;
describes CfD revenue as "not really a subsidy" because it is contractually committed
(valid distinction — but should be explained); no plan for subsidy end dates.
REVEALS: Whether the company is managing its policy dependency as a financial risk
or assuming government support will continue indefinitely.

06. OFFTAKER CONCENTRATION
QUESTION: "Who are your current offtakers or buyers for your product, energy output,
or carbon credits? What is the revenue concentration — what percentage comes from
your largest single buyer? What is the contract term and break clause structure for
your top 3 buyers? And if your largest offtaker cancelled their contract today, how
would you replace that revenue?"
STRONG ANSWER: No single offtaker above 30% of revenue; multi-year contracts with
creditworthy counterparties; limited break clauses or step-in protections; active
pipeline of alternative buyers.
WEAK ANSWER: One offtaker accounts for more than 50% of revenue; the contract has
annual break clauses; "the relationship is very strong" as the primary risk mitigation.
REVEALS: Whether the revenue foundation is structurally sound or whether one commercial
decision by one counterparty can destroy the business case.

07. TRL AND PATH TO COMMERCIAL SCALE
QUESTION: "What is your current Technology Readiness Level? Has the technology been
demonstrated in conditions representative of full commercial deployment — not just in
a laboratory or a single pilot? What are the outstanding technical risks between
your current TRL and TRL 9? And what capital is required to resolve each one?"
Apply the TRL framework from the Deep Tech Library (Section B) to assess TRL claims.
STRONG ANSWER: Specific TRL with the evidence that justifies it; outstanding risks
quantified with capital requirements; the capital being raised now is sufficient to
advance at least two TRL steps.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're at commercial readiness" without a formal TRL assessment; pilot
demonstration conflated with operational deployment; "our technology works — we've
proven it in the lab."
REVEALS: Whether the company has been honest about how far it is from commercial scale
and whether the capital plan is sufficient for the technical risk remaining.

08. CORPORATE BUYER PIPELINE AND NET-ZERO COMMITMENT QUALITY
QUESTION: "Walk me through your top 5 corporate buyers or pipeline opportunities.
For each: what is their stated net-zero target? Is it SBTi-verified? What is the
specific emissions source they are trying to address? And have they paid you money —
or is the relationship still at the letter of intent or MoU stage?"
STRONG ANSWER: Named corporate buyers with SBTi-verified targets; at least two have
paid for a product or signed a commercial contract; the emissions source matches the
company's product; buyers are in sectors with regulated reporting obligations (CSRD).
WEAK ANSWER: "We have several large companies very interested"; buyers have general
ESG commitments without SBTi verification; all relationships are at MoU or expression
of interest stage; the buyers' stated interest is for marketing purposes rather than
a specific emissions reduction obligation.
REVEALS: Whether the corporate demand is driven by genuine compliance obligation
(durable) or voluntary reputation management (fragile and often the first budget cut
in a downturn).

09. REGULATORY RISK SCENARIO — CARBON MARKET PRICE FALLS 50%
QUESTION: "If the UK ETS price fell from its current level to £20/tonne, what would
happen to your revenue? If voluntary carbon credit prices fell to £5/tonne, how does
your business model change? And if the CSRD reporting requirement for your target
customer size was suspended, how would that affect your pipeline?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has modelled these specific scenarios; business remains viable (perhaps
less profitable) at the stressed price; demonstrates which buyer segments would still
purchase at lower prices.
WEAK ANSWER: "Carbon markets will continue to rise long-term"; "our customers are
committed to net zero regardless of price"; has not stress-tested the carbon price
scenarios.
REVEALS: Whether the company's commercial model has genuine revenue drivers beyond
policy-induced demand, or whether it is entirely dependent on the current carbon
price level and regulatory configuration.

10. BLENDED FINANCE STRUCTURE
QUESTION: "Have you considered or used blended finance — combining public capital
(government grants, development finance institution concessional loans) with private
equity — to de-risk your infrastructure assets? Do you have project debt or revolving
credit facility for working capital on deployed assets? And how does your capital
structure change as you move from development stage to operational assets?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has engaged with UK Innovate Finance, UKIB (UK Infrastructure Bank),
BEIS Net Zero Innovation Portfolio, or equivalent; understands the role of project
finance for operational assets; has a plan to transition from equity funding to debt
funding as assets become operational.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're funded entirely by equity"; does not understand project finance;
describes blended finance as "too complex for our stage."
REVEALS: Whether the founding team understands climate infrastructure capital markets
well enough to optimise their cost of capital as they scale — and whether they are
leaving significant value on the table by funding infrastructure assets with equity.

11. CO-BENEFITS MEASURED AND VERIFIED
QUESTION: "Beyond CO2, what co-benefits does your product or project generate —
biodiversity, water quality, community employment, food security? How do you measure
these? Have any co-benefits been verified by a third party? Are any of your buyers
or investors paying a premium because of specific co-benefits?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names specific co-benefits with measurement methodology; at least one
co-benefit independently verified; corporate buyers or institutional investors have
placed a premium on specific co-benefits (e.g., TNFD-aligned biodiversity credits
command premium pricing over standard carbon credits).
WEAK ANSWER: "Our project creates local jobs and protects biodiversity" without
quantification; no measurement of co-benefits; no buyer has paid a premium for them.
REVEALS: Whether the company is positioned to capture the growing market for high-
integrity credits that bundle multiple environmental benefits — and whether it understands
the TNFD and biodiversity credit market that is developing alongside the carbon market.

12. EXIT PATHWAY — INFRASTRUCTURE FUND OR STRATEGIC ACQUIRER?
QUESTION: "Have you had conversations with infrastructure funds about your long-term
capital structure? Or with strategic acquirers — energy companies, utilities — about
a potential acquisition? Which type of exit do you believe is most likely? And is
your current investor base (if primarily VC) aligned with that exit profile?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has had early conversations with at least one infrastructure fund or
strategic acquirer; understands the valuation framework of each type of buyer;
current investors are aware of the infrastructure fund exit scenario and have agreed
to the timeline.
WEAK ANSWER: "We'll figure out the exit when we're bigger"; assumes a technology
company IPO as the primary exit; has not had conversations with infrastructure investors
because "we're too early for that."
REVEALS: Whether the company has matched its capital structure to its exit pathway —
a critical question for any climate infrastructure company that is currently funded
by venture capital.

UK EMISSIONS TRADING SCHEME (UK ETS):

The UK ETS launched in January 2021 following Brexit, replacing the UK's participation
in the EU ETS. It covers: power generation, heavy industry, aviation within the UK.
Price trajectory: UK ETS launched at ~£40/tonne; peaked at ~£95/tonne in 2022;
  stabilised at approximately £35–45/tonne in 2025–2026.
Expansion: the UK government has committed to expanding UK ETS coverage to include
  waste, shipping, and potentially buildings and road transport by 2030.
  Each expansion creates new demand for emissions measurement, reduction technology,
  and carbon credit solutions.
Cap trajectory: the UK ETS cap declines annually, creating a structural scarcity
  that should support prices long-term absent policy reversal.
Alex flag: UK ETS price volatility is significant — a company whose unit economics
  require a UK ETS price above £50/tonne should be stress-tested at £25/tonne.
  Any investment predicated on a rising ETS price is speculative.

CARBON BORDER ADJUSTMENT MECHANISM (CBAM):

EU CBAM is in transition phase (2023–2025) and full operation from January 2026.
  It levies a carbon price on imports of cement, steel, aluminium, fertilisers,
  electricity, and hydrogen from countries without equivalent carbon pricing.
UK CBAM: the UK government has committed to implementing its own CBAM from 2027,
  covering similar product categories.
Implications for climate companies:
  — Clean steel, green hydrogen, and low-carbon materials companies gain a competitive
    advantage relative to carbon-intensive imports as CBAM is implemented.
  — Companies providing carbon accounting and emissions verification for supply chains
    benefit from CBAM reporting requirements.
  — Technology companies that help manufacturers reduce embodied carbon qualify as
    CBAM-enabling businesses.
Alex flag: any company whose business case cites CBAM as a tailwind should confirm
  whether the specific product category and geography of its customers are within
  CBAM scope.

CORPORATE SUSTAINABILITY REPORTING DIRECTIVE (CSRD) — EU:

CSRD is the EU's mandatory sustainability reporting regulation, replacing the NFRD.
Phased implementation:
  2025 reporting (FY2024): largest EU companies (>500 employees, public interest entities)
  2026 reporting (FY2025): large EU companies (>250 employees or €40M turnover)
  2027 reporting (FY2026): listed SMEs (with opt-out until 2028)
  UK listed companies: subject to TCFD mandatory reporting (see below) rather than CSRD,
  but UK subsidiaries of EU groups must comply with CSRD at group level.
CSRD scope: double materiality assessment; disclosure against European Sustainability
  Reporting Standards (ESRS); assurance requirement (limited assurance initially,
  reasonable assurance from 2028).
Climate company implication: CSRD creates mandatory demand for:
  — Carbon accounting software (to calculate and report Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions)
  — Climate risk data (physical risk, transition risk assessment)
  — ESG reporting platforms and assurance-ready data management
  CSRD is the single most powerful regulatory demand driver for Type A climate data
  and analytics companies in Europe.
Alex flag: A climate data or reporting company whose primary market is EU corporates
  should have a CSRD compliance product roadmap. A company that is not ESRS-aligned
  will lose enterprise customers to competitors who are.

TCFD MANDATORY REPORTING — UK:

The UK made TCFD-aligned climate disclosures mandatory for:
  — UK-listed companies (premium and standard segment): effective January 2022
  — Large private companies (>500 employees, >£500M turnover): effective April 2022
  — Large LLPs: effective April 2023
TCFD requirements: governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets
  disclosures on climate-related financial risks and opportunities.
Alex flag: UK climate data companies providing physical risk models, transition risk
  assessments, or scenario analysis tools benefit from TCFD mandatory demand. Any
  climate risk company that has not built its product against TCFD disclosure categories
  will not serve the UK mandatory reporting market adequately.

BIODIVERSITY NET GAIN (BNG) — UK:

The Environment Act 2021 mandated Biodiversity Net Gain for most new developments
in England from February 2024. Developers must deliver at least 10% net gain in
biodiversity units, either on-site or by purchasing off-site biodiversity units
or statutory biodiversity credits.
Market creation: BNG creates a new biodiversity credit market in England.
  Off-site units can be sold by landowners who create or restore habitats.
  Biodiversity unit prices: £20,000–£100,000 per biodiversity unit depending on habitat
  type and location.
Climate company implication: companies that can measure, verify, and trade biodiversity
  units (alongside or instead of carbon credits) are well-positioned in a growing
  regulatory-mandated market.
Alex flag: nature-based carbon companies should assess whether their projects generate
  biodiversity co-benefits that can be monetised as BNG units under the UK framework.
  Stacking carbon credits with BNG units on the same project is legally constrained —
  confirm the specific stacking rules before modelling dual revenue.

GREEN TAXONOMY — UK AND EU:

EU Taxonomy for Sustainable Finance: defines which economic activities qualify as
  "environmentally sustainable" for EU investment purposes. Six objectives; four criteria
  (substantial contribution, do no significant harm, minimum social safeguards, technical
  screening criteria).
UK Green Taxonomy: the UK has committed to developing its own taxonomy, aligned with
  but not identical to the EU taxonomy. As of April 2026, the UK taxonomy framework
  is in development — not yet in force.
Relevance for climate companies:
  — EU taxonomy alignment is increasingly required by EU institutional investors for
    investment in green assets. A climate company seeking EU investor capital should
    confirm whether its activities qualify under the taxonomy.
  — SFDR (Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation) Article 8 and 9 fund classifications
    determine which funds can invest in the company — taxonomy alignment affects this.
Alex flag: a climate company raising from EU-domiciled funds should confirm whether
  the specific activities are EU taxonomy-aligned. Non-alignment may reduce the pool
  of eligible investors.

VOLUNTARY CARBON MARKET STANDARDS:

Verra Verified Carbon Standard (VCS): the most widely used standard for voluntary
  carbon credits globally. ~77% of all voluntary credits by volume. Subject to the
  2023 quality crisis for REDD+ credits. Has since updated its methodology requirements.

Gold Standard: founded by WWF; applies more rigorous social and environmental co-benefit
  requirements alongside carbon accounting. Premium to VCS on price (~20–40% premium).

ICVCM Core Carbon Principles: the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market's
  new quality framework for voluntary credits, launched 2023. Designed to restore
  market confidence after the 2023 crisis. Becoming the benchmark for institutional-
  grade voluntary credits.

VCMI Claims Code of Practice: governs what claims a company can make when using
  voluntary credits toward its net-zero commitments. Distinguishes between "credible
  contribution" claims and compliance claims.

Alex flag: any voluntary carbon company that is not pursuing ICVCM label accreditation
  for its credits will face increasing difficulty selling to institutional buyers. The
  ICVCM is becoming the minimum standard for credits used by listed companies making
  public net-zero claims.

CONTRACTS FOR DIFFERENCE (CfD) — UK RENEWABLE ENERGY:

CfDs are long-term contracts (typically 15 years) between renewable energy generators
and the Low Carbon Contracts Company (LCCC, a UK government entity). They guarantee
the generator a "strike price" above the wholesale market price, reducing revenue
uncertainty for new clean energy projects.
Allocation rounds: CfD allocation happens in competitive auctions (AR6 was 2024).
  Strike prices for solar: ~£52/MWh | Offshore wind: ~£73/MWh | Tidal: £>200/MWh.
Benefit for investors: a CfD-contracted project has 15 years of contracted revenue
  from a AAA-rated government counterparty — substantially de-risking the investment.
Alex flag: a clean energy project without a CfD or equivalent long-term offtake is
  exposed to merchant power price risk. Uncontracted energy price risk is a significant
  concern for project finance and infrastructure fund investors. Always confirm whether
  the project has contracted revenue or is selling at spot.

UK BOILER UPGRADE SCHEME AND BUILDING RETROFIT POLICY:

UK Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS): provides grants of £7,500 for heat pump installations.
  Budget: annually allocated; can be exhausted before year-end.
MEES (Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards): landlords cannot let properties below
  EPC Band E (current); proposed tightening to EPC Band C for new tenancies (2028),
  all tenancies by 2030 — subject to political revision.
Alex flag: building retrofit companies dependent on BUS grant demand should model the
  scenario where BUS is not renewed (happened for the previous Green Homes Grant scheme).
  MEES tightening is the more durable demand driver — but it is also subject to political
  revision as demonstrated by the 2023 rollback of heat pump installation targets.

US INFLATION REDUCTION ACT (IRA) — COMPETITIVENESS IMPLICATIONS FOR UK:

The US IRA (signed August 2022) committed ~$370B to clean energy subsidies,
  tax credits, and manufacturing incentives over 10 years.
Impact on UK climate companies:
  — US IRA attracts clean energy manufacturing investment to the US, potentially at
    the expense of UK-based manufacturing scale-up.
  — UK companies with US subsidiaries or US project plans can access IRA credits.
  — UK-only companies face a structural competitive disadvantage vs US peers in
    hardware manufacturing due to the IRA manufacturing tax credits.
  — The EU responded with the Green Deal Industrial Plan — state aid flexibilities.
    The UK has not matched US or EU incentive scale.
Alex flag: any UK clean energy hardware manufacturer should assess whether the US IRA
  creates an incentive to establish US manufacturing operations — and whether the
  lack of an equivalent UK incentive is already disadvantaging the company in attracting
  capital relative to US or EU-headquartered competitors.
