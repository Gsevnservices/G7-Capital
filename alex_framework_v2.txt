ALEX ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK — VERSION 2
FRAMEWORK 2: FOUNDER ARCHETYPE RECOGNITION SYSTEM
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PURPOSE: Alex identifies the founder archetype from signals in the deal
submission and applies the corresponding risk framework. This replaces
generic "strong/weak team" assessments with specific pattern recognition.

RULE ZERO: Identify the archetype before scoring the team. The archetype
determines which failure modes to interrogate, not whether the team is
generically impressive. Impressive credentials can still be the wrong
archetype for the stage and problem.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION A — THE 8 FOUNDER ARCHETYPES
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 1 — THE DOMAIN EXPERT FOUNDER

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Prior title is senior operator in the target industry (VP, Director,
  Head of, or C-suite). Not consultant to the industry — operator in it.
— Problem is described from the inside: uses institutional language,
  references internal processes, knows the buyer's budget cycle.
— Left a named organisation within the last 1–4 years to start this.
— First customers are former colleagues or direct professional network.
— Deck contains operational specificity that outsiders cannot fake.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: ABOVE AVERAGE.
Buyer credibility and problem knowledge eliminate the two most common
early-stage failure modes: wrong problem and wrong buyer.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Builds the product they wished they had at their
old company — optimised for the enterprise buyer they know personally,
not for the scalable product the broader market needs. Distribution
is personal network until the network runs out.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: Underestimates startup pace. Retains corporate
decision-making habits (consensus-seeking, committee approval, slow
iteration). Misses the 12-month window to find product-market fit
before the first cohort churns.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Gets first meetings nobody else can. Shortens the
sales cycle by 40–60% versus a founder without domain credibility.
Understands what "done" looks like from the buyer's perspective.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 2 — THE TECHNICAL CO-FOUNDER ALONE

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Solo founder with engineering, science, or research background.
— Product described with technical precision; architecture or
  methodology detailed in the deck.
— First customers are from the technical community, OSS users,
  or came inbound through product quality.
— No mention of commercial co-founder, VP Sales, or GTM hire.
— Revenue exists but growth is flat or spiky — not compounding.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: BELOW AVERAGE.
Not a capability failure — a distribution failure. Technical founders
alone rarely build the commercial motion before the runway runs out.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Product depth compounds while distribution
stagnates. Arrives at Series A with excellent product, insufficient
pipeline, and no commercial operator to present alongside the founder.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: First commercial hire is too junior, hired too
late, and given insufficient runway to prove results. The hire fails.
The founder concludes commercial hires don't work and doubles down on
product-led motion that cannot scale beyond a narrow community.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Builds the highest-quality product in the category.
When paired with distribution — through a co-founder, a key hire, or
a channel — wins on product merit decisively.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 3 — THE SECOND-TIME FOUNDER (PRIOR SUCCESS)

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Prior company named and exit stated or inferable (acquisition,
  IPO, or stated "sold").
— Deck references lessons from prior company directly.
— First-call investor interest disproportionate to current traction —
  suggests strong network pull.
— Existing investors include names from prior company's cap table.
— Team includes people who worked at or with the founder before.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: ABOVE AVERAGE.
Pattern recognition, capital access, and operator network compress
the time from zero to first commercial momentum significantly.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Applies the first company's exact playbook to
the second, even when the market structure is different. Prior success
creates confirmation bias — assumes the GTM motion that worked before
works here. It often does not.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: Overconfidence in network as a distribution
channel. First 10 customers come from direct network. Stops there.
Assumes network breadth will scale. It does not — structured sales
motion is still required and is built late.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Knows what a scaling organisation feels like from
the inside. Hires ahead of the curve. Builds management layer before
it is urgently needed. Does not learn the same expensive lessons twice.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 4 — THE SECOND-TIME FOUNDER (PRIOR FAILURE)

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Prior company mentioned without an exit. Timeline gap of 1–3 years
  between the prior company's apparent wind-down and this one.
— Founder explicitly references what the prior company taught them.
— Current company is solving a problem directly adjacent to the
  prior failure — evidence of learning applied.
— OR: prior company is unmentioned despite an obvious gap in the
  timeline. The omission is itself a signal requiring investigation.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: AVERAGE (bimodal — the distribution is wide).
Quality of the failure analysis determines everything. A founder who
can articulate precisely why their first company failed and demonstrate
they have structurally addressed the root cause is a stronger bet than
most first-time founders. A founder who cannot is repeating the same
mistake with new investors.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: No root cause analysis performed. The first
company failed due to a structural issue (wrong market, wrong unit
economics, wrong co-founder) that the founder has attributed to bad
luck, bad timing, or external factors. The same structural issue
is present in the current company.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: Overcorrection. First company moved too fast,
so this one validates endlessly. First company under-charged, so this
one prices too high for the market. The corrective behaviour becomes
the new failure mode.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Has felt what product failure feels like. Does
not romanticise the process. Makes hard calls faster than first-time
founders because they recognise the warning signs early.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 5 — THE VISIONARY WITHOUT AN OPERATOR

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Background is consulting, investment banking, VC, or advisory —
  not operational.
— TAM is very large and presented early. Narrative is polished.
— Deck design is exceptional; product screenshots are vague or absent.
— Impressive advisory board listed prominently, often before the
  product section.
— Revenue is pre-product or very early with no clear repeatability.
— Team section shows no operator with P&L or product execution history.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: BELOW AVERAGE.
Vision is not the scarce resource. Execution is. This archetype raises
well on vision and spends the funding proving the vision is harder to
execute than anticipated.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Raises Seed on narrative, builds for 18 months,
discovers the product does not match the vision, pivots, raises bridge,
pivots again. Never reaches repeatability before capital runs out.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: Hires VP Sales before product-market fit.
Burns £400K–£800K on a sales team selling a product that is not ready.
Blames the sales hire when the pipeline does not convert.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Fundraising and network. Gets meetings and term
sheets that technically superior founders cannot. Can attract advisors,
partners, and early enterprise pilots through narrative alone.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 6 — THE YOUNG PRODIGY

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Graduation year or age indicates founder is under 27.
— Limited professional work history; went from education to founding
  directly, or after 1–2 years.
— Product often demonstrates exceptional technical depth or product
  instinct disproportionate to age.
— Early traction is often viral or organic — product found an audience
  before the founder understood why.
— Deck may be unsophisticated in business framing despite impressive
  product or user metrics.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: AVERAGE (high-variance bimodal).
Top decile outcomes are disproportionately produced by this archetype.
Median outcome is failure on business basics that experience prevents.
The key variable is self-awareness and willingness to hire what they lack.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Does not know what they do not know. Legal, HR,
finance, customer success, and scaling processes are all learned through
expensive crises rather than deliberate preparation. Each gap becomes
a fire rather than a decision.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: Excessive early dilution due to inexperience
with fundraising mechanics. Arrives at Series A with 30–40% founder
ownership after seed instruments, leaving insufficient equity to
motivate the full company-building journey.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Unbounded product intuition unconstrained by
conventional thinking about how a product in this category should
work. Builds things experienced founders assumed could not be built.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 7 — THE SERIAL ENTREPRENEUR (PATTERN OF EARLY EXITS)

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Two or more prior companies, each with a stated or inferable exit
  below £15M (or equivalent).
— Short tenure at acquiring companies — typically departed within
  12–24 months post-acquisition.
— Current company is early-stage again despite founder's experience.
— Growth trajectory shows strong early execution but investors should
  examine prior company revenue ceilings.
— Comfort with risk and process: deck is operationally credible.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: AVERAGE.
Strong at zero-to-one. Pattern of early exits suggests either a
preference for early-stage work or a systematic ceiling at scale.
Distinguishing between the two requires archetype verification.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Optimises for an early exit rather than for
scale. Starts conversations with acquirers at £1M–£3M ARR. Accepts
a £10M acquisition that would have been a £100M outcome if scaled.
The company is sold when it should be grown.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: Has never built the management layer required
for Series B scale. Does not hire a COO, CFO, or VP Engineering at
the right time because prior companies never needed them. Each
function that breaks at scale comes as a surprise.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Fastest zero-to-first-revenue execution of any
archetype. Knows exactly what to do in the first 18 months. Does not
waste time or capital on unvalidated assumptions.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ARCHETYPE 8 — THE COMPLEMENTARY CO-FOUNDER PAIR

IDENTIFICATION SIGNALS:
— Two founders with verifiably different professional backgrounds —
  typically technical + commercial, product + operations, or
  domain expert + technical builder.
— Deck clearly delineates who leads what: one runs customers,
  one runs product. Responsibilities are not overlapping.
— How they met is often stated: prior company, university, shared
  industry experience. Relationship pre-dates the company.
— Neither founder's background alone would be sufficient for
  this specific problem.

SUCCESS PROBABILITY: ABOVE AVERAGE when genuinely complementary.
AVERAGE when one founder is dominant and the other is a nominal
co-founder added for completeness.
KEY TEST: remove one founder from the founding team — does the
company become impossible to build? If yes, genuinely complementary.
If no, the "pair" is actually one founder with a co-founder title.

PRIMARY FAILURE MODE: Co-founder relationship fractures under scaling
pressure. Differing risk appetite at Series A (one wants to raise more,
one wants to be cash-flow positive). Equity split becomes contentious
when one founder's role scales and the other's does not. Departure of
one founder at a critical moment creates disproportionate damage.

SECONDARY FAILURE MODE: The complementarity that made them strong
at Seed is not the complementarity needed at Series B. The company
needed CTO + Sales at zero. At scale, it needs CPO + COO. The founders
do not recognise they need to hire around their new gaps.

SIGNATURE STRENGTH: Full-stack founding team at the start. Covers
more ground per week than any solo archetype. Decision quality is
higher because one founder stress-tests the other's blind spots.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION B — ARCHETYPE IDENTIFICATION RULES
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 1 — COUNT FOUNDERS:
One founder → evaluate for Type 1, 2, 5, or 6.
Two founders → evaluate for Type 3, 4, 7, or 8. Also test for Type 1
  if both have domain background.
Three or more → apply Type 8 framework. Flag co-founder concentration
  risk: three-way equity splits are a known conflict accelerant.

STEP 2 — IDENTIFY DOMINANT BACKGROUND SIGNAL:
Deep industry operator background → Type 1.
Technical background, no commercial co-founder → Type 2.
Prior company with stated exit → Type 3.
Prior company without stated exit, or timeline gap → Type 4.
Consulting / finance / investor background, no operations history → Type 5.
Age under 27 or graduation year within 3 years → Type 6.
Two or more prior companies → Type 7.
Two founders with clearly delineated different skill sets → Type 8.

STEP 3 — AMBIGUITY RESOLUTION:
When signals point to two archetypes simultaneously:
— Apply the lower success probability type for scoring purposes
  (conservative bias).
— State both archetypes in the output.
— Flag the ambiguity explicitly: "Signals consistent with both
  Type [X] and Type [Y]. Applying Type [lower] for team score.
  Verification questions for both archetypes recommended."
— Recommend verification questions for both types in the output.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION C — TEAM SCORE MODIFIERS BY ARCHETYPE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Team dimension is scored out of 25. Apply base modifier before
evaluating specific team evidence.

TYPE 1 — DOMAIN EXPERT:
Base modifier: +3.
Upgrade to +5 if: first 3+ customers are named enterprise accounts
  acquired without paid marketing.
Downgrade to +1 if: no evidence of product iteration speed or startup
  operational rhythm — signals corporate decision-making retained.
Hard ceiling: none. Can score 25/25 if commercial traction confirmed.

TYPE 2 — TECHNICAL CO-FOUNDER ALONE:
Base modifier: −2.
Upgrade to 0 if: a credible commercial hire (VP-level or above) is
  confirmed and has been in post for >6 months.
Downgrade to −4 if: solo founder AND pre-revenue AND ACV >£15K
  (enterprise sales without commercial talent is near-impossible to execute).
Hard ceiling: 18/25 until commercial co-founder or C-suite commercial
  hire confirmed and has closed at least 3 customers independently.

TYPE 3 — SECOND-TIME (SUCCESS):
Base modifier: +4.
Upgrade to +6 if: prior exit was >£30M AND founder is deploying the
  same operator network in the current company.
Downgrade to +2 if: prior company was in a materially different
  industry with no transferable distribution or customer relationships.
Hard ceiling: none.

TYPE 4 — SECOND-TIME (FAILURE):
Base modifier: 0.
Upgrade to +3 if: founder articulates specific root cause of prior
  failure AND current company structurally addresses that root cause.
Downgrade to −3 if: prior failure is unacknowledged, attributed solely
  to external factors, or the current company has the same structural
  problem (same CAC problem, same unit economics, same GTM motion).
Hard ceiling: 20/25 until root cause analysis is clearly articulated
  and structural fix is demonstrated in current company metrics.

TYPE 5 — VISIONARY WITHOUT OPERATOR:
Base modifier: −4.
Upgrade to −2 if: an operational co-founder or COO-equivalent with
  P&L ownership history has joined the team.
Upgrade to 0 if: operational co-founder is confirmed AND product is
  in market with paying customers showing repeatability.
Downgrade to −6 if: advisory board is listed prominently in the deck
  but operational team is sparse — signals narrative over execution.
Hard ceiling: 15/25 until VP-level operator with execution history
  confirmed on team. This ceiling applies regardless of founder quality.

TYPE 6 — YOUNG PRODIGY:
Base modifier: −1.
Upgrade to +2 if: experienced operator (5+ years P&L history) has
  joined as co-founder or first executive hire.
Downgrade to −3 if: company is at Series A stage or beyond and still
  has no experienced operator on the team.
Hard ceiling: 17/25 until at least one team member with scaling
  experience (has taken a company from £1M to £10M+ ARR) is confirmed.

TYPE 7 — SERIAL ENTREPRENEUR (EARLY EXITS):
Base modifier: +1.
Upgrade to +3 if: founder explicitly states intention to build to
  scale AND prior company was sold due to external circumstances
  (acqui-hire, market shift) rather than founder preference.
Downgrade to −1 if: founder is already in conversation with potential
  acquirers at early stage, or deck language emphasises strategic fit
  over building an enduring business.
Hard ceiling: 19/25 until founder demonstrates a specific and credible
  reason this company will be held beyond 36 months. This reason must be
  structural (e.g. the business model requires 5+ years to reach
  exit-ready scale) not aspirational (e.g. 'we want to build something
  lasting').
FLAG IN OUTPUT: 'Serial early exit pattern identified — 2+ prior
  companies sold within 36 months. Team score is 19/25 maximum until
  long-term build intent is verified through a specific structural reason.'

TYPE 8 — COMPLEMENTARY CO-FOUNDER PAIR:
Base modifier: +3.
Upgrade to +5 if: both founders have 5+ years of verifiable experience
  in their respective disciplines AND have worked together previously.
Downgrade to 0 if: one founder has significantly less experience than
  the other and their role in the company is not clearly operational
  (suspected nominal co-founder).
Hard ceiling: none when genuinely complementary.

SECTION D — VERIFICATION QUESTIONS LIBRARY
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 1 — DOMAIN EXPERT:

Q1: "Walk me through the last three deals you lost and why."
STRONG: Names specific companies, quotes specific objections, identifies
  the pattern across losses and what product or positioning changes
  would address it.
WEAK: Vague ("the timing wasn't right"), blames external factors,
  cannot recall loss details.
REVEALS: Whether domain expertise extends to commercial pattern
  recognition, or is limited to product knowledge.

Q2: "What do you know about this problem that your first enterprise
  customers could not have told you?"
STRONG: Names specific operational insight from prior role that is
  embedded in the product — non-obvious to outsiders.
WEAK: Recites customer feedback. Cannot distinguish personal insight
  from validated customer discovery.
REVEALS: Whether the domain expertise is genuinely proprietary or
  is publicly available knowledge that any good researcher could acquire.

Q3: "Who at [named competitor] did you interview before building,
  and what did they tell you was wrong with your thesis?"
STRONG: Has spoken to at least one competitive operator. Can
  articulate the strongest version of the counter-argument.
WEAK: Has not sought out opposing views. Competitor knowledge is
  from public sources only.
REVEALS: Whether the domain expert has stress-tested their thesis
  or is operating on conviction without challenge.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 2 — TECHNICAL CO-FOUNDER ALONE:

Q1: "Describe your last three sales conversations — who you spoke to,
  what they said, and what happened next."
STRONG: Can describe conversations in commercial terms (budget owner,
  procurement process, competing priorities). Has clear next steps
  with each.
WEAK: Describes product conversations, not sales conversations.
  Cannot name the budget owner. "They seemed interested" with no
  defined next step.
REVEALS: Whether the technical founder has developed commercial
  instinct or is still in product-conversation mode.

Q2: "What commercial role would make you 3x more effective, and
  why haven't you hired it yet?"
STRONG: Has a clear, specific answer. Has already screened candidates.
  Has a defined bar for what good looks like.
WEAK: Doesn't have a clear answer. Or: believes they can do it
  themselves until revenue reaches a threshold.
REVEALS: Self-awareness about the commercial gap and urgency of address.

Q3: "What is your CAC today and how did you calculate it?"
STRONG: Knows the number. Can decompose it. Has a thesis for how
  it changes with scale.
WEAK: Does not know the number, or conflates it with cost of
  marketing spend without allocating time.
REVEALS: Whether the founder is tracking the commercial metrics
  that will determine the company's survival.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 3 — SECOND-TIME (SUCCESS):

Q1: "What is the single thing you did in your first company that
  you are deliberately not doing in this one?"
STRONG: Specific behavioural change with a clear reason. Names
  a decision or pattern from the prior company and shows how
  they have structurally changed the approach.
WEAK: Generic ("moved faster," "hired better"). Cannot name
  a specific behaviour.
REVEALS: Whether the prior success has produced genuine pattern
  recognition or is being used as a credential without reflection.

Q2: "Which of your prior investors is not investing in this round
  and why?"
STRONG: Names the investor, explains the strategic reason (focus
  area, fund lifecycle, portfolio conflict), and demonstrates
  it is not a relationship breakdown.
WEAK: Deflects, cannot explain the absence, or suggests all prior
  investors are in (which is implausible and warrants verification).
REVEALS: The real state of the relationship with prior backers —
  a strong signal of whether the prior success was a team effort
  or a market tailwind.

Q3: "Where does this company need to get to before you consider
  an exit, and what does that look like?"
STRONG: Specific financial threshold or market position milestone.
  Demonstrates intention to build for scale.
WEAK: Vague ("when it's the right time"), or references a specific
  acquirer too early. Signals the prior exit playbook is already
  being applied.
REVEALS: Whether the founder is building to scale or building
  to sell.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 4 — SECOND-TIME (FAILURE):

Q1: "What specifically caused your last company to fail, and
  what would you do differently on day one?"
STRONG: Names the root cause with precision (wrong ICP, unit
  economics that never worked, co-founder misalignment). States
  a specific structural change already implemented in this company.
WEAK: "The market wasn't ready." "We ran out of capital." "The
  timing was wrong." External attribution with no internal analysis.
REVEALS: Whether the failure has been processed as a learning or
  rationalised as bad luck.

Q2: "Show me a decision you made in this company based directly
  on what you learned from the last one."
STRONG: Names a specific decision — a pricing choice, a hiring
  decision, a market selection — with a direct line to the prior
  failure lesson.
WEAK: Cannot name a specific decision. Or names a process change
  (held more team meetings) rather than a structural change.
REVEALS: Whether the lessons from the first company are embedded
  in the current one or are talking points.

Q3: "What is the failure mode of this company and how far are
  you from it?"
STRONG: Precise answer. Names the single biggest risk, quantifies
  it, and explains the specific mitigation.
WEAK: Generic answer ("if we don't grow fast enough"). Cannot
  quantify proximity to failure.
REVEALS: Whether the prior failure has produced genuine risk
  awareness or whether risk is being managed by optimism.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 5 — VISIONARY WITHOUT OPERATOR:

Q1: "Who on your team is responsible for hitting the revenue number
  this quarter, and what are they doing this week to do it?"
STRONG: Names a specific person with a specific activity. Can quote
  the pipeline number and the conversion rate needed.
WEAK: The founder is responsible. Or: describes a strategy rather
  than a weekly activity.
REVEALS: Whether execution infrastructure exists or whether the
  company is running on vision and relationships.

Q2: "Walk me through how you closed your last three customers —
  who you spoke to, the objections, and the close."
STRONG: Specific commercial narrative. Names decision-makers,
  budget process, specific objections and how they were handled.
WEAK: "They came through our network." Lacks specificity. Cannot
  describe the objections or close mechanics.
REVEALS: Whether customer acquisition is repeatable or is
  entirely dependent on the founder's personal network.

Q3: "What does your product do today that it did not do six
  months ago, and who specifically asked for each change?"
STRONG: Names specific features, the customers who requested
  them, and the commercial outcome of each.
WEAK: Names features without customer attribution. Or: product
  has not changed in six months.
REVEALS: Whether the product is being driven by customer insight
  or by the founder's vision, which is the primary execution risk.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 6 — YOUNG PRODIGY:

Q1: "Walk me through how you manage a team member who is not
  performing. Give me a real example."
STRONG: Has done it. Describes the conversation, the timeline,
  and the resolution — including a dismissal if warranted.
WEAK: Theoretical answer. Has not yet managed a performance issue.
Reveals: Whether the founder has begun building management
  skills or is still in individual-contributor mode.

Q2: "What does your cap table look like today and how much equity
  do you and your co-founders own?"
STRONG: Knows the number. Has thought about dilution path to
  exit. Has structured the cap table deliberately.
WEAK: Does not know their own ownership percentage precisely.
  Has issued options without a clear vesting structure.
REVEALS: Financial literacy and whether the founder has been
  advised properly on structure.

Q3: "What is the most important hire you have made in the last
  12 months and what specifically made them the right person?"
STRONG: Names the hire, articulates the bar, and describes
  what "right" looks like in terms of output rather than credentials.
WEAK: Names someone impressive by credential without being able
  to articulate what they have built since joining.
REVEALS: Whether the founder is learning to hire for output or
  is still hiring for CV quality.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 7 — SERIAL ENTREPRENEUR (EARLY EXITS):

Q1: "In each of your prior companies, at what point did you first
  have a conversation with a potential acquirer and who initiated it?"
STRONG: Can state clearly that the acquirer approached them and that
  they declined multiple times before agreeing, with a clear rationale
  for the timing of the exit.
WEAK: Initiated contact early. Or: cannot articulate why they sold
  at the size they did rather than scaling further.
REVEALS: Whether exit timing was a choice or a preference — the
  most important indicator of whether this company will be sold early.

Q2: "What would the company look like at £50M ARR and who on the
  team would be different from today?"
STRONG: Specific answer. Names the roles that need to change, the
  management layer that does not exist yet, and has thought about
  the hiring sequence.
WEAK: Does not have a clear answer. Or: believes the current team
  can scale to that point without significant additions.
REVEALS: Whether the founder has thought about scale or is
  focused only on the immediate horizon.

Q3: "What is the thing about this company that you could not have
  built in your first two companies?"
STRONG: Names a specific technical, market, or timing advantage
  that is genuinely new.
WEAK: Describes incremental improvements on a familiar pattern.
Reveals: Whether this company represents a step-change in
  ambition or is a refinement of the same playbook.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 8 — COMPLEMENTARY CO-FOUNDER PAIR:

Q1: "Describe the last significant decision you disagreed on and
  how you resolved it."
STRONG: Names a specific decision, articulates both positions, and
  describes a resolution process that was not one-sided.
WEAK: Cannot remember a significant disagreement. Or: one founder
  answers for both.
REVEALS: Whether the co-founder relationship has genuine constructive
  tension or whether one is the decision-maker and the other is nominal.

Q2: "What does each of you own exclusively and what requires joint
  sign-off?"
STRONG: Clear delineation. Each founder has domains of clear
  ownership. Joint sign-off is reserved for a specific short list.
WEAK: Everything is joint. Or: the split is described in vague terms
  ("we work it out together").
REVEALS: Whether the partnership has operational structure or
  is running on good chemistry that will fracture under stress.

Q3: "What happens to the company if one of you leaves?"
STRONG: Has thought about it. Has vesting schedules in place.
  Can describe how the remaining founder continues and what
  they would change in the first 90 days.
WEAK: Has not thought about it. No vesting structure in place.
  Or: answer is "that would not happen."
REVEALS: Whether the pair has planned for the most common co-founder
  risk. The answer to this question is highly predictive of
  co-founder durability.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION E — ARCHETYPE INTERACTION WITH STAGE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PRE-SEED AND SEED (£0–£2M ARR):
Strongest archetypes: Type 1 (Domain Expert), Type 6 (Young Prodigy),
  Type 4 (Second-Time Failure — if failure is processed), Type 2
  (Technical — if problem is deeply technical).
Why: At this stage, the primary challenge is identifying a real problem
  and building a product that solves it. Domain knowledge and
  technical depth matter more than commercial and scaling experience.
  The cost of commercial gaps is low — the founder can still do sales.

SERIES A (£1M–£5M ARR, scaling GTM):
Strongest archetypes: Type 1 (Domain Expert with traction), Type 3
  (Second-Time Success), Type 8 (Complementary Pair).
Why: Series A requires building a repeatable commercial motion. Domain
  experts with traction have proven distribution. Second-time founders
  know how to build a team and a process. Complementary pairs cover
  the full product-to-revenue stack required at this stage.
Weakest archetypes at Series A: Type 2 (Technical Alone — GTM gap
  becomes critical), Type 5 (Visionary — execution gap is now visible
  in the metrics).

SERIES B AND BEYOND (>£5M ARR, building an organisation):
Strongest archetypes: Type 3 (Second-Time Success), Type 8
  (Complementary Pair — if they have built the management layer),
  Type 7 (Serial Entrepreneur — only if they have demonstrated
  intent to scale this time).
Why: At this stage the founder's primary job shifts from doing to
  building an organisation that does. Experience of what a scaling
  organisation feels like is the scarce resource.
Weakest archetypes at Series B: Type 6 (Young Prodigy — unless
  exceptional experienced operator joined at Series A level),
  Type 4 (Second-Time Failure — hard ceiling applies).

STAGE-AGNOSTIC:
Type 8 (Complementary Pair) performs above average at every stage
when genuinely complementary — the full-stack nature adapts to
whatever the company most needs at each stage.

SECTION F — RED FLAGS BY ARCHETYPE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 1 — DOMAIN EXPERT:

RED FLAG: All named customers are from the founder's direct prior
  employer or immediate professional circle.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Distribution is personal network, not repeatable
  sales motion. Will hit a ceiling when the network is exhausted.
VERIFICATION: Ask for the last 3 customers acquired and how the
  conversation was initiated.

RED FLAG: Product roadmap is described in terms of features that the
  founder's former employer asked for, not features driven by
  current customer discovery.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Building for one buyer archetype rather than a
  scalable product. First primary failure mode is active.
VERIFICATION: Ask which features have been requested by customers
  who have no prior relationship with the founder.

RED FLAG: Founder uses phrases like "everyone in the industry knows
  this problem" without being able to name 10 companies actively
  budgeting to solve it.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Confusing familiarity with demand. Known problem
  does not equal funded problem.
VERIFICATION: Ask for the last 3 budget conversations and whether
  the prospect had allocated spend before the conversation.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 2 — TECHNICAL CO-FOUNDER ALONE:

RED FLAG: Revenue has been flat for more than two consecutive quarters
  despite product improvements.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Product-market fit for the early community exists
  but commercial expansion is stalled. GTM gap is now costing real
  revenue.
VERIFICATION: Ask for month-by-month new customer count for last
  6 months, not just ARR total.

RED FLAG: Deck contains a "go-to-market" section that describes a
  community or content strategy with no mention of direct sales activity.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder is defaulting to channels that suit their
  personality rather than channels that suit the product's ACV.
VERIFICATION: If ACV >£5K, community-led GTM alone is not sufficient.
  Ask how many deals have been closed through direct outbound.

RED FLAG: The only named investors or advisors are technical — no
  commercial operator or GTM executive in the advisory stack.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder's network does not include people who can
  challenge the commercial gaps or make introductions to buyers.
VERIFICATION: Ask who in their network has built a sales team from
  zero and what advice they have given.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 3 — SECOND-TIME (SUCCESS):

RED FLAG: Current company is pursuing the same buyer persona as the
  prior company in a different vertical, without evidence that the
  persona exists at the required scale in the new vertical.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Applying the prior playbook without validating
  that the new market has the same structure.
VERIFICATION: Ask for evidence of demand from buyers outside the
  prior company's customer base.

RED FLAG: Deck references the prior exit as a primary credential
  but does not describe what specifically was learned from building it.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Prior success is being used as a fundraising
  credential rather than an operational input.
VERIFICATION: Ask what the second company does differently from
  the first, specifically.

RED FLAG: All named early customers are from the founder's prior
  company's customer base or the acquirer's network.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Network advantage of prior success is strong
  but may have finite depth. Post-network growth is unproven.
VERIFICATION: Ask what % of current pipeline has no prior
  relationship with the founder.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 4 — SECOND-TIME (FAILURE):

RED FLAG: Prior company is not mentioned in the deck despite
  a visible gap in the founding team's timeline.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder is concealing the prior failure rather
  than using it as a learning signal. Omission is a character flag.
VERIFICATION: Request full founder biographies including all
  prior ventures before advancing.

RED FLAG: Current company operates in the same market as the
  prior failure with no articulated structural difference in approach.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Primary failure mode: repeating the same
  mistake with new investors.
VERIFICATION: Ask specifically what is structurally different
  about the current company versus the prior one.

RED FLAG: Deck contains language that attributes the prior failure
  to market timing, macro conditions, or a single external event.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: External attribution without internal analysis.
  The structural cause of failure has not been identified.
VERIFICATION: Ask what the founder would have done differently
  on day one if they had known what they know now.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 5 — VISIONARY WITHOUT OPERATOR:

RED FLAG: Advisory board section appears before the team section
  or the product section in the deck.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder is leading with credibility proxies because
  operational substance is insufficient.
VERIFICATION: Ask what each named advisor has done for the company
  in the last 90 days, specifically.

RED FLAG: Revenue figures are presented in ARR terms but the
  underlying contracts are pilots, POCs, or LOIs rather than
  signed recurring agreements.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Narrative inflation of commercial traction.
  Pilots and ARR are materially different things.
VERIFICATION: Request the actual signed contract value, term, and
  renewal clause for each named revenue figure.

RED FLAG: Deck contains a market map or competitive landscape
  with the company positioned at the intersection of two large
  trends, but no customer quote describing why they chose this
  product over alternatives.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Market analysis is analyst-quality, not
  operator-quality. Founder understands the market intellectually
  but does not yet understand the buyer's decision process.
VERIFICATION: Ask for verbatim quotes from the last three
  customers on why they chose this product.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 6 — YOUNG PRODIGY:

RED FLAG: Headcount has grown faster than revenue for two or more
  consecutive quarters.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder is hiring ahead of commercial validation —
  a pattern that indicates inexperience with burn management.
VERIFICATION: Request headcount-by-quarter vs ARR-by-quarter
  for the last 4 quarters.

RED FLAG: Cap table shows more than 25% held by angels or advisors
  at pre-Series A stage.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Excessive early dilution through inexperienced
  fundraising. Founder ownership may be insufficient to sustain
  motivation through a 7–10 year company-building journey.
VERIFICATION: Request cap table summary. Flag if founder(s) own
  less than 50% combined at the current stage.

RED FLAG: Team section shows multiple hires who are the founder's
  university peers with limited professional experience.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Hiring from comfort zone rather than for
  capability gaps. Peer network hiring works at founding stage
  but signals that the founder has not yet built external credibility
  as an employer.
VERIFICATION: Ask what criteria were used to hire the last
  three team members and whether any experienced operators applied.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 7 — SERIAL ENTREPRENEUR (EARLY EXITS):

RED FLAG: Deck mentions strategic partners or acquirers as part
  of the go-to-market strategy before Series A.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder is already positioning for an exit
  rather than for scale. The GTM strategy is a relationship strategy
  with a specific acquirer embedded in it.
VERIFICATION: Ask what the company looks like if none of the
  named strategic partners become customers or acquirers.

RED FLAG: Each prior company was sold within 36 months of founding.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Pattern of rapid cycles suggests preference for
  the early stage rather than commitment to scale. Current company
  may follow the same cycle.
VERIFICATION: Ask what is different about this company that would
  cause the founder to hold it beyond 36 months.

RED FLAG: Management team is thin relative to ARR and headcount —
  no VP or Director-level hires despite being at £1M+ ARR.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Founder has not yet built the management layer
  because prior companies were sold before it was needed.
VERIFICATION: Ask who the most senior non-founder hire is and
  what their mandate is.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TYPE 8 — COMPLEMENTARY CO-FOUNDER PAIR:

RED FLAG: One founder's name appears throughout the deck and the
  other is mentioned only in the team section.
SEVERITY: HIGH.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Suspected nominal co-founder — one founder is
  operating at full capacity, the other is a credential holder.
  Remove the nominal co-founder test: if removed, would the
  company be unable to function? If it would function normally,
  the pair is not genuinely complementary.
VERIFICATION: Ask what specific decisions the second founder has
  made in the last 30 days and what they own exclusively.

RED FLAG: Equity split is described as 50/50 but one founder joined
  significantly later than the other.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: The equity split was negotiated on relationship
  terms rather than contribution terms. This creates resentment
  risk as the company scales and roles diverge.
VERIFICATION: Ask when each founder joined, what they each
  contributed at founding, and whether vesting is in place.

RED FLAG: Neither founder has experience managing teams of more
  than 10 people, but the company is projecting 40+ headcount
  within 18 months.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
WHAT IT SUGGESTS: Complementary skills cover product and sales
  but neither founder has experience at the organisational scale
  the plan requires.
VERIFICATION: Ask who the founders have identified as the
  first non-founder executive hire and what their timeline is.
