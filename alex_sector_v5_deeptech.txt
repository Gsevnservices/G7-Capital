SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Deep Tech / Hardware):
— Core product requires a scientific breakthrough or novel engineering that took years
  of R&D to achieve and cannot be replicated by a competent engineering team in 6–12
  months without the same foundational research
— Product contains physical components — hardware, materials, devices, or biological
  constructs — not purely software running on commodity infrastructure
— Commercial timeline from current state to first paying revenue is 18 months or more,
  driven by technical development, regulatory approval, or manufacturing scale-up
— Primary defensibility is IP: granted or pending patents, trade secrets, exclusive
  licences, or proprietary datasets that took years to generate — not go-to-market speed
— Technology Readiness Level (TRL) framework is the most appropriate stage assessment
  rather than ARR or customer count

CRITICAL DISTINCTION — DEEP TECH vs HARDWARE PRODUCT vs SOFTWARE:
A company building a new kind of battery chemistry that requires 4 years of materials
science R&D is Deep Tech.
A company building a consumer IoT device using off-the-shelf sensors and a cloud
backend is a hardware product company — apply SaaS Library benchmarks to the software
component and consumer hardware benchmarks to the device economics.
A company building novel AI software architectures without physical product may qualify
as Deep Tech Software (sub-category 8) if the innovation constitutes a genuine scientific
breakthrough — not standard ML engineering.
Test: would a top-10 engineering school need to dedicate a funded PhD programme to
replicate this? If yes — Deep Tech. If a talented senior engineer could replicate it
in 12 months with existing tools — not Deep Tech.

APPLYING THE MODIFIED FRAMEWORK NOTE:
Alex's standard screening framework does not apply directly to pre-revenue deep tech.
For companies at TRL 4–6, the screening note must substitute:
— Revenue traction → Non-revenue validation signals (government grants, paid pilots,
  LOIs, peer-reviewed publications, defence contracts)
— NRR / logo churn → IP portfolio strength and freedom to operate status
— CAC / LTV → Capital efficiency to next TRL milestone
— Burn multiple → Capital requirement to first commercial revenue
— Founder archetype → Type 2 modified (scientific founder framework) as defined in
  Framework 2 of the Analytical Frameworks section

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A robotics company that uses off-the-shelf motors and standard computer vision is a
  hardware product company with SaaS software — do not apply Deep Tech protection to
  commodity hardware assembly
— A biotech company in clinical trials for a pharmaceutical drug is NOT covered by
  this library — apply a pharma-specific framework (drug discovery timelines and FDA/MHRA
  clinical trial pathways differ fundamentally from Deep Tech Hardware commercialisation)
— A "quantum-inspired" algorithm company that runs on classical computers is a software
  company; true quantum technology requires quantum hardware or quantum-native algorithms

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. ADVANCED MATERIALS
Signals: product is a novel material or material processing technology with demonstrably
superior properties (conductivity, strength, weight, biocompatibility, thermal management)
compared to existing alternatives; submission references material characterisation data,
synthesis routes, or manufacturing process IP; competitive set includes established
material suppliers (BASF, DuPont, 3M, Toray) rather than software or SaaS companies;
TRL is typically 3–7 at investment stage; development timeline is measured in years
not months.
Business model: typically B2B materials supply (per-kg or per-unit pricing) or licensing
of synthesis/processing IP to manufacturers. Gross margin at scale depends heavily on
whether the company manufactures itself (30–55%) or licences its process (60–80%).
Key risk: materials companies face a double scale-up challenge — proving the material
works at lab scale AND proving it can be manufactured at commercial scale with consistent
quality. Each step requires a separate capital raise and 18–36 months.

2. SEMICONDUCTORS / CHIPS
Signals: company is designing custom silicon — AI accelerators, power management chips,
photonic integrated circuits, RF chips, or application-specific integrated circuits
(ASICs); submission references tape-out timeline, process node (e.g., TSMC 5nm, Samsung
3nm), die area, or power consumption benchmarks; team includes chip architects with
prior experience at Intel, AMD, Arm, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, or Apple silicon; funding is
being used for EDA tooling, IP licensing, and foundry tape-out costs.
Fabless model: company designs the chip, outsources manufacturing to TSMC, Samsung, or
GLOBALFOUNDRIES. Capital requirement: tape-out costs range from £2M (mature node, small
die) to £50M+ (leading-edge node, large die). No manufacturing capex but concentrated
foundry risk.
IDM or fab model: company builds or co-invests in its own fabrication. Capital requirement:
£500M–£10B+. Not relevant for early-stage investment — if a startup claims to be
building its own fab, treat as a serious red flag unless it has sovereign or strategic
funding commitments of this scale.
Key risk: chip design has a long cycle — from tape-out to first silicon is 12–18 months.
A company that has not yet taped out has no verified hardware and its projected
performance specifications are estimates, not measured results.

3. ROBOTICS / AUTOMATION
Signals: product is a physical robotic system integrating custom hardware (actuators,
sensors, structural components) with proprietary software (perception, motion planning,
control systems); submission references robot throughput, payload capacity, precision
specifications, or cost per task comparison to human labour; competitive set includes
ABB, Fanuc, KUKA, Boston Dynamics, or vertical-specific incumbents; revenue model is
hardware sale + software subscription (the latter provides recurring revenue and
protects the ongoing customer relationship).
Business model: hardware margin (typically 30–50%) + software subscription per robot
per year (typically £2K–£20K/robot/year depending on application complexity).
Unit economics target: software subscription revenue should cover customer support and
ongoing R&D cost within 3–4 years of first deployment.
Key risk: hardware manufacturing introduces supply chain risk absent from software —
component shortages (as experienced in 2021–2023) can halt production for months.
Also: robots deployed in industrial settings require safety certification (CE marking,
ISO 10218) which adds 6–18 months to commercial deployment timeline.

4. QUANTUM COMPUTING / SENSING
Signals: product is based on quantum mechanical principles — superposition, entanglement,
or quantum tunnelling — that provide a genuine computational or sensing advantage over
classical alternatives; submission references qubit count, coherence time, gate fidelity,
or quantum volume; team includes quantum physicists with peer-reviewed publications;
current funding is primarily from government sources (Innovate UK, DARPA, ARIA, EIC,
national quantum programmes); competitive set includes IBM, Google, IonQ, Quantinuum,
or Pasqal depending on the qubit modality.
Important context: as of 2026, quantum computing has not demonstrated commercial quantum
advantage for any real-world problem at scale. Any company claiming current commercial
quantum advantage (not sensing, but computing) should be scrutinised carefully.
Quantum sensing is significantly further ahead commercially — quantum magnetometers and
quantum gyroscopes are at TRL 6–7 for defence and industrial applications.
Business model: typically government contract and grant funded at early stage; commercial
revenue from cloud access (quantum-as-a-service) or specialised hardware sale to defence,
national labs, and financial institutions is a 5–8 year commercialisation path for computing.
Key risk: very long time to commercial revenue; government funding risk if political
priorities shift; technology may be superseded by improved classical algorithms (quantum
advantage window is not permanent).

5. SPACE TECHNOLOGY
Signals: product is a space vehicle, satellite, ground station, or in-space service;
submission references launch manifest, orbital parameters, satellite bus specifications,
or Earth observation revisit frequency; regulatory licences include UK Space Agency
licence (under UK Space Industry Act 2018), FCC frequency coordination, or ITU filing;
revenue sources include government contracts, anchor commercial customers, and data
product licensing; competitive set includes Airbus Defence and Space, Boeing, Lockheed
Martin, SpaceX, Planet Labs, or sub-sector specific incumbents.
Business model: highly variable — launch services (per-launch contracts, typically
£5M–£100M per mission); small satellite manufacturing (£500K–£10M per satellite);
Earth observation data products (subscription or per-image licensing).
Key risk: extreme capital intensity; single launch failure can destroy the primary
asset and the company simultaneously; launch windows create rigid commercial timelines;
US ITAR regulations can restrict team composition and technology exports.

6. CLEANTECH HARDWARE
Signals: product is a physical device or system for energy production, storage, efficiency,
or environmental remediation; submission references energy density, efficiency coefficients,
cost per kilowatt-hour, or carbon capture rates; revenue model is hardware sale,
performance-based contract, or energy-as-a-service; funding often includes government
grants (Net Zero Innovation Portfolio, Innovate UK, US DOE loan programmes);
competitive set includes established energy companies, battery manufacturers, or
industrial equipment suppliers.
Business model: hardware sale with installation services (gross margin 20–45%); may
include performance monitoring SaaS (gross margin 70%+) as an overlay revenue stream.
Key risk: policy dependency — cleantech hardware economics are often contingent on
government subsidies, tax credits, or regulatory mandates (e.g., heat pump installation
targets, EV charging infrastructure requirements). Policy reversal can eliminate the
commercial rationale overnight.

7. BIOTECH / SYNTHETIC BIOLOGY
Signals: product is an engineered biological system — novel proteins, engineered microbes,
synthetic biology platforms, biologically produced materials, or biological manufacturing
processes; submission references expression yield, titre, selectivity, or in vivo efficacy
data; team includes PhD-level scientists in molecular biology, biochemistry, or
bioengineering; IP strategy centres on patents for novel organisms, processes, or
biological constructs; competitive set includes established biotech companies, chemical
manufacturers (BASF, DSM), or agricultural companies (Bayer, Corteva).
Business model: varies by application — industrial biotechnology (licensing or manufacturing
for chemicals, materials, fuels); agricultural biotech (trait licensing per acre);
medical biotech (product sale or licensing to pharma companies).
Key risk: biological systems are complex and often unpredictable at scale — fermentation
scale-up from flask to industrial bioreactor introduces yield losses that can make
economics unviable at commercial scale. Regulatory approval for GMO applications varies
significantly between UK (post-Brexit divergence), EU, and US.

8. DEEP TECH SOFTWARE
Signals: software product is based on a novel scientific approach — not standard
engineering using existing frameworks; examples include novel AI architectures that
require fundamental research to develop (not fine-tuning existing models), computational
physics simulation software that required years of algorithmic development, or
cryptographic systems based on novel mathematical constructs; submission includes
peer-reviewed publications from the founding team; competitive set does not include
standard SaaS companies but rather research labs, national computing centres, or
the R&D departments of major technology companies.
Business model: typically SaaS subscription once productised; may include API access
model similar to Developer Tools sub-category in B2B SaaS Library.
Key risk: the primary risk is that the scientific advantage is narrower or shorter-lived
than anticipated — a competitor with more compute resources can often approximate the
results of novel algorithms within 18–24 months, especially in AI. IP protection for
software is weaker than for hardware (patents are harder to enforce; trade secrets are
harder to maintain).

SECTION B — STAGE-SPECIFIC METRIC BENCHMARKS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Format: STRONG (top quartile) | ACCEPTABLE (median) | CONCERN (flag) | FAIL (disqualifying)

NOTE: Deep tech companies are assessed primarily on TRL progression, IP strength,
and non-revenue validation signals at early stages. Standard SaaS revenue benchmarks
do not apply until TRL 8–9. Apply the revenue benchmarks in this section alongside
TRL assessment — a company at TRL 7 with £2M ARR is exceptional; at TRL 4 with no
revenue is expected.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TECHNOLOGY READINESS LEVEL (TRL) FRAMEWORK:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TRL 1 — Basic principles observed and reported.
  Research only. Not investable for VC. Appropriate for university seed grants.

TRL 2 — Technology concept and application formulated.
  Theoretical design. Not investable for VC. May attract government programme funding.

TRL 3 — Analytical and experimental proof of concept.
  Lab demonstration of core principle. Not yet investable unless IP is exceptional and
  team is world-class. ARIA, Innovate UK Frontier grants appropriate.

TRL 4 — Component and subsystem validation in laboratory environment.
  Core technology demonstrated in lab. Pre-seed investable if: strong IP, world-class
  team, clear path to TRL 6 within 18 months, and market need validated.
  Investment risk: very high. Expected outcome: advance to TRL 5–6.

TRL 5 — Component and subsystem validation in relevant environment.
  Technology works outside the laboratory in conditions approaching real use.
  Seed investable. Minimum viable for most deep tech investors.
  Investment risk: high. Expected outcome: first customer pilots within 12–18 months.

TRL 6 — System and subsystem model or prototype demonstration in relevant environment.
  Prototype demonstrated in conditions representative of real deployment.
  Series A appropriate when: prototype demonstrated to customers, letters of intent
  received, clear manufacturing pathway to TRL 9 identified.
  Investment risk: medium-high.

TRL 7 — System prototype demonstration in operational environment.
  Full-scale prototype tested in operational conditions. First paid pilots running.
  Series A or B depending on capital requirements and revenue evidence.
  Investment risk: medium.

TRL 8 — System complete and qualified.
  Product complete, qualified, and demonstrated in its operational environment.
  First commercial deployments possible. Series B appropriate.
  Investment risk: medium-low.

TRL 9 — Actual system proven in operational environment.
  Full commercial deployment. Revenue benchmark applies at this stage.
  Investment risk: commercial execution risk (same as any early-revenue company).

INVESTABILITY BY TRL AND STAGE:
Pre-seed:  TRL 3–5 (exceptional IP + world-class team required at TRL 3–4)
Seed:      TRL 4–6 (TRL 4 requires exceptional circumstances)
Series A:  TRL 6–8 (TRL 6 acceptable with strong LOIs and capital plan)
Series B:  TRL 8–9 (revenue evidence required)

TRL PROGRESSION BENCHMARKS:
STRONG: advances 1 TRL level per 12 months with planned capital | ACCEPTABLE: 1 level per 18 months
CONCERN: 1 level per 24 months or repeated misses of TRL milestones
FAIL: claimed TRL higher than what technical diligence supports (TRL inflation)

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
NON-REVENUE VALIDATION SIGNALS (for TRL 4–7):
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TIER 1 — Strongest validation (equivalent to revenue for pre-commercial deep tech):
— Paid pilot or proof-of-concept contract with a named strategic customer at
  commercial-intent pricing (not a research grant or in-kind contribution)
— Defence or government procurement contract with milestone payments
— Signed Letter of Intent from a customer committing to purchase at commercial scale
  contingent on TRL milestone achievement
— Strategic partnership with a named corporate including an equity investment in
  the company (signifies the corporate has assessed and validated the technology)

TIER 2 — Strong validation:
— Innovate UK Smart Award, Innovate UK KTP, ARIA grant, or EIC Accelerator (EU) —
  awarded through competitive peer-review process
— SBRI (Small Business Research Initiative) contract with UK government department
  (MOD, NHS, UKSA, BEIS) — customer validation plus non-dilutive capital
— DARPA or US government SBIR/STTR award for UK-US dual-listed or US subsidiary companies
— Peer-reviewed publication in a top-10 journal for the relevant field demonstrating
  the claimed technical performance

TIER 3 — Supporting validation (not sufficient alone):
— Academic collaboration with a named university group as co-development partner
— Membership of a government-backed accelerator (Catapult programmes, DSTL partnerships)
— Non-binding expression of interest from a named customer
— Industry award or competition win (note: these validate recognition, not commercial intent)

FAIL CONDITION for non-revenue validation: a deep tech company at TRL 6 with no Tier 1
  or Tier 2 validation signals after 3+ years of development has not yet demonstrated
  that its technology has value to any external organisation willing to pay for it.
  This is the most important red flag in deep tech: laboratory success that no customer
  or government programme has validated commercially.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CAPITAL BENCHMARKS BY DEEP TECH SUB-CATEGORY:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

These are the typical total capital required from founding to first significant commercial
revenue (£1M+ ARR or equivalent government contract value):

Advanced Materials:       £5–25M (lab validation + pilot manufacturing line + first customers)
Semiconductors (fabless): £15–60M (design + tape-out + bring-up + first customer silicon)
Robotics:                 £5–20M (prototype + safety certification + pilot deployments)
Quantum Computing:        £20–100M+ (qubit system + dilution refrigeration + team)
Quantum Sensing:          £5–25M (device + calibration + first commercial deployments)
Space Technology:         £30–200M+ (depending on mission type — small sat vs launch)
Cleantech Hardware:       £10–50M (pilot installation + performance validation)
Synthetic Biology:        £5–30M (strain development + fermentation scale-up + regulatory)
Deep Tech Software:       £2–10M (research team + compute infrastructure + first customers)

Flag: any deep tech company that has raised less than 50% of the expected capital requirement
  for its sub-category and is claiming to be within 12 months of commercial revenue without
  a credible manufacturing or deployment partner is using an unrealistic timeline.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
REVENUE BENCHMARKS AT TRL 8-9 (Commercial Stage):
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SEED (First commercial revenue — TRL 8):
Revenue:       STRONG >£500K | ACCEPTABLE £100–500K | CONCERN <£100K with no pipeline
Customers:     STRONG ≥3 paying at commercial pricing | ACCEPTABLE 1–2 | CONCERN: all still pilots
Gross margin:  STRONG >45% | ACCEPTABLE 25–45% | CONCERN 10–25% | FAIL <10%
  Note: hardware gross margin at first commercial scale is often 20–35%; concern threshold
  is lower than SaaS because hardware margins improve with scale through manufacturing
  learning curves. Flag if gross margin is not expected to exceed 40% at 10x volume.

SERIES A (Scaling commercial deployment — TRL 9):
Revenue:       STRONG >£3M | ACCEPTABLE £1–3M | CONCERN <£1M with no clear catalyst
YoY growth:    STRONG >100% | ACCEPTABLE 60–100% | CONCERN 30–60% | FAIL <30%
  Note: deep tech revenue growth rates from first commercial year are often 200–400%
  due to the low base; the concern is below 60% from a base above £1M.
Gross margin:  STRONG >55% (hardware) or >70% (software layer) | ACCEPTABLE 35–55% | FAIL <25%
Pipeline:      STRONG: ≥5 named customers in formal procurement with total value >3x current ARR
Recurring revenue %: STRONG >40% of total revenue from software subscriptions or consumables
  Note: a deep tech company at Series A with 0% recurring revenue is entirely hardware-
  sale dependent — lumpier cash flows and lower valuation multiple. Push for recurring
  revenue layer evidence.

SERIES B (Scaling manufacturing and market):
Revenue:       STRONG >£10M | ACCEPTABLE £4–10M | CONCERN <£4M
Gross margin:  STRONG >60% (hardware) | ACCEPTABLE 40–60% | CONCERN <40% | FAIL <30%
Manufacturing capacity: STRONG: can 5x current production volume within 12 months
  with committed capital; CONCERN: bottlenecked on single manufacturing partner
Bill of Materials (BoM) cost trend: STRONG: declining 15–25% per doubling of units
  (Wright's Law learning curve); CONCERN: flat BoM cost after 3x volume increase

VALUATION BENCHMARKS — Deep Tech (2025–2026 market):
Pre-seed (TRL 3–5): £1–5M enterprise value — almost entirely team + IP premium
Seed (TRL 5–7):     £5–20M | Strong validation: £15–30M
Series A (TRL 7–8): Revenue multiple not applicable; £20–80M based on strategic value
Series B (TRL 9):   4–12x ARR (hardware) | 8–20x ARR (software-dominant deep tech)
Premium driver: exclusive IP position in a market that cannot be addressed without the
  technology. A deep tech company that is the only commercially viable path to a
  £500M+ addressable market commands a strategic premium above any revenue multiple.
Note: acquirer-led valuations dominate deep tech exits. Strategic acquirer premiums
  (Intel, Qualcomm, BASF, Boeing) frequently exceed 10–20x the last private market valuation.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. IP NOT FULLY OWNED BY THE COMPANY — UNIVERSITY SPIN-OUT RISK
SIGNAL: Company is a spin-out from a university and the core IP was developed in a
university laboratory. The IP licence agreement has not been reviewed or the university
retains: (a) ownership of the underlying patents with only a field-of-use licence to the
company; (b) sub-licensing rights; (c) the right to terminate the licence upon certain
conditions; or (d) a significant revenue share or equity stake that creates a misaligned
incentive at exit.
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: A deep tech company that does not own its core IP outright cannot freely
sell, licence, or exploit the technology without the university's consent. In an
acquisition, the buyer is acquiring a licence — not the asset — which materially reduces
the acquirer's willingness to pay and their ability to defend the IP post-acquisition.
Universities have in the past blocked transactions or extracted material value from
spin-outs at exit.
VERIFICATION: Request the full IP assignment or licence agreement between the company
and the originating institution. Confirm: (1) whether patents are owned by or assigned
to the company; (2) whether there are any third-party licences, government "march-in"
rights, or revenue share obligations attached to the IP.

02. LEAD SCIENTIST HAS NO EQUITY AND NO LONG-TERM COMMITMENT
SIGNAL: The company's primary scientific innovator — the person who invented or developed
the core technology — is not on the founding team with equity, or is a part-time
consultant, or remains employed by the university and only advises the company.
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: Deep tech companies run on the lead scientist's continued active
contribution. If the lead scientist leaves — to return to academia, join a competitor,
or pursue a different project — the company loses its primary technical capability.
Unlike software companies where the codebase persists after a founder departs, deep
tech IP is often tacit knowledge — it lives in the scientist's head and in unpublished
laboratory methods that cannot be fully documented.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Is [lead scientist] a full-time employee of the company with equity
on a standard vesting schedule? What is their current employment status? If they left
tomorrow, what would the company lose that it cannot replace within 12 months?"

03. NO MANUFACTURING SCALE-UP PLAN OR BUDGET
SIGNAL: Company is at TRL 6–7 and has demonstrated the technology in a prototype but
has no documented manufacturing scale-up plan. The submission describes a "clear path
to manufacturing" without naming the manufacturer, the process, the capital requirement,
or the timeline to first production unit.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: For hardware deep tech, the gap between a working prototype and a
manufacturable product is often as large as the gap between an idea and a prototype.
Manufacturing scale-up requires: identifying a contract manufacturer or building in-house
capability; qualifying the manufacturing process; achieving consistent yield; and reducing
BoM cost to achieve target gross margin. A company that treats manufacturing as "the next
step after technical validation" is underestimating the time and capital required by 2–4x.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Who is your manufacturing partner or what is your manufacturing
plan? What is the capital requirement to achieve your first 1,000 production units?
What is your target BoM cost and when will you achieve it? Have you done a Design for
Manufacturing review with a contract manufacturer?"

04. HARDWARE COST NOT DECLINING PER LEARNING CURVE
SIGNAL: Company has produced 5–10 production units and the cost per unit (BoM +
manufacturing labour) has not declined materially from the first unit. Wright's Law
predicts that cost should decline 15–30% per doubling of cumulative production for
most hardware categories. Flat cost after several production runs indicates either
a novel manufacturing challenge or a BoM dominated by commodity components with no
learning curve improvement potential.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A deep tech product that does not benefit from manufacturing learning
curves has a structural gross margin problem. If the target gross margin of 40%+ requires
a BoM cost reduction that the learning curve is not delivering, the business model
at commercial scale is financially unviable. Investors who funded the business on the
assumption of learning-curve BoM improvement need to reassess the gross margin projection.
VERIFICATION: Request BoM cost breakdown for each production batch to date. Calculate
cost reduction per doubling of units. Compare against the Wright's Law expectation for
the manufacturing process type. If cost is flat, ask: "What specifically needs to happen
to achieve your target BoM cost? At what production volume does it become achievable?"

05. TRL CLAIMED HIGHER THAN DEMONSTRATED
SIGNAL: Company claims TRL 7 or 8 but technical diligence reveals the product has only
been demonstrated in a controlled laboratory environment (TRL 5–6) rather than in an
operational environment. May describe a laboratory demonstration as "field deployment"
or a simulation as "operational testing."
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: TRL inflation is common in deep tech fundraising because each TRL step
significantly increases investability and valuation. A company claiming TRL 7 on the
basis of a controlled lab demonstration is overstating its readiness by 12–18 months
of development time. Investors who fund at TRL 7 valuation and discover the product
is at TRL 5 have funded 2 TRL steps they did not price for.
VERIFICATION: Apply the NATO/NASA TRL definitions strictly. For TRL 7, the specific
test is: "System prototype demonstration in an operational environment." Ask: "Where
specifically was the demonstration carried out? Was it a controlled laboratory environment
or an environment that represents actual operational conditions? Who conducted the evaluation?"

06. GOVERNMENT GRANT DEPENDENCY WITHOUT PRIVATE CAPITAL BRIDGE
SIGNAL: Company has been funded primarily or entirely by government grants (Innovate UK,
ARIA, Horizon Europe, MOD DSTL). It has never raised a private equity round. Its next
development milestone requires £2M+ that it is planning to fund through another grant
application rather than a private capital raise.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: A deep tech company that has never been validated by a private investor
has an unresolved commercial question: is the technology valuable enough for someone
who does not have a public mandate to fund science to invest their own capital? Government
grant validation (Tier 2 in Section B) is evidence of scientific merit, not commercial
merit. A company in a permanent grant-dependency cycle is potentially a research project
with a company structure, not an investable business.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What private investment have you raised to date? Why have you not
yet sought private venture capital? What would a private investor need to see to justify
the capital required for your next development stage? Have you had discussions with
venture investors?"

07. SINGLE MANUFACTURING OR SUPPLY CHAIN PARTNER FOR CRITICAL COMPONENT
SIGNAL: Company's product depends on a single supplier for a critical component — a
specific alloy, a custom semiconductor, a biological feedstock, or a proprietary sensor
— that cannot be sourced from an alternative supplier without a 12–24 month qualification
process.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Supply chain concentration is the hardware equivalent of banking partner
concentration in Fintech. A supplier who knows they are sole-source for a critical
component has pricing power and reliability leverage. If the supplier is acquired by
a competitor, has a production failure, or unilaterally changes commercial terms, the
deep tech company cannot fulfil its customer commitments.
VERIFICATION: For each critical component in the BoM, ask: "How many qualified suppliers
exist for this component globally? Have you qualified an alternative supplier? What is
the lead time to qualification if your primary supplier fails?"

08. NO PATH TO GROSS MARGIN ABOVE 40% AT SCALE
SIGNAL: Product gross margin at current production volume is below 25%, and the projected
gross margin at 100x production volume does not exceed 40% based on the BoM cost
structure. The primary BoM cost driver is a commodity material or component that will
not improve with the company's own scale.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A deep tech hardware company with a structural gross margin ceiling below
40% cannot generate sufficient margin to fund R&D, sales, and overhead at scale while
remaining profitable. The unit economics of the business model are permanently impaired.
This is distinct from a company with 25% gross margin today that will reach 55% at scale
— the former is acceptable at early stage; the latter is a structural failure.
VERIFICATION: Request the BoM at current scale and at projected 100x scale. Calculate
gross margin at each volume level. If 100x scale gross margin is below 40%, ask: "What
would need to change in your manufacturing process or BoM to achieve 50%+ gross margin?
Is there a software or services layer you have not yet modelled?"

09. FOUNDING TEAM IS ALL SCIENTISTS — NO COMMERCIAL OPERATOR
SIGNAL: Founding team consists entirely of scientists or engineers with no commercial
background. There is no CEO with commercial operator experience, no business development
lead who has sold hardware to enterprise customers, and no finance function that has
managed the capital requirements of a hardware scale-up.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: This is the Scientist-Only Founding Team failure pattern (Section D).
Deep tech companies that fail commercially despite excellent science almost always share
this characteristic. The skills required to validate a technology (scientific rigour,
experimental methodology, peer review) are different from the skills required to
commercialise it (customer discovery, pricing, manufacturing operations, fundraising).
A team that has never done the second set of tasks will discover the cost of learning
them during a period when the company's most valuable resource is the lead scientist's
focus.
VERIFICATION: For each founding team member, ask: "What is your experience with hardware
commercialisation — specifically, have you taken a hardware product from TRL 7 to commercial
revenue before? Have you managed a supply chain at production scale? Have you personally
closed a £500K+ hardware contract?" One or more founders must answer yes.

10. PATENT STRATEGY INSUFFICIENT FOR IP PROTECTION
SIGNAL: Company's primary IP protection is a single patent or patent application in one
jurisdiction (typically UK or US only). The patent covers the end product but not the
manufacturing process or the enabling materials. There are no continuation applications
covering variations that could be designed around by a competitor.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A single patent in one jurisdiction is a limited moat. A well-resourced
competitor (BASF, Qualcomm, Boeing) can design around a narrow patent, file in
jurisdictions where the patent has no coverage, or simply wait for the patent to expire
(20 years from filing). A deep tech company whose entire commercial thesis rests on
one patent is in a fragile IP position.
VERIFICATION: Request the IP schedule: list of all granted patents, pending applications,
jurisdictions covered, filing dates, and expiry dates. Ask: "Who conducted the freedom
to operate analysis? Are there any third-party patents that could block your commercial
activity? What is your strategy if a competitor files a design-around?"

11. NO CUSTOMER COMMITTED TO PAYING FOR THE COMMERCIAL PRODUCT
SIGNAL: Company has been developing its technology for 3+ years and is at TRL 6–7
but does not have a single customer who has committed to paying for the commercial
product — even conditionally. Customer engagement consists of research collaborations,
unpaid pilots, or informal expressions of interest.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: At TRL 6, a deep tech company should have at least Tier 1 or Tier 2
validation (see Section B). If after 3 years of development no customer has been willing
to commit money — not just interest — the technology may be solving a problem that does
not have a commercial buyer at any price. This is the deepest form of product-market fit
failure in deep tech: technically impressive but commercially unwanted.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Is there any customer who has paid you money — or committed to pay
you money contingent on achieving a specific milestone — for your technology? What is
the earliest such commitment? What do your most engaged potential customers say when you
ask them what they would pay for the commercial product?"

12. REGULATORY APPROVAL TIMELINE UNDERESTIMATED BY 2+ YEARS
SIGNAL: Company's financial model projects regulatory approval and commercial launch
within 18 months. The regulatory pathway — medical device approval, CE marking for
a new materials class, space launch licence, or GMO authorisation — realistically
requires 3–5 years. Company may have received informal positive signals from a
regulatory body without a formal application in process.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Regulatory approval is the second most common cause of deep tech company
failure after the Valley of Death. A company that has modelled its capital requirements
assuming an 18-month regulatory timeline and then discovers it needs 48 months will run
out of capital mid-process. The cost of a failed regulatory application — and the time
required to resubmit — is typically not included in financial models built by founding
teams without regulatory counsel.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Who is your regulatory counsel? Have you submitted a formal
application or had a pre-submission meeting with the relevant regulator? What is the
official expected timeline from the regulator, in writing?"

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE VALLEY OF DEATH
DESCRIPTION: A deep tech company successfully validates its technology at TRL 4–5 in
the laboratory and raises a seed round of £1–3M. The seed round covers laboratory
validation, initial IP filing, and the first prototype. At TRL 6–7, the company needs
£5–15M to build a manufacturable prototype, qualify a manufacturing process, and conduct
customer pilots at commercial scale. This capital requirement is too large for angel
investors and too early-stage (no revenue, TRL 7 not yet proven) for most institutional
VCs. The company cannot bridge the gap between seed validation and Series A readiness.
It applies for more grants, extends its runway by reducing headcount, and loses 12–18
months of development time. Key scientific staff find more stable positions elsewhere.
The technology is real, the IP is strong, but the company cannot raise the capital to
get from where it is to where it needs to be to attract institutional capital. This is
the Valley of Death — and it kills more viable deep tech companies than any competitive
or technical failure.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Seed round was entirely government grant funded with no private
equity; the founding team has not mapped the capital requirement from current TRL to
commercial revenue; the company does not have a plan B if the next grant application
is rejected; the capital requirement to Series A readiness exceeds 3x the seed round
raised; no institutional deep tech investor has engaged with the company at seed stage.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any deep tech company at TRL 5–7 with less than 18 months of runway
and no institutional investor committed to the next round. The Valley of Death is not
a metaphor — it is a specific and predictable capital gap that kills companies at this
exact stage.

2. THE MANUFACTURING SCALE-UP WALL
DESCRIPTION: A company successfully demonstrates a product at TRL 8 — in an operational
environment, with paying pilot customers, at acceptable performance specifications.
It raises a Series A to scale manufacturing. In the first production run at 10x the
pilot volume, yield is 30% below the pilot yield. The product works but the manufacturing
process that worked at small scale introduces defects, inconsistencies, or performance
degradation at higher throughput. The company spends 12–18 months and an additional
£3–5M of unplanned capital resolving manufacturing quality issues. Customer deliveries
are delayed. Pipeline customers who were waiting for commercial availability lose patience
and evaluate alternatives. The Series A capital is consumed solving a manufacturing
problem that the team did not anticipate because they had never scaled that manufacturing
process before.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Product was manufactured by the founding team or in a university
facility without a qualified contract manufacturer being involved; no Design for
Manufacturing (DfM) review was conducted before the first production run; the team has
no prior experience of hardware manufacturing at commercial scale; the BoM includes
components or processes that have not been qualified at target volume; yield data from
pilot production has not been shared with investors.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any deep tech hardware company raising Series A capital to scale
manufacturing without a contracted manufacturer with relevant experience at the required
volume and without documented pilot production yield data.

3. THE IP LEAKAGE TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A university spin-out company is founded with a licence to use the
university's patents. The founders believe — and describe to investors — that the IP
is effectively owned by the company. A strategic acquirer conducts IP due diligence
and discovers: the patents are owned by the university, not the company; the licence
includes a field-of-use restriction that limits the company's ability to expand into
adjacent markets; the university retains the right to licence the same IP to a third
party outside the specified field; or there is a government "march-in" right (from
government-funded research) that allows the government to licence the IP to a third
party if the company fails to commercialise it within a specified period. The acquisition
price is reduced materially, or the deal falls through entirely, because the acquirer
cannot obtain clean title to the IP.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: The IP schedule shows patents assigned to a university, a
research council, or a government body rather than the company; the company describes
its IP protection as a "licence from [university name]"; the founding agreement with
the university was negotiated without specialist IP counsel; the company has not
conducted a freedom-to-operate analysis; the university holds board observer rights
or approval rights over licensing decisions.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any university spin-out where the core IP was developed in a university
laboratory using university facilities or funding. This is the most common structural
failure in UK deep tech spin-outs — it is not rare, it is the default risk.

4. THE SCIENTIST-ONLY FOUNDING TEAM
DESCRIPTION: A company is founded by a team of brilliant scientists who have spent 5–10
years developing a breakthrough technology. They are world-class at the scientific
problem. They have no experience of hardware commercialisation, enterprise sales,
manufacturing operations, or raising institutional capital. They raise a seed round
on the strength of the technology. Over the next 24 months they discover: customer
discovery is harder and slower than expected; pricing is lower than the market will
bear for early products; manufacturing partners do not take them seriously without
a commercial operator in the room; the process of raising a Series A requires a level
of business narrative construction they are not trained to perform. The technology
continues to improve. Revenue does not materialise. The company raises a bridge round
to avoid folding, brings in an external CEO — but at this point the cultural fit between
the scientific founders and a commercial operator is difficult, the external CEO does not
understand the technology, and the company stalls for another 12–18 months during the
leadership transition.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Founding team's LinkedIn profiles show no commercial operator
experience — all career history is academic or government research; the company has
been funded for 24+ months but has no signed commercial contracts; when asked about
their commercial strategy, founders describe the technology rather than the customer
problem; the board has no one with hardware commercialisation experience; the founding
team has not yet hired a business development lead.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any deep tech company where the founding team's collective CV contains
no hardware commercialisation, enterprise sales, or manufacturing operations experience.

5. THE GOVERNMENT GRANT DEPENDENCY
DESCRIPTION: A company builds its entire development programme on a sequence of
government grants — Innovate UK, ARIA, Horizon Europe, MOD DSTL. The scientific
work is excellent. Each grant is won competitively, validating the technology's merit.
After 4–5 years and £3–5M of grant funding, the company has reached TRL 6–7 but has
never raised private equity. When the founding team approaches institutional venture
investors, they discover: institutional VCs are uncomfortable with the grant-dependent
funding history (it signals the team cannot raise commercial capital); the IP ownership
is complex because some grants require licensing IP back to the government; the
commercialisation milestones expected by VCs (customer pilots, LOIs, manufacturing
partnership) have not been prioritised because grant milestones focused on scientific
outputs; and the company's culture is closer to an academic research group than a
commercial entity. The technology is real but the business is not commercial-ready.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Funding history is 100% grants with no private equity; company
has never hired a commercial operator; grant reporting milestones reference publications
and conference presentations rather than customer validations; the team describes their
next grant application as the primary capital plan; no institutional investor has
conducted a formal investment process (even without completing an investment).
WHEN TO APPLY: Any deep tech company with more than £2M raised entirely from government
grants and no private equity investors in the cap table.

6. THE TECHNOLOGY OBSOLESCENCE TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A company spends 4–6 years developing a technology to solve a problem
that exists at the start of development. By the time the technology reaches TRL 8–9,
an alternative approach — using different materials, different physics, or a software-
only solution — has emerged that addresses the same problem more cheaply or more
effectively. The company's technology is technically excellent but has been overtaken
by an approach it could not anticipate at the start of development. This happens most
often when: (1) the incumbent problem (e.g., battery range) improves so much through
incremental progress that the breakthrough solution's advantage narrows from 3x to 1.2x
by launch; (2) a foundation model or algorithm achieves in software what the company
was building in hardware (e.g., AI replacing sensor arrays); (3) a much better-funded
competitor (Qualcomm, Google, TSMC) releases a competing solution with a product
development budget 100x larger than the startup's total capitalisation.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: The company's competitive advantage is benchmarked against
the current generation of competing technology, not a projection of where competing
technology will be in 3–5 years; the founding team is focused on technical improvement
relative to today's baseline rather than relative to the expected baseline at commercial
launch; the problem the technology solves has been substantially addressed by an
adjacent approach in the last 12 months; a well-funded incumbent has announced an
R&D programme in the same technical area.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any deep tech company with a development timeline of 4+ years where
the competitive landscape or the problem itself may change materially before commercial
launch. Apply Market Timing Matrix (Framework 3) — ask whether the market timing window
will remain open for the 3–4 years remaining before commercialisation.

Semiconductors:
  Strategic 5–20x revenue (fabless, commercial) | Acqui-hire at pre-revenue: £20–100M

  Note: semiconductor acquisitions are subject to national security review — UK NSI Act,

  Strategic 4–15x revenue (hardware + software) | PE 3–8x revenue (stable cash flows)

  hardware-only trades at 4–6x revenue.

Space Technology:
  Highly variable — launch services, satellites, and data products have entirely different

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. TRL — CURRENT AND PATH TO 9
QUESTION: "What is your current Technology Readiness Level using the standard NATO/NASA
definition? What specific demonstration have you completed that justifies that TRL?
What are the milestones and capital requirements to reach TRL 9 from where you are today?
And what is your realistic timeline for each TRL step?"
STRONG ANSWER: States a specific TRL with the specific evidence that defines it (e.g.,
"TRL 6 — we demonstrated the full system in [specific operational environment] in
[month, year] with [specific customer]"); has a documented TRL roadmap with capital
requirements per stage and realistic timelines based on prior TRL progression speed.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're around TRL 6–7"; describes a laboratory demonstration as TRL 7;
cannot separate the TRL roadmap from the fundraising narrative; the timeline to
commercial revenue is described as "12–18 months" from TRL 6.
REVEALS: Whether the team has a realistic understanding of how far they are from commercial
deployment and whether the financial model is built on achievable milestones.

02. IP OWNERSHIP — COMPANY OR UNIVERSITY
QUESTION: "Who legally owns the core patents? Is it the company, a university, a
research council, or a combination? If it is a university, what are the terms of the
licence — field of use, sub-licensing rights, revenue share, termination conditions?
Has a specialist IP solicitor reviewed the IP assignment or licence agreement on behalf
of the company (not the university)?"
STRONG ANSWER: Patents are assigned to the company; any university licence is exclusive,
broad field of use, irrevocable, and fully assigned at the company's option; a specialist
IP firm (not the university's in-house counsel) has reviewed and advised.
WEAK ANSWER: "The IP is licensed from the university — we have exclusive rights";
cannot confirm whether the licence is irrevocable; "the university is supportive";
the founding agreement was negotiated without separate IP counsel.
REVEALS: Whether the company's core commercial asset is actually owned by the company
or is a licence that limits commercial freedom at exit.

03. MANUFACTURING PARTNER AND SCALE-UP PLAN
QUESTION: "Who will manufacture your product at commercial scale? Have you signed a
manufacturing agreement or heads of terms with a contract manufacturer? What is your
target BoM cost at 1,000 units? At 10,000 units? What is the capital requirement to
achieve your first production run of 1,000 units and what yield do you expect?"
STRONG ANSWER: Named contract manufacturer with existing relationship; heads of terms
or MOU in place; specific BoM cost targets with a documented learning curve projection;
yield expectations based on actual pilot production data.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're evaluating several manufacturers"; "manufacturing is the next step
after we prove TRL 8"; cannot state a BoM cost target; yield projections are theoretical.
REVEALS: Whether the company has confronted the manufacturing challenge seriously or
is treating it as a problem to solve later — which is the Manufacturing Scale-Up Wall
failure in formation.

04. CAPITAL REQUIREMENT TO FIRST COMMERCIAL REVENUE
QUESTION: "How much total capital do you need from today to reach your first £1M of
commercial revenue? How is that capital allocated across: continued R&D, manufacturing
setup, regulatory approval, sales and business development, and working capital? What
are the three biggest risks that could increase the capital requirement beyond this figure?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific total capital requirement with allocation breakdown; has modelled
the three biggest risk scenarios with capital impact; total requirement is consistent
with sub-category benchmarks in Section B of this library.
WEAK ANSWER: "We need £5M to reach commercial launch" without allocation detail; does
not address risks that could inflate the requirement; total requirement is significantly
below the sub-category benchmark (suggests underestimation rather than efficiency).
REVEALS: Whether the team has done a rigorous capital plan or has reverse-engineered a
fundraise target from what they think is raisable.

05. LEAD SCIENTIST EQUITY AND VESTING
QUESTION: "What equity does the lead scientist hold? Is it on a standard 4-year vesting
schedule with a 1-year cliff? Is the lead scientist a full-time employee of the company?
What is their employment contract term? If they left the company tomorrow, what would
you lose that cannot be recovered or replaced within 12 months?"
STRONG ANSWER: Lead scientist holds 10–40% equity on a standard vesting schedule; is
a full-time employee; the company has a documented knowledge transfer and laboratory
process documentation programme so that core methods are institutionalised, not only
in the scientist's head.
WEAK ANSWER: Lead scientist is a "part-time technical advisor"; equity is minimal (below
5%) because they joined late; no standard vesting; "they're very committed to the project."
REVEALS: Whether the company's most critical asset — the person who made the scientific
breakthrough — is structurally committed to the company's success or could walk away
with their knowledge and leave the company technically paralysed.

06. GOVERNMENT GRANT DEPENDENCY AND BRIDGE STRATEGY
QUESTION: "What percentage of your total funding to date has come from government grants?
What is your next planned grant application and when do you expect to hear? If that grant
is rejected, what is your plan B? At what point in your development plan do you expect
private institutional capital to replace grant capital as the primary funding source?"
STRONG ANSWER: Grants are a minority of total funding; has a private institutional investor
interested in co-funding the next stage; has a specific grant-rejection contingency plan;
can identify the TRL milestone at which the technology will be commercially compelling
to institutional investors without requiring grant co-funding.
WEAK ANSWER: Grants are 100% of funding to date; "we'll apply for the next Innovate UK
round"; no private investor engagement; "grants are the most efficient capital for our stage."
REVEALS: Whether the company is building a venture business or a research programme with
a company structure — and whether the founding team understands the distinction.

07. REGULATORY APPROVAL PATHWAY AND TIMELINE
QUESTION: "What regulatory approval does your product require before first commercial
sale? Who is your regulatory counsel? Have you had a pre-submission meeting with the
relevant regulator? What is the regulator's stated timeline, and what happens to your
business plan if it takes twice as long as projected?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names the specific regulatory pathway; has regulatory counsel with
experience in the specific product class; has had a formal pre-submission meeting with
the regulator and can cite the outcome; financial model includes a regulatory delay
scenario.
WEAK ANSWER: "We've spoken informally to the regulator"; "we believe we're Class I
so the timeline is short"; regulatory approval is not on the critical path of the
financial model.
REVEALS: Whether regulatory risk has been properly modelled or has been discounted
because the founding team does not want to acknowledge the timeline it implies.

08. BILL OF MATERIALS COST AND LEARNING CURVE
QUESTION: "What is your current BoM cost per unit? What is your target BoM cost at
1,000 units and at 10,000 units? What is the primary cost driver and when does it
start declining? Have you seen any BoM cost reduction from your first unit to your
most recent production batch?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific BoM cost at current scale and projected at target volumes;
primary cost driver identified with a credible reduction pathway (volume discounts on
components, process improvement, design simplification); evidence of cost decline in
production to date.
WEAK ANSWER: "BoM cost will improve significantly with scale" without specific numbers;
no evidence of cost decline in pilot production; the primary cost driver is a commodity
material with no company-controlled learning curve.
REVEALS: Whether the gross margin projection at commercial scale is grounded in actual
cost data or is a speculative model that has never been validated against real production costs.

09. CUSTOMER COMMITTED TO PAYING FOR COMMERCIAL PRODUCT
QUESTION: "Is there any customer who has paid you money — or signed a letter of intent
committing to purchase at a specified commercial price — for your product? Who is it?
What is the value? What milestone or condition triggers the commercial purchase? And
what does your most engaged potential customer say when you ask them what they would
pay today for the commercial product?"
STRONG ANSWER: At least one named customer who has either paid for a pilot at commercial-
intent pricing or has signed an LOI with a specified commercial commitment; can describe
the specific trigger conditions and the customer's stated rationale for the commitment.
WEAK ANSWER: "Multiple customers are very excited about the technology"; "we have verbal
commitments"; "once we reach TRL 8 we're confident customers will pay"; cannot name a
customer who has paid any money for any form of the product.
REVEALS: Whether the commercial demand for the technology is real and validated or is
the founding team's extrapolation from positive research conversations.

10. FREEDOM TO OPERATE ASSESSMENT
QUESTION: "Has a freedom-to-operate (FTO) analysis been conducted by an independent
patent attorney? When was it last updated? Were any blocking patents identified? If so,
what is your strategy to design around them or licence them? And have you identified all
third-party IP that your manufacturing process relies on?"
STRONG ANSWER: FTO analysis conducted within the last 18 months by an independent
patent attorney; no blocking patents identified, or specific design-arounds implemented
for identified risks; manufacturing process IP is clean or licensed.
WEAK ANSWER: "We've reviewed the patent landscape ourselves"; "our patents are
differentiated enough that we don't have FTO concerns"; FTO has not been conducted.
REVEALS: Whether the company's commercial activities could be blocked by a third-party
patent holder — which is a common and existential risk in deep tech, especially in
crowded IP landscapes like semiconductors, biotech, and advanced materials.

11. DEFENCE OR GOVERNMENT CONTRACT STATUS
QUESTION: "Have you received any government or defence contracts — SBRI, MOD DSTL,
UK Space Agency, Defence and Security Accelerator? What is the value, duration, and
deliverable of each contract? And do any of those contracts include restrictions on
who you can sell to or licence to — including foreign government or commercial restrictions?"
STRONG ANSWER: Describes specific government contracts with values and deliverables;
confirms whether contracts include any IP licensing restrictions, security classifications,
or export restrictions on the resulting technology; understands the implications of
government IP ownership clauses in contract R&D.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're in discussions with MOD"; "we've applied to DSTL but haven't heard";
"the government has been supportive of what we're doing"; does not address whether
contract terms restrict commercial exploitation.
REVEALS: Whether government validation is real (contracted and paid) or aspirational —
and whether government contracts may inadvertently restrict future commercial freedom.

12. INTERNATIONAL EXPANSION CONSTRAINTS — ITAR, EXPORT CONTROLS
QUESTION: "Have you assessed whether your technology is subject to UK export controls
or US ITAR? Have you had any engagement with ECJU (Export Control Joint Unit) or the US
State Department? Does your technology have dual-use potential — applications in both
civilian and military contexts? Are there any restrictions on who can be employed by or
invest in the company based on technology classification?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has taken specialist export control legal advice; knows whether the
technology is listed on the UK Military List, Dual-Use List, or US Commerce Control List;
understands the restrictions on foreign nationals accessing controlled technology;
has a compliance programme in place if export controls apply.
WEAK ANSWER: "We don't have any defence applications so ITAR doesn't apply"; "we'll
deal with export controls when we try to sell overseas"; has not taken specialist advice.
REVEALS: Whether the company has thought carefully about the international commercial
restrictions that apply to its specific technology — export control failures can result
in criminal prosecution, and the assumptions about who can invest in or acquire the
company may be wrong.

UK EXPORT CONTROLS AND DUAL-USE TECHNOLOGY:

Export Control Joint Unit (ECJU):
  ECJU administers UK export controls under the Export Control Order 2008 and the
  UK's implementation of international export control regimes (Wassenaar Arrangement,
  Australia Group, Nuclear Suppliers Group, Missile Technology Control Regime).
  Key lists:
  — UK Military List: weapons systems, military electronics, military vehicles, controlled
    software for military applications.
  — UK Dual-Use List: technologies with both civilian and military applications —
    including advanced materials (certain composites), semiconductors above specified
    performance thresholds, space launch technology, advanced sensors, biotech
    (certain micro-organisms and toxins).
  Export licence requirement: any transfer of controlled technology — including
    intangible transfer (email, cloud access, verbal briefing) to a non-UK person —
    may require an export licence from ECJU.
  Alex flag: a deep tech company with a technology on the Dual-Use List must have an
    ECJU compliance programme. A company that has not assessed its export control
    classification is not compliant with UK law, regardless of whether it has yet made
    any international transfers.

ITAR — INTERNATIONAL TRAFFIC IN ARMS REGULATIONS (US):

  ITAR is a US regulation that controls the export and import of defence-related articles
  and services. It has extraterritorial reach: a UK company using US-origin defence
  technology — including technology developed in the US, by US persons, or with US
  government funding — is subject to ITAR.
  ITAR applies to: space technology (launch vehicles, satellites), advanced semiconductors
    for defence applications, military-grade sensors, cryptography for defence use,
    directed energy technologies, autonomous weapons systems.
  ITAR Implications for UK deep tech companies:
  — A UK company with ITAR-controlled technology cannot employ foreign nationals from
    ITAR-restricted countries without a US State Department licence.
  — A foreign company cannot acquire a UK company with ITAR-controlled technology
    without US State Department approval.
  — Non-compliance carries criminal penalties under US law.
  Alex flag: any UK deep tech company with: (a) US investors, (b) US government contracts
    or subcontracts, (c) US-origin technology components, or (d) technology that has
    both civilian and military applications should have taken specialist ITAR counsel
    advice. Ignorance of ITAR applicability is not a defence.

US SEMICONDUCTOR EXPORT CONTROLS (US CHIPS ACT AND COMMERCE DEPARTMENT):

  US Commerce Department BIS (Bureau of Industry and Security) expanded semiconductor
  export controls in October 2022 and October 2023, restricting:
  — Advanced chips (above specified logic density and memory bandwidth thresholds)
    to China, including Huawei and affiliated entities.
  — EDA (Electronic Design Automation) software exports to China.
  — Advanced chip manufacturing equipment exports to China.
  Implications for UK semiconductor companies:
  — UK fabless design companies that use US EDA tools (Cadence, Synopsys — both US) are
    subject to US export controls when designing chips for Chinese customers.
  — UK companies that license US semiconductor IP (ARM architecture, RISC-V implementations
    from US companies) may be restricted in selling to certain Chinese entities.
  Alex flag: a UK semiconductor company with Chinese customers, investors, or manufacturing
    partners (including SMIC) may be subject to US BIS export controls. Specialist US
    export control counsel is required — not optional.

NATIONAL SECURITY AND INVESTMENT (NSI) ACT 2021 — MANDATORY NOTIFICATION:

  The NSI Act 2021 is the UK's primary foreign investment screening regime. It requires
  mandatory notification to the Investment Security Unit (ISU) before completing certain
  acquisitions in 17 sensitive sectors.
  MANDATORY notification triggers (acquisition of 25%+ of shares or voting rights in
  a qualifying entity, or acquisition of material influence over its policy):
  1. Advanced Materials
  2. Advanced Robotics
  3. Artificial Intelligence
  4. Civil Nuclear
  5. Communications
  6. Computing Hardware
  7. Critical Suppliers to Government
  8. Cryptographic Authentication
  9. Data Infrastructure
  10. Defence
  11. Energy
  12. Military and Dual-Use
  13. Quantum Technologies
  14. Satellite and Space Technologies
  15. Suppliers to the Emergency Services
  16. Synthetic Biology
  17. Transport
  Timeline: notification must be made before transaction completion. ISU has 30 working
    days to decide (extendable to 45 days for complex cases).
  Outcome options: clear unconditionally | clear with conditions | block the transaction.
  Alex flag: ANY deep tech company in one of the 17 sectors is subject to NSI Act
    mandatory notification on acquisition. This means: (a) any foreign acquirer must
    factor ISU review into transaction timeline (add 3–6 months); (b) acquirers from
    countries designated as higher-risk may face conditions or blocking; (c) Chinese
    and Russian acquirers should be treated as high-risk for NSI Act purposes.
    A deep tech company that is acquired without mandatory NSI notification is subject
    to a transaction being unwound by the UK government.

SPACE REGULATION — UK SPACE INDUSTRY ACT 2018:

  The UK Space Industry Act 2018 established a domestic UK launch licensing regime,
  replacing reliance on the US FAA for UK-based launches.
  Civil Aviation Authority (CAA): regulates spaceflight activities in the UK.
  Licence types: orbital launch licence, sub-orbital launch licence, spaceport operator
    licence, range control services licence.
  Application timeline: 12–18 months for an initial orbital launch licence.
  Alex flag: any space company planning to launch from UK soil (SaxaVord Spaceport in
    Shetland, Spaceport Cornwall) must hold a CAA licence. A company that has announced
    UK launch dates without a licence in place is presenting a timeline that is not credible.

BIOTECH / GMO REGULATION — UK POST-BREXIT DIVERGENCE:

  Post-Brexit, the UK has diverged from EU GMO regulation. The Genetic Technology
  (Precision Breeding) Act 2023 (England) permits the commercial use of precision bred
  organisms (including gene-edited plants and animals) without the strict authorisation
  requirements that apply to GMOs under EU law.
  EU position: gene-edited organisms are still classified as GMOs under EU law (pending
    revision under the EU New Genomic Techniques regulation, in progress as of 2026).
  Implication for UK biotech companies: a precision-bred crop or animal product approved
    in England may not be permitted to be sold in the EU without additional authorisation.
    A UK synthetic biology company selling to EU customers must comply with EU GMO rules.
  Alex flag: UK biotech companies selling into EU markets must maintain dual regulatory
    compliance — UK Precision Breeding Act and EU GMO Directive — until EU legislation
    is updated. A company that assumes UK approval is sufficient for EU sales is
    operating on an incorrect legal assumption.

INNOVATE UK AND ARIA — GRANT FRAMEWORK:

  Innovate UK (part of UK Research and Innovation, UKRI) is the primary source of
  non-dilutive early-stage funding for UK deep tech companies. Key programmes:
  — Innovate UK Smart: competitive open grants of £25K–£500K for single companies.
  — Innovate UK KTP (Knowledge Transfer Partnership): collaborative grants with universities.
  — Catapult Programmes: sector-specific technology and innovation centres (High Value
    Manufacturing Catapult, Connected Places Catapult, Digital Catapult) providing
    facilities and expertise access.
  ARIA (Advanced Research and Invention Agency):
    Established in 2022 with a £800M budget over 5 years. Funds high-risk, high-reward
    science with minimal bureaucratic constraints. Modelled on DARPA.
    Key difference from Innovate UK: ARIA explicitly funds moonshots with >50% failure
    probability. Winning an ARIA programme grant is strong scientific validation but
    does not imply commercial timeline certainty.
  IP ownership clauses:
    Innovate UK grant conditions include IP ownership provisions — the company generally
    retains IP but may have obligations to exploit it for UK benefit and to acknowledge
    UKRI funding in publications and commercial activities.
    ARIA grant IP conditions may vary by programme — review individual programme terms.
  Alex flag: confirm whether any government grant conditions restrict IP assignment,
    licensing to foreign entities, or require commercialisation within a specific
    geographic or sector scope.

AI SAFETY REGULATION — FRONTIER AI:

  For deep tech companies developing frontier AI systems (large language models, advanced
  AI training systems, or AI with significant dual-use or safety implications):
  UK AI Safety Institute (AISI): established in 2023, responsible for frontier AI safety
    evaluation. Voluntary framework as of 2026 but moving towards mandatory notification.
  EU AI Act (in force 2024): general-purpose AI models with computational training above
    10^25 FLOPs are subject to systemic risk provisions — mandatory adversarial testing,
    incident reporting, and model capability disclosure.
  International norms: the Bletchley Declaration (November 2023) established an
    international framework for frontier AI safety; UK, US, EU, and China are signatories.
  Alex flag: a UK deep tech software company developing a frontier AI system must be
    aware of: (a) AISI voluntary reporting expectations; (b) EU AI Act requirements for
    any EU deployment; (c) export control implications for advanced AI models (US BIS
    has signalled interest in AI model export controls).
