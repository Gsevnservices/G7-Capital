ALEX — RED FLAG LIBRARY
CATEGORY 7: DEAL STRUCTURE RED FLAGS
Version 1.0. Built: April 2026.
10 flags. Apply mechanically to every deal submission.
These flags are observable from a submission without a meeting.
Two analysts applying these rules reach the same conclusion.
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION RULE:
For every deal submission, Alex checks all 10 flags before completing
the screening note. Flags are not discretionary — if the signal is
present, the flag is raised. Flags are then weighed in aggregate.
One CRITICAL flag = automatic deep investigation required.
Two or more HIGH flags = note this in the Risk Assessment section.
Deal structure flags operate differently from business quality flags.
A structurally broken deal can contain an excellent business —
and a structurally clean deal can contain a mediocre one. These flags
are not signals about business quality. They are signals about whether
the investment terms, as presented, allow a new investor to achieve
returns proportionate to the risk they are taking. A deal that scores
well on every business dimension but fails two or more deal structure
flags is a deal that requires renegotiation, not a pass — but the
renegotiation must happen before capital is committed, not after.

FLAG DS01: VALUATION INCONSISTENT WITH METRICS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The asking valuation — expressed as a pre-money valuation relative to
the disclosed ARR — implies an ARR multiple that is materially above
the sector benchmark for the growth rate and gross margin presented.
Observable by dividing the pre-money valuation by the stated ARR to
derive the implied revenue multiple, then comparing that multiple to
the current market benchmark for the company's growth rate tier and
gross margin profile. A B2B SaaS company growing at 80% YoY with 70%
gross margin asking at 20× ARR in Q2 2026 is at the upper boundary
of market. The same metrics asking at 40× ARR is materially above
benchmark. The signal is the gap between the implied multiple and
what a comparable company at a comparable stage would achieve in
the current fundraising environment.

WHAT IT MEANS:
An above-market valuation is not automatically a reason to pass —
premium companies command premium multiples. The flag triggers
when the valuation is above market and the metrics do not justify
the premium. The failure modes are distinct: (1) the company will
struggle to raise its next round at a higher valuation because the
metrics do not support the step-up — creating a flat round or a
down round that triggers anti-dilution provisions and damages
investor returns; (2) the current investor enters at a price that
compresses the return multiple to below the fund's threshold — a
5× gross return target at entry requires at least a 5× multiple
on exit before fees, and entry at an inflated valuation makes this
mathematically difficult at any realistic exit scenario; (3) the
company has anchored its self-worth to a valuation that the
current investors' expectation of 3–5 years of compounding cannot
support, creating a misalignment between founder expectations and
investor return requirements that surfaces acutely at exit. The
2021–2022 vintage of deals at 50–100× ARR multiples is the
reference class: the majority of those companies either failed
to raise a subsequent round or raised at a significant down round,
with the downstream governance and incentive consequences documented
in the Governance and Traction red flag libraries.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The implied ARR multiple is more than 3× the current sector benchmark
for the company's growth rate AND the company's ARR is below £2M —
early-stage companies with small ARR bases have high valuation
uncertainty and a 3× premium over sector benchmark at this size
implies either an unsupported narrative premium or a founder
expectation that is incompatible with institutional investment terms.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has a documented reason for the premium: a signed
LOI or term sheet from a strategic acquirer at a premium valuation,
a proprietary dataset or network effect that creates genuine scarcity
value, or a competitive process with multiple term sheets at the
same price — external validation replaces analyst judgement on
what the market will pay.
(b) The ARR multiple is above benchmark but the company's growth
rate is in the top quartile for its stage — premium growth justifies
a premium multiple up to approximately 1.5× the sector benchmark.
(c) The company is willing to restructure the deal with a lower
pre-money valuation and warrants or milestone-based step-ups that
tie the premium to performance — converting a valuation dispute
into an alignment mechanism.

VERIFICATION:
Calculate: pre-money valuation ÷ ARR = implied multiple. Compare
to current sector benchmark for the company's growth rate tier:
growth >150% YoY: 15–25× ARR fair value range; growth 80–150%:
8–15×; growth 40–80%: 5–8×; growth <40%: 2–5×. These are
indicative ranges for 2026 — apply sector adjustments for
gross margin and market size. Ask: "How did you arrive at your
valuation? What comparable transactions or multiples did you use
as reference points?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When the implied ARR multiple is more than 1.5× the sector benchmark
for the stated growth rate, model the return at entry valuation under
three exit scenarios (1× sector average, 2× sector average, 3× sector
average) — if the fund return threshold is not achievable under the
2× scenario, the valuation is the deal-breaker, not the business.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG DS02: LIQUIDATION PREFERENCE ABOVE 1×
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Prior round investor documentation, term sheet history, or shareholder
agreement disclosures show any series holding a 2× or higher
liquidation preference on their invested capital. Observable from
any term sheet, shareholder agreement extract, or investor rights
summary disclosed in the data room or deal materials. Also detectable
when the cap table shows a liquidation preference amount that exceeds
the total capital raised by the series — i.e. a £5M Series A with
a £10M liquidation preference indicates a 2× or higher multiple.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A 2× liquidation preference means the series holding it receives
twice their invested capital before any other equity class receives
anything. At a 1× preference, a £5M invested at a £20M pre-money
valuation holds a £5M liquidation preference — reasonable protection.
At 2×, the same investment holds a £10M preference. The practical
consequence: in any exit below the sum of all liquidation preferences
across all series, common shareholders receive zero. With multiple
series each holding 2× preferences, the threshold below which common
shareholders receive zero becomes very high — often exceeding the
realistic exit range for the business. The incentive consequences
are direct: employees holding common stock or options see their
equity as worthless, leave for companies where their equity has
value, and the company loses the team it needs to achieve the exit
that would clear the preference stack. The above-market liquidation
preference is also a signal about the circumstances of a prior round:
2× preferences are typically negotiated when founders were in a weak
position — low runway, struggling metrics, a failed prior process —
and accepted harsh terms to stay alive. The deal history that produced
the 2× preference is therefore itself a red flag about a prior
operating crisis that may not be disclosed in the current materials.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The sum of all liquidation preferences across all series exceeds
the pre-money valuation of the current round — meaning that in a
sale at the current round's valuation, existing preference holders
would take 100% of the proceeds before the current round investor
receives anything. This is a structural condition that makes the
deal uninvestable at the proposed terms.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The current round includes a renegotiation of prior liquidation
preferences as a condition of the round — converting 2× preferences
to 1× non-participating, with existing investors agreeing to this
restructuring in the term sheet.
(b) The 2× preference is held by a single prior investor at a very
small investment amount (under £500K) where the absolute preference
amount does not materially affect the waterfall at any plausible
exit scenario.
(c) The company's exit range — based on current metrics and sector
comparables — comfortably exceeds the sum of all liquidation
preferences, such that the preference stack is not binding in
any realistic outcome.

VERIFICATION:
Request the complete liquidation waterfall model showing each series,
the preference amount, the preference multiple, whether the preference
is participating or non-participating, and the exit value at which
common shareholders first receive proceeds. Calculate: sum of all
liquidation preferences across all series ÷ pre-money valuation
of the current round = preference coverage ratio. Ask: "Can you
provide a full waterfall analysis showing proceeds by share class
at your target exit valuation?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When any prior round holds a 2× or higher liquidation preference,
model the waterfall at the expected exit range before advancing —
preference stacks that consume the entire exit proceed in the
realistic outcome range make the deal uninvestable for any new
investor whose return depends on exit proceeds above the preference.

FLAG DS03: FULL RATCHET ANTI-DILUTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The shareholder agreement, term sheet, or investor rights summary
for any prior round contains a full ratchet anti-dilution provision —
meaning that in the event of a down round, the prior series is
re-priced to the new lower price on a share-for-share basis, without
the weighted-average adjustment that is standard market practice.
Observable from any investor rights agreement extract or term sheet
summary that uses the language "full ratchet" explicitly, or from
provisions stating that prior investors receive additional shares
at the new round price to make them whole on a per-share basis
rather than a weighted-average basis.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Full ratchet anti-dilution is the most founder-punishing and
co-investor-punishing anti-dilution mechanism available in a term
sheet. The mechanics: a company raises a Series A at £1 per share,
then raises a Series B down round at £0.50 per share. Under broad-
based weighted-average anti-dilution — the market standard — the
Series A conversion price adjusts modestly, reflecting the dilution
proportionately to the amount raised in the down round. Under full
ratchet, the entire Series A converts to shares priced at £0.50 —
the Series A investor effectively doubles their share count at the
expense of every other shareholder. In a company with 10 million
shares and a full ratchet Series A of 2 million shares at £1, a
down round at £0.50 converts the Series A to 4 million shares —
an additional 2 million shares materialise, diluting founders,
employees, and any other investors who do not hold full ratchet
protections. The consequence is reliably catastrophic: the dilution
to common holders after the full ratchet triggers makes option
grants worthless, founders' equity negligible, and the company
effectively impossible to finance further because new investors
will not accept the terms that created the situation in the first place.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A prior round holds full ratchet anti-dilution provisions AND the
company's current metrics suggest a down round is a plausible
outcome within the next 12–18 months — i.e. the current round
valuation is above sector benchmark for the current metrics,
the growth rate is decelerating, and the runway post-current-round
is under 18 months. In this scenario the full ratchet is not
a latent risk but an imminent one.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The full ratchet provision is held by an investor who has
already agreed in writing to convert to broad-based weighted-average
anti-dilution as a condition of the current round — and this
conversion is documented in the current term sheet.
(b) The company's metrics are strong enough that a down round
is genuinely implausible within a 5-year horizon — strong NRR,
declining burn multiple, clear path to profitability — and the
full ratchet is a legacy term from an earlier, weaker period
that is very unlikely to ever be triggered.
(c) The full ratchet is held by an investor with a very small
share count (under 1% fully diluted) where even a full ratchet
trigger produces a dilution amount that is not material to the
overall cap table.

VERIFICATION:
Request: all existing shareholder agreements and investor rights
agreements — specifically the anti-dilution provisions for each
series. Confirm whether the mechanism is (a) no anti-dilution,
(b) ratchet (full), (c) weighted-average (narrow-based),
or (d) weighted-average (broad-based). Standard market practice
is (d). Any series using (b) requires immediate escalation.
Ask: "Do any prior rounds contain full ratchet anti-dilution
provisions? If so, has the existing investor agreed to convert
this to weighted-average as part of the current round?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When any prior round contains full ratchet anti-dilution provisions,
request the full investor rights agreement and model the dilution
impact of a 30% down round on all other shareholders before advancing —
full ratchet provisions that have not been removed before a difficult
fundraising environment are the primary reason otherwise-recoverable
companies become uninvestable.

FLAG DS04: ROUND NOT FULLY COMMITTED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The current round has no confirmed lead investor — either the deck
describes the round as "open" with no committed lead, or the founder
confirms in the submission that they are raising without a lead and
seeking commitments from multiple investors simultaneously. Also
detectable when the founder's language implies they are using one
investor's interest to attract another: "We're in conversations with
[named firm] and looking to fill the remaining allocation" without
evidence of a firm commitment from [named firm]. Observable from
any description of the current round structure in the deck, data room,
or submission that does not name a lead investor with a confirmed
commitment.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A round without a committed lead is a round that no institutional
investor has yet chosen to own. The implications are specific:
(1) no lead means no one has done sufficient diligence to commit
capital — which either means the company has not yet been through
serious diligence or that it has and the lead investor has not
emerged, both of which are informative; (2) a round raised from
many small commitments without a lead tends to produce a fragmented
cap table of investors who each hold too little to be actively helpful
but collectively have enough votes to be disruptive; (3) a company
that is using one investor's soft interest to attract others is
a company that is managing its process through social proof rather
than through the quality of the business — which is a founder
behaviour signal as much as a deal structure signal; (4) first-close
dynamics: a round that does not close quickly after the first
commitment often does not close at all. The absence of a lead
is the most reliable leading indicator of a round that will take
longer than the company's runway allows.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company is raising a Series A or larger round (over £3M) and
has been in market for more than 6 months with no lead investor
confirmed. A round that has been open for 6 months at Series A
level without producing a lead has been rejected by a large fraction
of the relevant investor universe — the absence of a lead is not
a search problem but an evidence problem.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is raising a seed or pre-seed round where lead
investors are less common and syndicates of smaller cheques are
the standard market structure.
(b) The company has a confirmed lead at the term sheet stage
but has not yet announced it publicly — confidentiality around
lead investor name is normal until legal documents are signed.
(c) The firm reviewing the deal is itself the proposed lead —
in which case the absence of a prior lead is the deal structure,
not a red flag.

VERIFICATION:
Ask directly: "Who is the lead investor in this round? Do you
have a signed term sheet or commitment letter? How long have you
been raising and how much is committed to date?" Request evidence
of the lead commitment — a countersigned term sheet or a commitment
letter from the lead is the minimum standard. Soft interest,
verbal commitments, and "in advanced conversations" are not
confirmed commitments.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a round is presented without a named lead investor or confirmed
commitment, ask directly for the lead name and commitment evidence
before investing time in deep diligence — rounds without committed
leads fail to close at a materially higher rate than led rounds,
and the diligence cost of a failed close is not recoverable.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG DS05: INSIDER-LED ROUND WITHOUT EXTERNAL VALIDATION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The current round is entirely composed of existing investors —
existing Series A investors leading a Series B, existing angels
filling the entire seed extension — with no new external investor
participating. Observable when the cap table post-round shows
no new investor names, or when the founder confirms in the
submission that all committed capital is from prior investors
with no new external participation at any size. Also observable
when the deck describes the round as a "pro-rata exercise" by
existing investors without mentioning any new external capital.

WHAT IT MEANS:
An insider-led round with no external validation has two distinct
readings and the difference matters: (1) the company has attempted
to raise from external investors, failed to attract new capital,
and existing investors are bridging to avoid writing off their
prior investment — this is the adverse interpretation and is the
more common one; (2) the company's existing investors are so
convinced of the opportunity that they are exercising full pro-rata
rights and deliberately pre-empting the process before external
investors can participate — this is the positive interpretation
and is less common. The flag's purpose is to determine which
scenario is operative. The signal is informative precisely because
existing investors have the most information about the company —
they have seen board meetings, monthly metrics, and management
in action. When existing investors are willing to put in more money,
that is a positive signal. When external investors who have seen
the same data are not willing to participate, that is a negative
signal. The conflict between these two signals is what makes the
flag worth investigating.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has been running an external fundraising process for
more than 4 months, has met with more than 20 institutional investors,
and has received no external term sheet — and existing investors
are bridging the gap. This pattern — broad process, no external
lead, existing investors preserving optionality — is the clearest
signal that the external market has assessed the company and declined.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Existing investors are exercising pro-rata rights in a
competitive round where external investors were offered allocation
but the round was oversubscribed by existing investors — pro-rata
exercise in a hot round is a positive signal, not a negative one.
(b) The company is deliberately avoiding a broad external process
to maintain confidentiality around a strategic initiative or M&A
process — and external capital was available but not sought.
(c) The firm reviewing the deal is being invited as the new
external investor for an allocation that existing investors have
not taken — the insider-led dynamic is the context, not the
structure being offered.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "Have you run an external fundraising process for this round?
How many external investors did you approach and what was the outcome?
Is the current round open to external investors or is it exclusively
for existing investors exercising pro-rata?" The specificity and
directness of the response — how many investors, what feedback —
is more informative than the answer itself.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the current round is entirely insider-led with no external
participation, ask directly how many external investors were
approached and what their feedback was — the answer distinguishes
between a genuinely pre-emptive round (positive signal) and a
round that external investors have already declined (negative
signal requiring a full independent assessment of why).

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG DS06: BRIDGE ROUND MASQUERADING AS SERIES ROUND
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The round is labelled "Series A" or "Series B" but the capital
raised is insufficient to reach the next fundable milestone within
the timeline implied by the financial model. Detectable by comparing
the post-round cash runway to the time required to achieve the
milestone that would justify the next round. Observable when:
(1) the round size is below £3M for a "Series A" label; (2) the
runway post-round is under 18 months when the financial model
implies the next meaningful milestone requires 24 or more months
to achieve; (3) the financial model shows a second raise required
within 9 months of closing the current round under the base case
scenario. Also observable when the use of proceeds is described
as "extending runway" rather than "achieving X milestone."

WHAT IT MEANS:
A bridge disguised as a Series round is structurally dangerous for
a new investor for three compounding reasons: (1) the investor
enters at a Series A valuation with Series B risk — they are
paying for a company that has not yet achieved Series A milestones,
because if it had, the round would be large enough to reach the
next milestone; (2) the company will be back in market within 9
months, raising from a position of urgency and without having achieved
the milestones the current round was supposed to fund — which means
the next round will be at equal or lower valuation (a flat or down
round), triggering anti-dilution provisions and damaging the current
investor's entry economics; (3) the 9-month fundraise clock begins
immediately after closing — the founders spend the first 6 months
post-close trying to execute against the milestone plan and the
last 3 months back in fundraising mode, leaving the company in a
permanent capital-raising cycle that prevents focused execution.
The labelling also matters: founders who present a bridge as a
Series round are either unaware that the capital is insufficient
(worrying) or aware and choosing not to disclose it (disqualifying).

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The current round will provide less than 12 months of runway at
the stated burn rate AND the company's most recent quarter showed
increasing rather than decreasing burn — a company with less than
12 months of runway on an increasing burn trajectory is raising
in survival mode, not growth mode, and the current round does not
change the underlying trajectory.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The round is explicitly structured as a bridge with full
disclosure — the founder is raising a £1.5M bridge to reach a
specific milestone (e.g. signing a named customer or reaching
£500K MRR) that will unlock a £5M Series A on defined terms,
and the bridge terms reflect this — lower valuation, warrants,
conversion discount to the Series A. Transparency about the
bridge nature converts a deceptive structure into a normal
early-stage financing instrument.
(b) The company has a committed Series A investor who has agreed
to lead the next round contingent on the milestone, making the
bridge de-risked from a financing perspective even if the
milestone itself remains uncertain.

VERIFICATION:
Calculate: post-close cash ÷ current monthly burn = months of
runway. Then ask: "What specific milestone does this round fund
you to? When do you expect to begin raising the next round?
What does the financial model show as the break-even date under
the base case?" The answers triangulate whether the round is
genuinely milestone-funded or whether the company will be
raising again before it can demonstrate the results this round
was supposed to produce.

PATTERN LESSON:
When post-round runway is under 18 months and the financial model
implies the next meaningful milestone requires more time than that,
model the company's capital needs through to the following round
before committing — a bridge-as-Series-round is frequently the
last institutional funding a company receives before a restructuring,
because the investors who funded the bridge are the only ones with
the relationship and the basis to fund the follow-on.

FLAG DS07: DRAG-ALONG RIGHTS ABSENT OR WEAK
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The shareholder agreement either does not contain a drag-along
provision, or contains a drag-along that requires the consent of
minority shareholders representing more than 10% of the company
to exercise — giving a small group of shareholders effective veto
power over an exit that the board, the founders, and the majority
of investors have approved. Observable from any shareholder agreement
extract, term sheet, or investor rights summary that describes the
drag-along mechanism. Also observable from absence: a shareholder
agreement disclosed in the data room that does not mention drag-along
rights is a de facto absence of the provision.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Drag-along rights allow a defined majority of shareholders to compel
all other shareholders to accept an acquisition on the same terms.
Without adequate drag-along provisions, any shareholder — including
a former employee with 0.5% in unvested options, a departed
co-founder, or a small angel with 2% — can block an acquisition
by refusing to sign the sale agreement. The blocking scenario is
not theoretical: it occurs in almost every contested exit where
the consideration is not uniformly attractive to all shareholder
classes. The specific scenarios where absent or weak drag-along
becomes a live issue: (1) a strategic acquirer offers a price that
is excellent for preference holders but leaves common shareholders
at or near zero — common holders have no economic incentive to sign
and may hold out for a renegotiation; (2) a departed co-founder
who left on bad terms uses their signature as leverage to extract
a side payment or enhanced terms; (3) a former employee who was
improperly managed out attempts to use their unsigned status to
revisit their departure terms. Each of these scenarios adds months
to an exit process and in some cases has caused acquirers to walk.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has a fragmented early shareholder base with more than
20 individual shareholders and no drag-along provision at all —
in this structure, securing unanimous shareholder consent for an
exit requires contacting and obtaining signatures from all shareholders,
some of whom may be unreachable, deceased, or uncooperative. An
exit in this structure is a legal project, not a commercial one.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The shareholder agreement contains a drag-along that requires
a simple majority of shareholders by value (not by count), with
a threshold of 50% or higher — this is sufficient to prevent
minority blocking in most scenarios.
(b) The company's shareholder base is small (under 10 shareholders)
and all shareholders are professional investors or active founders
who have signed a shareholders' agreement that includes standard
exit cooperation provisions.
(c) The company is proposing to add a drag-along provision as
part of the current round's shareholder agreement update — and
existing investors have agreed to the updated terms.

VERIFICATION:
Request the shareholders' agreement and identify the drag-along
provision: (1) what percentage threshold triggers the drag-along;
(2) which share classes must consent; (3) whether the drag-along
extends to option holders and warrant holders; (4) whether there
are carve-outs for any shareholders who are not subject to drag.
Ask: "If the board and majority investors agreed to an acquisition,
could any minority shareholder block the sale? Are there shareholders
who have not signed the current shareholders' agreement?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When the shareholder agreement contains no drag-along or a drag-along
with a threshold above simple majority by value, include an updated
drag-along provision as a condition of investment — the legal cost
of installing adequate drag-along at investment is trivial compared
to the cost of resolving a blocked exit under time pressure from
an acquirer.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG DS08: INFORMATION RIGHTS INADEQUATE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The shareholder agreement or investor rights agreement for prior
rounds does not include standard information rights — specifically:
(1) monthly or quarterly management accounts delivered within 30
days of period end; (2) annual audited accounts; (3) board observer
rights for investors above a defined threshold (typically £250K+);
(4) prompt notification of material events (a key customer loss,
a regulatory action, a founder departure). Observable from any
investor rights agreement or shareholders' agreement that is
disclosed in the data room, or from founders who confirm that
prior investors "don't require regular reporting."

WHAT IT MEANS:
Information rights are not a bureaucratic nicety — they are the
mechanism by which investors detect problems early enough to act.
A company whose prior investor agreements do not require regular
reporting has been operating without investor oversight, which
creates two compounding risks for a new investor: (1) the absence
of a reporting culture means the company has not been building
the financial discipline and management information systems that
institutional investors require — the new investor's first request
for monthly accounts may reveal that these accounts have never
been produced; (2) a company that has not been held to regular
reporting obligations may have accumulated problems — customer
churn, cash flow deterioration, team departures — that are known
to management and existing investors but have not been formally
disclosed because there was no mechanism requiring disclosure.
The absence of information rights in prior rounds also tells you
something about the quality of the prior investors: professional
institutional investors always include standard information rights.
If prior investors did not, they are likely angels or friends-and-
family investors who were not in a position to negotiate, which
means the company has not been professionally investor-governed.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A company at Series A or later with prior institutional investors
who hold no information rights — if an institutional investor with
a board seat did not require quarterly reporting as a condition of
investment, either the investor was not acting professionally or
the terms were renegotiated away, and both explanations are relevant
to how the company has been run.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The new investment round includes a full investor rights agreement
with standard information rights as a condition of investment —
the gap in prior rounds is remediated for the current round forward.
(b) The company has been providing voluntary regular reporting to
all investors despite the absence of a contractual obligation —
verifiable by requesting the last 6 months of investor updates
sent to all shareholders.
(c) Prior rounds were from friends and family investors who are not
actively engaged as investors — the information rights gap reflects
the investor composition, not the company's reporting practice.

VERIFICATION:
Request: all investor rights agreements and shareholders' agreements
showing information rights provisions. Confirm the frequency, format,
and timeline for required reporting. Ask: "What financial reporting
do you currently provide to existing investors and on what schedule?
Can you share the last 3 board packs or investor updates as examples?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When prior investor agreements contain no standard information rights
and the company cannot produce a track record of voluntary reporting,
include robust information rights as a non-negotiable condition of
investment and request the last 6 months of management accounts
during diligence — companies with no prior reporting obligations
frequently have management account quality issues that surface only
when a new investor requires them.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG DS09: PRO-RATA RIGHTS BLOCKING NEW INVESTOR OWNERSHIP
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
Prior investors hold pro-rata rights — the right to maintain their
percentage ownership in future rounds — that, if fully exercised,
would fill the majority of the current round's allocation before
any new investor can participate. Observable when the cap table
shows prior investors with pro-rata rights on a total stake that
represents more than 50% of the proposed round size, such that
full exercise leaves a new lead investor with a position too small
to justify leading. Also detectable when the founder confirms that
the current round "has limited room for new investors" or that
existing investors have "first right of allocation."

WHAT IT MEANS:
Pro-rata rights are standard and investor-friendly in isolation —
they protect investors from dilution and are a mark of a well-
negotiated prior round. The structural problem emerges specifically
for new investors when pro-rata rights are sufficiently concentrated
that they prevent a new lead investor from building a meaningful
ownership position. A new lead investor who can only achieve 3%
ownership (because existing investors exercise pro-rata on the
remaining 97%) has a position too small to justify the diligence
cost, the board seat, and the active management the investment
requires. The second-order problem: a company where existing investors
fully exercise pro-rata in every round does not have a new price
discovery mechanism — the valuation at each round is set between
the company and its existing investors, without an independent
external investor assessing and validating the price. Valuations
set without external validation tend to drift above market,
creating the conditions for Flag DS01.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Existing investors hold pro-rata rights on 80% or more of the
current round's proposed allocation AND they have confirmed they
intend to exercise those rights — leaving less than 20% of the
round available for new investors. A new lead investor cannot
lead a round where they receive less than 20% of the capital
deployed without the economics making the deal unworkable.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Existing investors with pro-rata rights are willing to waive
some or all of their pro-rata allocation to allow a new lead
investor to build a meaningful position — waiver letters from
existing investors confirming the available allocation are the
required evidence.
(b) The round is structured with a new investor allocation carved
out from the start — the term sheet defines a new investor tranche
that is not subject to pro-rata rights from prior investors.
(c) The new investor's target ownership is achievable even after
existing investors exercise full pro-rata — the deal is large
enough to accommodate both.

VERIFICATION:
Request: all investor rights agreements showing pro-rata provisions,
including the percentage and share class to which the right applies.
Calculate: sum of all pro-rata allocations ÷ proposed round size =
percentage of round consumed by existing investor pro-rata if
fully exercised. Ask: "If existing investors exercise their full
pro-rata rights, how much allocation is available for a new lead
investor? Are existing investors committed to exercising their
pro-rata in this round?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When existing pro-rata rights would consume more than 50% of the
proposed round, request a written waiver or allocation carve-out
from existing investors before advancing — a diligence process
completed without confirming available allocation frequently results
in a deal that cannot close because the new investor cannot achieve
the ownership position their investment thesis requires.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG DS10: CONVERTIBLE NOTE OVERHANG NOT MODELLED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Deal Structure
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The cap table presented to new investors does not include the
dilutive effect of convertible notes that will convert at or before
the current round — or the deck presents a pre-money valuation
and implied ownership stakes that are calculated before accounting
for the conversion of outstanding notes. Detectable when: (1) the
deal materials mention convertible notes, SAFEs, or ASAs outstanding
but the cap table does not show their post-conversion impact on the
fully diluted share count; (2) the implied ownership percentage
a new investor receives (calculated as investment amount ÷ (pre-money
valuation + investment amount)) does not match the actual ownership
they will hold post-conversion of all outstanding instruments;
(3) the founder discloses notes outstanding but states a pre-money
valuation that excludes the notes from the calculation, presenting
an ownership percentage to new investors that overstates their
actual post-conversion stake.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Convertible note overhang is the single most common source of
ownership surprise at close. The mechanics: a company has raised
£1M in convertible notes at a 20% discount to the next round
with a £5M valuation cap. When the Series A closes at £8M pre-money,
the notes convert at the cap (£5M equivalent price), issuing more
shares than the founder's cap table shows — because the founder
built the cap table using the £8M pre-money price for everyone.
The new Series A investor who was told they would own 20% of the
company post-round actually owns 15%, because the notes converted
at a lower price and diluted everyone including the Series A.
This is not a theoretical error — it is the most common source of
post-close investor complaints in early-stage deals. The founder
may not have done this deliberately: many founders do not correctly
model note conversion and genuinely do not understand the dilution
impact until a lawyer explains it at close. But the effect on
the new investor is identical whether the omission was deliberate
or innocent: they paid for ownership they do not receive.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The convertible notes outstanding represent more than 20% of the
post-conversion fully diluted share count AND the founder presented
a cap table that did not include the notes — this is a material
misrepresentation of the new investor's actual ownership stake,
and the deal cannot proceed on the disclosed terms.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The founder acknowledges the notes, provides a full post-conversion
cap table including all outstanding instruments at their conversion
terms, and the new investor's actual post-conversion ownership
matches their investment thesis requirements.
(b) The notes are small relative to the round (under 5% dilution
on a post-conversion fully diluted basis) and the founder has
disclosed them completely — the dilution is real but not material
enough to change the deal economics.
(c) The notes are being repaid in cash rather than converting —
the principal is returned to note holders at close and does not
result in new share issuance.

VERIFICATION:
Request: (1) a complete list of all convertible instruments
outstanding — notes, SAFEs, ASAs, warrants — including principal,
discount rate, valuation cap, and maturity date for each; (2) a
post-conversion, fully diluted cap table that shows the share
count and ownership percentage for every investor after all
instruments convert at the current round price; (3) confirmation
that the new investor's stated ownership percentage is calculated
on a post-conversion, fully diluted basis. Ask: "Can you provide
a fully diluted cap table showing the ownership of all parties —
including all convertible note holders — after this round closes
and all instruments convert?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a company has outstanding convertible notes and has not
provided a post-conversion fully diluted cap table, require this
document before issuing a term sheet — undisclosed note overhang
is the most common source of post-term-sheet ownership disputes
and is entirely preventable with a correct pre-term-sheet cap table.
