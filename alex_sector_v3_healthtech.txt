SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Healthtech / Medtech):
— Product is used in the diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, or management of patients
  or health conditions — whether by clinicians or directly by consumers
— Primary buyer is the NHS, a private hospital group, a GP practice, an ICB, a pharma
  company, or a health insurance company — rather than a generic enterprise buyer
— Company requires or is seeking regulatory approval (MHRA, CE mark, UKCA, FDA) to
  commercialise the product or make clinical claims about it
— Revenue model includes reimbursement pathway (NHS tariff, insurance reimbursement,
  DTx prescription), clinician licensing, or hospital system contracts with procurement
  frameworks (NHS Supply Chain, G-Cloud, Crown Commercial Service)
— Product handles patient data classified as special category under GDPR and Data
  Protection Act 2018 — requiring explicit consent and specific security controls

CRITICAL CLASSIFICATION DISTINCTION — HEALTHTECH ENABLER vs CLINICAL PRODUCT:
A company selling procurement or HR software to NHS trusts is a B2B SaaS company
that happens to sell into healthcare. Apply the SaaS Library.
A company whose product touches patients, makes clinical claims, or is used in a
clinical pathway is a Healthtech company. Apply this Library.
Test: does the product affect a clinical decision, involve patient data, or make
a health outcome claim? If yes — Healthtech. If no — B2B SaaS.

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A mental health app without clinical evidence and sold as a wellness product is
  Consumer Health (sub-category 7), not a Digital Therapeutic — do not apply DTx
  regulatory requirements unless the company has made clinical claims
— A company building AI for hospital operations (scheduling, bed management, staffing)
  may be classified as Software as a Medical Device depending on whether clinical
  decisions are influenced — check MHRA AIaMD guidance before classifying
— Genomics companies with a B2B data licensing model are Health Data companies
  (sub-category 4), not Medtech — the distinction matters for regulatory treatment

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. CLINICAL DECISION SUPPORT (CDS)
Signals: product is used by clinicians (GPs, radiologists, nurses, surgeons) to aid
diagnosis, triage, or treatment decisions; output of the product influences a clinical
choice; uses AI, machine learning, or clinical algorithms to generate recommendations;
competitive set includes existing clinical guidelines, radiology reading software,
or pathology analysis tools; company may be classified as Software as a Medical Device
(SaMD) under MHRA / FDA guidance.
Regulatory threshold: if the product influences a clinical decision that could harm a
patient if wrong, it is likely a medical device. MHRA classification: Class I (low
risk) to Class III (highest risk). AI used in diagnostic imaging is typically Class IIb
or Class III.
Key risk: clinical evidence requirement for enterprise NHS procurement is high.
A product with no peer-reviewed published evidence will not pass NHS Digital Technology
Assessment Criteria (DTAC) procurement scrutiny.

2. ELECTRONIC HEALTH RECORDS / CLINICAL WORKFLOW
Signals: product manages patient records, clinical notes, appointment scheduling,
referral management, or care coordination across a clinical setting; buyer is an NHS
trust, GP practice, private hospital, or community care provider; submission references
HL7 FHIR integration, NHS Spine connectivity, or compatibility with EMIS/SystmOne/TPP;
ACV is typically high (£50K–£2M per trust) with multi-year contracts.
Key risk: NHS procurement cycles are 12–24 months; NHS Digital service standards and
DTAC compliance add 3–6 months of due diligence before contract award. Sales cycles
are project-managed, not salesforce-driven.
Misclassification risk: check whether the product is SNOMED CT or HL7 FHIR-coded —
if not, it may not integrate with NHS infrastructure and the stated pipeline may be
aspirational rather than achievable.

3. DIGITAL THERAPEUTICS (DTx)
Signals: product is software intended to prevent, manage, or treat a medical condition;
company uses terms like "prescription digital therapeutic," "clinically validated,"
or "evidence-based"; submission references randomised controlled trials (RCTs),
NICE submission, or clinical trial registration (ClinicalTrials.gov or ISRCTN);
product is prescribed by a clinician or recommended by a healthcare pathway, not
self-selected by a consumer.
Regulatory threshold: a DTx making clinical claims is likely a medical device under
MHRA classification. Must hold UKCA mark or be in CE mark transition to be legally
prescribed. A DTx without a mark cannot be prescribed on the NHS.
Business model: NHS reimbursement (via Innovation and Technology Payment, ITP, or
formulary listing), private pay clinic networks, employer self-insured benefits.
Key risk: NICE evidence standards are high. A company with one pilot RCT of 60 patients
cannot secure formulary listing. The evidence bar is typically: two RCTs, minimum 12-
month follow-up, pre-specified endpoints, peer-reviewed publication.

4. HEALTH DATA / ANALYTICS
Signals: primary product is the aggregation, structuring, or analysis of health data —
not the delivery of care; revenue is from pharma, insurers, NHS bodies, or academic
institutions paying for access to real-world data or analytical insights; submission
references anonymisation, data linkage, or de-identification processes; company may
hold NHS data sharing agreements; competitive set includes IQVIA, Veeva, Medidata, or
academic health data networks.
Key risk: patient data is special category under GDPR — any re-identification risk,
inadequate consent, or data sharing outside the approved purpose triggers ICO action.
NHS data sharing agreements are institution-specific and not transferable — losing one
hospital trust partner can remove access to a material portion of the dataset.
Regulatory note: health data companies are not typically classified as medical devices.
But they must hold NHS DSPT certification and comply with the NHS Data Security and
Protection Toolkit at the level appropriate to their data access.

5. MEDTECH / MEDICAL DEVICES
Signals: product is a physical device combined with software; submission references
hardware components, manufacturing, CE/UKCA mark, or FDA 510(k)/PMA clearance; product
claims include diagnostic or therapeutic action (measuring, monitoring, or treating);
competitive set includes established medical device companies (Medtronic, Abbott,
Roche Diagnostics, Philips, Siemens); investor materials reference clinical trials
and regulatory timelines alongside commercial milestones.
Regulatory requirement: all medical devices sold in the UK must be registered with
the MHRA and carry appropriate conformity marking. Post-Brexit, CE marks from EU
notified bodies are accepted in the UK under transitional provisions until 30 June 2030,
after which UKCA marking is required from a UK Approved Body.
Key risk: hardware manufacturing introduces COGS complexity absent from pure software.
Gross margins are typically 50–70% vs 70–85% for software. Unit economics must be
assessed on the hardware-inclusive cost structure.

6. HEALTHTECH ENABLERS / INFRASTRUCTURE
Signals: product supports NHS or private healthcare operations without directly touching
patients or making clinical claims; examples include clinical trial management systems,
pharmacy dispensing software, NHS procurement and supply chain tools, medical coding
and billing software, healthcare workforce management; buyer is an NHS trust operations
team, a pharmaceutical company clinical operations department, or a private hospital
group procurement function.
Regulatory note: infrastructure products typically do not require MHRA registration unless
they influence a clinical decision or process patient data in a way that constitutes SaMD.
Apply SaaS Library benchmarks with the following adjustment: sales cycles are 50–100%
longer than standard enterprise SaaS due to NHS procurement frameworks. A 6-month SaaS
sales cycle becomes 12–18 months in an NHS context.
Key risk: NHS procurement risk is highest for infrastructure companies — framework
compliance (Crown Commercial Service, NHS Supply Chain frameworks) is mandatory for many
trust buyers. A company not on any NHS procurement framework cannot sell to most trusts.

7. DIRECT TO CONSUMER HEALTH (D2C Health)
Signals: product is purchased directly by consumers (patients) without a clinical
prescription or referral; revenue is primarily subscription, one-time purchase, or
freemium-to-paid conversion; marketing channels include social media, influencer
content, and app stores; competitive set includes consumer wellness apps, OTC products,
or gym and fitness services; clinical claims are either absent or mild (does not claim
to diagnose or treat a specific condition).
Key risk: the distinction between a wellness product (not a medical device) and a health
product making clinical claims (potentially a medical device) determines whether MHRA
registration is required. A D2C health company that begins making clinical efficacy
claims to acquire users without regulatory registration is creating a liability.
Apply SaaS/consumer benchmarks adjusted for health context: Day-90 retention below 20%
in chronic disease management apps is a structural failure — the product is not creating
a daily behaviour change. Mental health apps typically see 80%+ of users disengage
within 30 days.

8. HEALTH INSURANCE TECH / BENEFITS
Signals: product is used by health insurance companies, self-insured employers, or
employee benefits administrators to manage claims, model risk, or administer benefits;
revenue is B2B SaaS subscription or percentage of claims processed; submission references
claims automation, prior authorisation, benefits navigation, or population health risk
scoring; competitive set includes legacy benefits administration platforms (Mercer, Aon),
claims processors, or TPA (Third Party Administrator) software vendors.
Regulatory note: if the product influences insurance underwriting decisions or claims
payment decisions, it may be subject to FCA and PRA supervision in the UK depending
on whether the company or its customers are regulated insurers. Confirm regulatory
boundary clearly.
Apply SaaS Library benchmarks. Healthcare context adjustment: ACV is higher than
standard HR SaaS (£50K–£500K per insurer); sales cycles are 9–18 months; data
security requirements mirror GDPR special category data requirements.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. REGULATORY APPROVAL ABSENT FOR DEVICE OR DTx CLAIM
SIGNAL: Company's product makes clinical claims (diagnoses, treats, monitors, or
recommends a clinical action) but does not hold a UKCA mark, CE mark, or MHRA
registration for that claim. Company may describe itself as "in regulatory process"
or state that the claim is "informational, not diagnostic."
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: A product making a clinical claim without regulatory registration is
operating illegally under the Medical Devices Regulations 2002 (as amended post-Brexit).
The MHRA can issue a Warning Notice or Field Safety Corrective Action, forcing the
product off the market. Any NHS trust using an unregistered device is also exposed to
liability.
VERIFICATION: Request the MHRA registration reference number or UKCA/CE Notified Body
certificate. Search the MHRA device register (devices.mhra.gov.uk) for the company name.
If not found, ask: "Under what regulatory classification do you currently operate?"

02. REIMBURSEMENT PATHWAY UNDEFINED
SIGNAL: Company is building a product for NHS adoption but has not identified whether
it will sell through NHS tariff reimbursement, an Innovation and Technology Payment
(ITP) designation, direct trust procurement, or the NHS Innovation Accelerator programme.
Company may assume that if the product works, the NHS will pay for it.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The NHS does not buy products because they work. It buys products that
are on an approved procurement framework, have DTAC compliance, and fit within existing
budget codes. A company without a specific reimbursement pathway is building without
a route to revenue at scale — however good the clinical evidence is.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What is the specific route by which an NHS trust pays for your
product? Is it a capital purchase, a revenue budget line item, or reimbursed via a
specific tariff or ITP designation? Who at NHSE have you spoken to about a pathway?"

03. CLINICAL EVIDENCE INSUFFICIENT FOR TARGET BUYER
SIGNAL: Company is targeting NHS trust procurement or NICE guidance submission but
its clinical evidence base consists of a single pilot study, a non-randomised
observational study, or data from fewer than 100 patients.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: NHS DTAC and NICE evidence standards require: for clinical tools, at
least two peer-reviewed studies with pre-specified endpoints and an appropriate
control condition. A company that has not yet met this standard cannot realistically
win NHS procurement beyond a single early-adopter trust.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What peer-reviewed clinical evidence has been published for your
product? What is the sample size, trial design, and journal? Are you registered on
ClinicalTrials.gov or ISRCTN for any current or planned trials?"

04. SINGLE NHS TRUST PILOT MISREPRESENTED AS COMMERCIAL TRACTION
SIGNAL: Submission references one NHS trust as a customer or pilot site and implies
this represents validated commercial demand. Investigation reveals the trust is using
the product as a funded pilot, not under a commercial contract, and the relationship
was initiated by a personal connection between the founder and a specific clinician.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: This is the healthtech equivalent of the Reference Customer Illusion
(PASS Deal 013 in Training Library). A single trust pilot driven by a clinical champion
is not commercial traction. If the clinical champion moves, leaves, or loses interest,
the pilot ends. It is a proof of concept, not evidence of scalable procurement.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Is your relationship with [trust name] governed by a signed commercial
contract with a specified annual value? Or is it a funded pilot or evaluation? Who
is the primary sponsor of the relationship within the trust — and what happens to the
relationship if they leave?"

05. NHS PROCUREMENT FRAMEWORK ABSENT
SIGNAL: Company is selling to NHS trusts but is not listed on any of the major NHS
procurement frameworks — Crown Commercial Service, NHS Supply Chain, G-Cloud, or
specific category frameworks for clinical software.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The majority of NHS trusts cannot legally purchase products above their
local procurement threshold (typically £10K–£25K per year) without going through an
approved framework. A company not on any framework must either sell below the threshold,
persuade a trust to conduct a standalone procurement exercise (12+ months), or join
a framework (6–18 month process). Without framework access, the TAM is dramatically
smaller than the total NHS addressable market.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Which NHS procurement frameworks are you listed on? If not yet
listed, what is your plan and timeline for achieving framework inclusion?"

06. PATIENT DATA GOVERNANCE GAPS
SIGNAL: Company accesses, stores, or processes patient data but has not completed
NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit (DSPT) certification, does not hold a Data
Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) for its data processing activities, or has not
described its data sharing agreement with NHS data suppliers.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: NHS data is among the most regulated data in the UK. A company without
DSPT certification cannot access NHS patient data under any NHS data sharing agreement.
An ICO investigation triggered by a patient data breach can destroy a healthtech company
faster than any commercial failure — enforcement notices, fines of up to 4% of global
turnover, and reputational damage with NHS buyers are all consequences of inadequate
data governance.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What is your DSPT certification status? Have you completed a DPIA
for your data processing activities? Do you have a signed Data Sharing Agreement in
place with each organisation that provides you with patient data?"

07. KEY OPINION LEADER / CLINICAL CHAMPION DEPENDENCY
SIGNAL: Company's adoption in two or more NHS trusts can be traced directly to a single
clinician (or a small group of 2–3 clinicians) who have championed the product internally.
There is no evidence of independent adoption — every site has been brought in by a
personal referral from the same clinical network.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Clinical champion dependency is the healthtech equivalent of single-channel
dependency. If the champion loses interest, changes role, retires, or moves to a competing
product, adoption at all their associated sites is at risk. A company with six NHS trust
"customers" all brought in by one radiologist is operationally fragile.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "For each NHS trust you work with, who initiated the relationship?
Is there more than one clinical advocate within each trust? Has any trust adopted the
product independently — without a direct introduction from your existing clinical network?"

08. CE MARK OR UKCA MARK ABSENT FOR DEVICE
SIGNAL: Company is selling or demonstrating a medical device in the UK without a valid
UKCA or CE mark (the latter accepted under transitional provisions until June 2030).
May describe the device as "in research phase" or "being used in an observational study"
to avoid the registration requirement.
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: Selling an unregistered medical device in the UK is a criminal offence
under the Medical Devices Regulations 2002. An NHS trust procurement officer who
discovers the device is unregistered will immediately halt the trial and may report
the company to the MHRA. Criminal liability attaches to the directors.
VERIFICATION: Check the MHRA device register. For any device used in NHS settings,
request the device class and registration reference. Any device described as "research
only" that is being used in clinical care — even informally — is in scope of the
Medical Devices Regulations.

09. CLINICAL LIABILITY INSURANCE ABSENT OR INSUFFICIENT
SIGNAL: Company does not hold product liability insurance for clinical use of its software
or device, or the coverage is below £5M per claim for a product used in an NHS clinical
pathway.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: NHS trusts require minimum product liability insurance levels as a
condition of any procurement agreement. For clinical decision support or diagnostic
tools, the standard requirement is £5M–£10M per claim minimum. A company without
this insurance cannot sign an NHS contract. If a patient adverse event occurs and the
company has no liability insurance, a single claim can destroy the company.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What product liability insurance do you hold? What is the per-claim
and annual limit? Does it specifically cover NHS clinical use of your product?"

10. OUTCOME CLAIMS NOT SUPPORTED BY EVIDENCE
SIGNAL: Company's marketing materials or investor deck claim a specific clinical outcome
improvement ("reduces misdiagnosis rate by 40%," "improves medication adherence by
60%") without citing a published peer-reviewed study. The claim may be based on
a small internal pilot or a single centre's retrospective analysis.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: If an NHS clinician relies on an unsupported outcome claim and a patient
is harmed, the company faces both regulatory action (MHRA) and litigation. More
immediately, NHS procurement due diligence teams will validate every outcome claim —
a claim that cannot be evidenced will end a procurement process and damage the company's
credibility across the trust network.
VERIFICATION: For each specific outcome claim, ask: "Is this claim supported by a
peer-reviewed, published study? Can you share the citation?" If the answer is "we have
internal data showing this" — it is not an evidence-supported claim for procurement purposes.

11. CONSUMER HEALTH CHURN BEFORE DAY 90
SIGNAL: D2C health company reports strong downloads (50K–500K) but does not disclose
Day-30 or Day-90 retention. Monthly active users are a small fraction of total registered
users. Company focuses investor communications on top-of-funnel metrics (downloads, sign-ups)
rather than retention or paying conversion.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Consumer health apps have some of the worst retention rates of any software
category. If 90-day retention is below 15%, the product is not creating a behaviour change
— it is a novelty. The business model depends on continuously acquiring new users to
replace those who disengage, which is structurally unsound unless CAC is extremely low.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of users who sign up are still active at Day 30?
Day 90? What percentage have converted to a paying plan?"

12. REGULATORY RECLASSIFICATION RISK MID-SCALE
SIGNAL: Company has been operating under a specific regulatory classification (e.g.,
general wellness device, Class I SaMD, or non-device software) and has recently
expanded its product to include features that may trigger reclassification to a higher
risk class by the MHRA.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: MHRA can reclassify a product mid-market if new functionality makes
clinical claims that the existing registration does not cover. Reclassification to
Class IIb or Class III stops commercial sales while new regulatory approval is sought.
Revenue growth halts during the approval process.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Has your product changed materially since your original regulatory
registration? Have you notified your Notified Body of any new features? Has the MHRA
or your regulatory affairs team reviewed the current version against your registration?"

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE CLINICAL EVIDENCE GAP
DESCRIPTION: A healthtech company with a product that demonstrably works in small
pilots secures seed or Series A funding on the strength of early clinical data.
The founders, confident in their product, begin scaling commercially to NHS trusts.
Enterprise procurement teams request NICE-standard evidence — two RCTs, peer-reviewed
publication, pre-specified endpoints. The company's evidence base is a single-centre
observational pilot with 40 patients. Every enterprise procurement conversation stalls
at the due diligence stage. Revenue plateaus at 2–3 early-adopter trusts willing to
take a risk. The company runs out of runway before its own RCT completes. The product
worked — but it could never be proven to the standards that justify NHS-wide adoption.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Clinical evidence at Series A is a single pilot study below
100 patients; no RCT registered or in planning; company's slide deck describes the
evidence as "strong preliminary data" rather than "published peer-reviewed evidence";
enterprise NHS sales pipeline is all early-stage with no procurement decisions expected
within 12 months; founder describes NHS procurement as "slow" rather than understanding
that the procurement delay is an evidence gap.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any clinical tool targeting NHS trust-level procurement where the
company cannot provide at least one published peer-reviewed study and an RCT in progress.

2. THE NHS PROCUREMENT WALL
DESCRIPTION: A healthtech company builds a product that clinical users love. It is
used informally by clinicians at several trusts who have adopted it personally (on
personal devices or under departmental discretionary spend). The company attempts
to convert these informal users into formal institutional contracts. It discovers that
formal procurement requires DTAC compliance, information governance sign-off, clinical
procurement committee approval, and a legal framework agreement — a process that takes
12–18 months per trust even when everyone wants to proceed. The company cannot convert
its enthusiastic users into contracted revenue fast enough to survive. It runs out of
money while its procurement pipeline is processing.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Multiple trusts described as "very interested" but no signed
contracts after 12 months of commercial activity; company describes its sales motion as
"relationship-driven" or "clinician-led" without a formal procurement team; DTAC
compliance has not been completed; company is not on any NHS procurement framework;
pipeline includes trusts where no formal procurement case has been submitted.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any NHS-focused product company where the ratio of "interested" or "in
discussion" trusts to signed contracts is greater than 5:1.

3. THE REIMBURSEMENT PIVOT
DESCRIPTION: A digital therapeutics or clinical software company builds for NHS
reimbursement — a specific pathway where the NHS will pay for the product per patient
treated. The founders have received encouraging informal signals from NHS England. The
company raises a Series A on the assumption that NHS reimbursement will arrive within
18 months. NHSE does not finalise the pathway. The clinical evidence is not yet at
NICE submission standard. With no reimbursement pathway, the company cannot generate
volume revenue — each trust procurement is a one-off deal generating £50–200K. The
company restructures to pursue direct-pay private clinic networks and employer benefits,
which has a different and smaller TAM than the NHS pathway it was built for. Investors
who funded the NHS thesis are now funding a materially different business.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Business plan explicitly references NHS Innovation and Technology
Payment (ITP) designation or NICE formulary listing as the primary revenue pathway;
no ITP application has been submitted; NICE engagement has not begun; company has
informal signals but no formal written commitment from NHSE; the Series A valuation
assumes NHS reimbursement revenue within 24 months.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any DTx or clinical technology company where the financial model depends
on a specific NHS reimbursement pathway that is not yet formally agreed.

4. THE REGULATORY RECLASSIFICATION TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A software company sells a product that analyses health data or assists
clinical decision-making and operates under a minimal regulatory classification —
"general wellness," Class I SaMD, or no classification at all. As it scales and the
product matures, new AI features are added that clearly influence clinical decisions.
The MHRA or a Notified Body review triggers a reclassification of the product to Class
IIb or Class III. The company must immediately cease commercial sales of the reclassified
product and begin a Notified Body conformity assessment — a process that takes 18–36
months and costs £500K–£2M. Existing contracts with NHS trusts must be suspended pending
the new approval. Revenue drops to near zero while the approval is in progress.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: AI feature set has expanded substantially since original
regulatory classification review; new features generate clinical recommendations that
influence prescribing, diagnosis, or treatment pathway decisions; the company has not
conducted a new regulatory classification review after adding AI capabilities; the
regulatory affairs function is understaffed or treated as a compliance box-tick rather
than a core business function.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any AI-enabled clinical tool that started as a Class I or unregistered
product and has since added features that generate clinical recommendations, diagnostic
outputs, or risk scores that clinicians rely on for patient decisions.

5. THE CONSUMER HEALTH CHURN CLIFF
DESCRIPTION: A D2C health app launches and achieves strong early traction — 100K+
downloads, press coverage, and impressive Month-1 retention. Investor interest follows.
The company raises seed funding on the strength of its growth metrics. In the 6 months
after raising, the founders and investors discover that Day-90 retention is 8%. The
cohort of users who downloaded in Month 1 has almost entirely disengaged. New user
acquisition via paid marketing is required to replace the disengaged cohort. CAC rises.
LTV/CAC ratios collapse. The company is not building a health behaviour product —
it is acquiring and discarding users faster than it can monetise them. The product
never generates the clinical evidence needed for NHS adoption because users disengage
before completing a meaningful intervention period.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Investor materials focus on total registered users or downloads
rather than MAU/DAU ratio; Day-30 and Day-90 retention not disclosed; NPS is high
but does not correlate with sustained engagement; paid acquisition is the primary
growth channel; the product has not changed a health behaviour for any user cohort
that can be measured over 90+ days.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any D2C health app or digital wellbeing product where Day-90 retention
has not been disclosed or is below 20%.

6. THE CLINICAL CHAMPION DEPENDENCY
DESCRIPTION: A clinical software or decision support company achieves adoption in 8–12
NHS trusts. Investigation reveals that 7 of the 12 trusts were introduced by a single
clinical champion — a professor, consultant, or clinical director who is personally
enthusiastic about the technology and has used their professional network to facilitate
introductions. The company treats this as business development success. When the clinical
champion takes a new position, moves to a competing product, retires, or experiences a
falling out with the company, the introductions stop. The 7 trusts associated with the
champion enter a period of uncertainty. Without the champion's advocacy, renewal
conversations become difficult. The company's apparent momentum was entirely dependent
on one person's enthusiasm and professional relationships — neither of which are assets
on the balance sheet.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Founder cannot describe the company's institutional relationships
independently of specific named clinicians; more than 50% of trust relationships were
introduced by the same clinical advisor or champion; clinical advisors are equity-holders
or have financial interests in the company's success; no trust has adopted the product
through an independent procurement process without a personal introduction.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any NHS-facing clinical company where the founder describes growth
primarily in terms of clinical relationships rather than institutional contracts.

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. REGULATORY APPROVAL STATUS AND PATHWAY
QUESTION: "What is the regulatory classification of your product under MHRA guidance?
Do you hold a UKCA mark, a CE mark, or any other device registration? If in process,
who is your Notified Body and what stage is the conformity assessment at?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific MHRA device class stated; registration reference number available;
Notified Body named with a specific conformity assessment stage and expected timeline.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're working through the regulatory process"; "we believe we're Class I
so we don't need a Notified Body"; describes the regulatory pathway without specifics.
REVEALS: Whether the company can legally continue selling and whether a regulatory
approval gap will halt commercial sales at scale.

02. CLINICAL EVIDENCE — STATUS AND STANDARD
QUESTION: "What peer-reviewed clinical evidence has been published for your product?
What are the trial design, sample size, primary endpoints, and journal? Are you
registered on ClinicalTrials.gov or ISRCTN for any ongoing or planned RCTs?"
STRONG ANSWER: Cites at least one published study with journal name, sample size, and
trial design; has a second RCT registered or in recruitment; endpoints are pre-specified
and clinically meaningful (not proxy measures).
WEAK ANSWER: "We have strong preliminary data" from an internal pilot; "we're planning
to run a study"; cites unpublished conference presentations or white papers.
REVEALS: Whether the company has the evidence required for NHS enterprise procurement
and whether it is investing in generating it.

03. REIMBURSEMENT PATHWAY — SPECIFIC ROUTE
QUESTION: "What is the specific route by which the NHS pays for your product? Is it
via a framework contract, a trust-level capital purchase, a specific tariff or payment
code, an Innovation and Technology Payment designation, or NHS Innovation Accelerator
support? Have you had written confirmation of this pathway from NHS England?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names a specific reimbursement route with written confirmation; has either
an ITP designation, is on a named framework, or has signed contracts that specify the
exact budget code the trust is using.
WEAK ANSWER: "The NHS will pay for products that work"; "we're in conversations with
NHS England"; "trusts can fund it from their innovation budget."
REVEALS: Whether there is a real and specific route to NHS revenue at scale or whether
the company is operating on the assumption that a pathway will materialise.

04. NHS PROCUREMENT FRAMEWORK ACCESS
QUESTION: "Which NHS procurement frameworks are you listed on — Crown Commercial Service,
NHS Supply Chain, G-Cloud, or category-specific frameworks? If not yet listed, what is
the timeline and who is leading your framework application?"
STRONG ANSWER: Named on at least one NHS procurement framework relevant to its product
category; can describe the lot and category number; understands the process for renewal.
WEAK ANSWER: "Most of our customers find ways to procure directly"; "we're not on a
framework yet but it hasn't been a problem"; cannot name any procurement framework.
REVEALS: Whether the company can scale NHS sales beyond early-adopter trusts that are
willing to conduct standalone procurement processes.

05. NHS TRUST CONTRACT INDEPENDENCE FROM CLINICAL CHAMPION
QUESTION: "For each NHS trust you work with, who initiated the relationship? Is the
primary contract held at trust level with institutional sign-off, or is it a departmental
relationship driven by a specific clinician? If the key clinical contact left tomorrow,
what would happen to the contract?"
STRONG ANSWER: Contracts are signed at trust or procurement level; multiple clinical
stakeholders are engaged at each site; at least two trusts adopted the product through
an independent procurement process without a direct introduction from the same network.
WEAK ANSWER: "Our relationships are very much driven by [named clinician]"; cannot
describe institutional ownership of the contract; "the contract would probably continue
but we'd need to rebuild the relationship."
REVEALS: Whether commercial traction is institutionally embedded or is a single person's
enthusiasm expressed as a contract.

06. PATIENT DATA GOVERNANCE — CERTIFICATION STATUS
QUESTION: "What is your DSPT certification level? Have you completed a Data Protection
Impact Assessment for all data processing activities involving patient data? Do you have
signed Data Sharing Agreements in place with every organisation that provides patient data?"
STRONG ANSWER: DSPT certified at the appropriate level; DPIA completed and up-to-date;
all data sharing agreements signed and in scope for the current data use; data minimisation
principles applied.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're working on the DSPT"; "we treat all data as confidential"; cannot
confirm whether DSAs are in place for all data sources; describes GDPR compliance
generically without addressing the NHS-specific requirements.
REVEALS: Whether the company can access NHS patient data legally and whether a data
governance failure could halt operations.

07. CLINICAL LIABILITY INSURANCE
QUESTION: "What product liability insurance do you hold? What is the per-claim limit
and the annual aggregate limit? Does the policy specifically cover NHS clinical use of
your product, including use in a clinical decision pathway? Have you disclosed your
current product version to your insurer?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names insurer, states per-claim and annual limits (minimum £5M for
clinical tools); confirms NHS clinical use is explicitly covered; insurer has been
notified of current product version.
WEAK ANSWER: "We have general liability insurance"; cannot confirm clinical coverage;
"we haven't had any claims so it hasn't been an issue."
REVEALS: Whether an adverse clinical event would be financially survivable or would
destroy the company — and whether the company understands its clinical liability exposure.

08. TIME TO FIRST NHS TRUST GO-LIVE
QUESTION: "For your existing NHS trust contracts, what was the time between first
commercial conversation and clinical go-live? What caused the longest delays in that
process and what would you change?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific timelines for each trust; demonstrates learning — subsequent
trusts went live faster than the first; identifies specific DTAC, IG, or procurement
steps that created delays and has a process to address them.
WEAK ANSWER: "It varies a lot between trusts"; cannot provide specific timelines; blames
NHS bureaucracy without demonstrating a strategy to navigate it faster.
REVEALS: Whether the company understands the NHS implementation process as a repeatable
operation or as an unpredictable event — the former suggests scalability, the latter does not.

09. ICB / ICS RELATIONSHIP AND PIPELINE
QUESTION: "Are you working at the ICB or ICS level, or primarily at individual trust
level? Have any ICBs committed to funding your product for their whole system? And
what is your strategy for moving from individual trust contracts to system-wide adoption?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has at least one ICB-level conversation in advanced stage; can describe
the specific ICB contact (CCIO, CMO, Director of Digital) and the nature of the
commitment; has a clear strategy for how a trust contract becomes an ICB conversation.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're focused on individual trusts for now"; "ICBs are on the roadmap";
cannot describe any specific ICB relationship.
REVEALS: Whether the company's growth strategy includes the system-level contracts that
generate £500K–£3M+ annual values, or whether it is building a long tail of small
departmental relationships that will be expensive to maintain.

10. NICE GUIDANCE — STATUS OR PLAN
QUESTION: "Have you engaged with NICE regarding any guidance, technology appraisal,
or evidence standards framework review for your product? If so, what has the engagement
produced? If not, why not — and what evidence standard would NICE require?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has had a formal NICE engagement (even exploratory); can describe the
evidence standard required for a technology appraisal; has a plan and timeline for
NICE submission aligned with its RCT completion.
WEAK ANSWER: "We'll engage with NICE when the evidence is stronger"; cannot describe
the NICE evidence standards framework; treats NICE as a distant regulatory step rather
than a strategic milestone.
REVEALS: Whether the company is building evidence with NICE submission in mind or
building a product that may never reach the evidence bar required for NHS-wide adoption.

11. FUNDING AND RUNWAY TO NEXT REGULATORY OR EVIDENCE MILESTONE
QUESTION: "What is your next major regulatory or clinical evidence milestone — and what
is your funding runway to reach it? Is your current funding sufficient to complete the
RCT, obtain the mark, or reach NICE submission without another fundraise?"
STRONG ANSWER: Clear milestone (e.g., "CE mark by Q3 2026," "RCT results published Q4
2026"); funding runway covers the milestone with 3+ months to spare; has modelled the
cost of the milestone with a contingency.
WEAK ANSWER: "We have 12 months of runway and we're working on multiple milestones";
cannot confirm whether current funding covers the next milestone; describes fundraising
as "ongoing."
REVEALS: Whether the company is at risk of running out of money before reaching the
evidence or regulatory milestone that its valuation is based on — a common and
catastrophic failure mode in capital-intensive healthtech.

12. AI MODEL VALIDATION AND CLINICAL SAFETY
QUESTION: "If your product uses AI or machine learning to generate clinical recommendations
or outputs: how was the model validated? On what patient population? Has it been tested
for performance across different demographic groups to detect bias? Who is the named
responsible person under SMCR for the AI's clinical outputs?"
STRONG ANSWER: External clinical validation study with a demographically diverse patient
population; bias testing completed and results disclosed; named clinical safety officer;
MHRA AIaMD guidance applied.
WEAK ANSWER: "The model performs well on our training data"; validated internally only;
"we use a third-party AI model so the validation is their responsibility"; cannot name
a responsible person for AI clinical outputs.
REVEALS: Whether the company has addressed the clinical safety requirements for AI in
healthcare — a regulatory and liability exposure that is increasingly on the MHRA's
enforcement agenda.

MHRA MEDICAL DEVICE CLASSIFICATION (UK — POST-BREXIT):

The UK operates its own medical device regulatory framework post-Brexit, implemented
through the Medical Devices Regulations 2002 (as amended by SI 2019/791 and subsequent
statutory instruments). Key classifications:

Class I (Low Risk): General medical devices with no invasive or measuring function.
  Examples: wound dressings, stethoscopes, most general wellness devices.
  Registration: self-declaration; MHRA registration required from 1 July 2023.
  Notified Body: not required for Class I (except sterile or measuring devices).

Class IIa (Low–Medium Risk): Devices used in diagnosis, monitoring, or temporary
  implantation. Examples: blood pressure monitors, dental drills, contact lenses.
  Registration: requires UK Approved Body conformity assessment.

Class IIb (Medium–High Risk): Devices with higher potential for harm, including most
  Software as a Medical Device that influences clinical decisions.
  Examples: AI diagnostic software, blood glucose monitors, infusion pumps.
  Registration: requires UK Approved Body conformity assessment; clinical investigation
  data typically required.

Class III (Highest Risk): Devices that sustain life, are implanted long-term, or carry
  highest risk of harm. Examples: pacemakers, joint replacements, IVDs for high-risk
  conditions.
  Registration: full clinical evidence package required; MHRA/PRA approval pathway.

Software as a Medical Device (SaMD):
  MHRA applies a specific decision framework for software. Software is a medical device
  if it influences a clinical decision that could harm a patient if wrong.
  AI as a Medical Device (AIaMD): MHRA published specific guidance (2024) requiring:
  — Pre-market: clinical validation, transparent performance metrics, bias assessment.
  — Post-market: continuous performance monitoring, adverse event reporting.
  — Accountability: named responsible person for each AI clinical output under SMCR.
  Alex flag: any AI tool used in clinical decision-making must be assessed against
  AIaMD guidance regardless of its current regulatory classification.

CE MARK VS UKCA MARK TRANSITION:
  Post-Brexit, UK introduced UKCA marking for medical devices sold in Great Britain.
  Current transitional provisions (as of April 2026):
  — CE marks issued by EU Notified Bodies are accepted in Great Britain until 30 June 2030.
  — After 30 June 2030, UKCA marking from a UK Approved Body will be required.
  — Northern Ireland: CE marking remains the requirement (NI Protocol / Windsor Framework).
  — EU sales: UK companies selling into the EU still require CE marking from an EU Notified Body.
  Practical implication: a company targeting both UK and EU markets needs both UKCA
  (or transitional CE) for the UK and CE for EU. Two Notified Body processes.

FDA PATHWAYS (FOR US EXPANSION):
  510(k) Clearance: demonstrates substantial equivalence to a predicate device. Most
    common pathway for Class II medical devices. Timeline: 3–12 months.
  De Novo Request: for novel devices with no predicate. Creates a new device classification.
    Timeline: 12–24 months.
  PMA (Pre-Market Approval): required for Class III devices. Full clinical evidence package.
    Timeline: 2–5 years. Most expensive and rigorous pathway.
  FDA Breakthrough Device Designation: expedited review for devices addressing serious
    conditions. Reduces FDA review timeline by 50%+. Must apply and be granted.
  Alex flag: a UK healthtech company targeting the US market with a Class IIb or III
  device should have FDA clearance or a clear PMA timeline as a condition of investment.
  A company that mentions "US expansion" without a named FDA pathway is not planning
  a US launch — it is aspirationally discussing one.

NHS DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY ASSESSMENT CRITERIA (DTAC):
  DTAC is the NHS England framework for assessing digital health technologies before
  NHS procurement. Five domains: clinical safety, data protection, technical security,
  interoperability, usability and accessibility.
  Mandatory for most NHS trust and ICB procurement decisions above local thresholds.
  Completion timeline: 3–6 months for a company with existing clinical evidence and
    information governance documentation.
  Alex flag: a company targeting NHS procurement that has not begun DTAC should add
    6+ months to every projected NHS contract signing timeline.

NICE EVIDENCE STANDARDS FRAMEWORK (ESF):
  NICE categorises evidence requirements for digital health technologies into four tiers:
  Tier A: First in class or low risk — feasibility and acceptability evidence required.
  Tier B: Novel technologies with moderate claims — proof of concept, comparative data.
  Tier C: Technologies making clinical outcome claims — RCT-level evidence required,
    peer-reviewed publication, health economic model.
  Tier D: Technologies that should replace current standard of care — systematic review
    level evidence required.
  Most clinical decision support and DTx products target Tier C or D.
  Alex flag: a company pitching a Tier C or D clinical claim without at least one RCT
    in progress is not on a realistic NICE approval trajectory within a 3-year investment horizon.

DATA SECURITY AND PROTECTION TOOLKIT (DSPT):
  Mandatory for all organisations accessing NHS patient data.
  Three certification levels: Approaching Standards | Standards Met | Standards Exceeded.
  NHS trusts cannot share patient data with third-party companies that have not achieved
  at least "Approaching Standards" status.
  Annual renewal: DSPT must be re-assessed and resubmitted each year.
  Alex flag: any company claiming to work with NHS patient data that cannot confirm
    their current DSPT status is operating outside NHS data governance requirements.

PATIENT DATA GOVERNANCE — SPECIAL CATEGORY DATA:
  Under GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018, health data is special category data.
  Requirements beyond standard GDPR:
  — Explicit consent or another specific lawful basis (often substantial public interest
    or medical purpose under Schedule 1, DPA 2018).
  — Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is mandatory for large-scale processing
    of health data.
  — Data minimisation: only data necessary for the stated purpose may be processed.
  — NHS Data Sharing Agreements: required before any patient data is shared between
    organisations. Institution-specific — not transferable.
  — National Data Opt-Out: patients who have opted out of NHS data sharing for research
    must be excluded from datasets. Companies must query and apply the opt-out register.
  Alex flag: a health data company that does not describe its National Data Opt-Out
    compliance is not meeting NHS data governance requirements.

CQC REGISTRATION:
  Care Quality Commission registration is required for providers of regulated activities
  in England — including clinical care, diagnostics, and treatment.
  Applies to: providers of clinical services (not software vendors, unless the software
    constitutes the clinical service itself).
  Digital-physical hybrid models: companies providing clinical consultations via app
    or remote diagnostic services may be CQC-registerable.
  Alex flag: any company providing clinical consultations, treatment recommendations,
    or diagnostic services directly to patients — even digitally — should clarify
    its CQC registration status.

AI AS MEDICAL DEVICE (AIaMD) — MHRA GUIDANCE (2024):
  MHRA's AIaMD framework applies to any AI system used in clinical decision-making.
  Key requirements:
  — Pre-deployment validation on a patient population representative of the intended
    clinical use population.
  — Bias and fairness testing across protected characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity).
  — Post-market surveillance: continuous monitoring of AI performance in deployment;
    adverse event reporting to MHRA.
  — Transparency: clinicians must be informed they are using an AI tool; the AI must
    not present as a human clinical judgement.
  — Explainability: the reasoning behind AI outputs must be explainable to clinicians
    in terms they can evaluate.
  EU AI Act (applicable to UK companies with EU operations): AI used in medical devices
    is classified as HIGH RISK. Conformity assessment, human oversight, accuracy testing,
    and EU database registration are all required. In force from August 2026 for
    high-risk AI providers.
