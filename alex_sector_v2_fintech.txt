SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Fintech):
— Revenue is derived from financial flows: take rates on GMV/TPV, net interest margin,
  insurance premiums, AUM-based fees, or interchange from card transactions
— Company requires or holds a financial regulatory licence to operate legally
  (EMI, PI, banking licence, insurance authorisation, FCA-regulated activity permission)
— Primary value proposition is moving, lending, insuring, or investing money on behalf
  of end users — not selling software tools to people who do those things
— Unit economics reference financial terms: NIM, combined ratio, loss rate, take rate,
  TPV, AUM — rather than ARR, NRR, or CAC in isolation

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION — FINTECH VS REGTECH:
A company selling compliance software to banks is B2B SaaS (Section A of SaaS Library).
A company providing regulated payment or lending services to end users is Fintech.
Test: does the company hold the regulatory risk, or does its customer hold the risk?
If the company holds the risk — it is Fintech.

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A company with a "fintech platform" that earns pure SaaS subscription fees from banks
  without holding any regulatory licence or financial risk is B2B SaaS, not Fintech
— A marketplace connecting lenders to borrowers that bears no credit risk is a marketplace;
  if it bears credit risk, it is a lending company
— Crypto and DeFi protocols follow distinct economics — classify separately and apply
  deep regulatory scrutiny before any PRIORITY REVIEW recommendation

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. PAYMENTS INFRASTRUCTURE
Signals: revenue stated as a percentage of GMV, TPV, or transaction volume; primary
metric is Total Payment Volume with take rate layered on top; technology sits in the
payment authorisation, processing, or settlement flow; named competitive set includes
Stripe, Checkout.com, Adyen, Worldpay, or Braintree; company may hold PI or EMI licence.
Business model: take rate on GMV. Benchmark take rate: 0.10%–0.35% for infrastructure
plays; 1.5%–3.0% for SMB-facing payment gateways.
Key risk: take rate compression from Visa/Mastercard scheme changes and competitive
intensity at the gateway layer.

2. NEOBANK / CHALLENGER BANK
Signals: company holds a full banking licence or EMI licence and offers current accounts,
savings, or debit cards; revenue stated as a blend of interchange, premium subscription,
and lending margin; metric references include active accounts, card spend, and revenue
per user; competitive set includes Monzo, Revolut, Starling, N26, or traditional banks.
Business model: interchange on card spend + subscription premium tier + lending margin
on credit products. UK debit interchange is regulated at ~0.2%; credit card interchange
0.3% under interchange fee regulation.
Key risk: customer acquisition cost is high (£40–£120 per active account) and revenue
per user is low until credit products are added (£20–£80/year for transactional-only users).

3. LENDING / CREDIT
Signals: company originates, funds, or facilitates loans; revenue is primarily net interest
margin or origination fees; submission references loan book size, default rates, or credit
loss provisions; regulated under FCA Consumer Credit Act permissions or institutional
lending exemptions; competitive set includes Iwoca, Funding Circle, OakNorth, or Tide.
Business model: net interest margin (gross interest rate minus cost of funds minus loss
provision minus servicing cost). NIM benchmark: 4%–12% for SMB lending; 1%–4%
for consumer credit in competitive segments.
Key risk: credit loss rate can spike acyclically; funding line withdrawal risk;
regulatory action on irresponsible lending.

4. INSURTECH
Signals: company holds insurance authorisation or operates as an MGA (Managing General
Agent); revenue is gross written premium minus claims minus operating costs; submission
references combined ratio, loss ratio, or claims frequency; competitive set includes
Lemonade, Zego, Marshmallow, Bought By Many, or incumbent insurers.
Business model: insurance premiums minus claims (loss ratio) minus operating expenses
(expense ratio) = operating profit. Combined ratio = loss ratio + expense ratio.
Combined ratio below 100% = underwriting profit; above 100% = underwriting loss.
Key risk: a single catastrophic claims event or adverse weather season can trigger
regulatory capital breach; pricing models may misprice risk at scale.

5. WEALTHTECH / INVESTMENT
Signals: company manages or facilitates the management of investment assets; revenue
is a percentage of AUM, flat subscription, or trading commission; submission references
AUM growth, assets per user, or investment returns; regulated under FCA investment
management or investment advisory permissions; competitive set includes Nutmeg (acquired
by JP Morgan), Freetrade, Plum, Moneybox, or Hargreaves Lansdown.
Business model: AUM-based fee (0.15%–0.75% per annum) or flat subscription (£3–£15/month).
Revenue scales linearly with AUM; AUM grows with market returns + net new deposits.
Key risk: market drawdown simultaneously reduces AUM (and revenue) while increasing
customer churn; subscription model requires very high customer acquisition volume at
low ARPU to generate venture returns.

6. REGTECH / COMPLIANCE TECHNOLOGY
Signals: company sells software specifically to financial services firms to manage
regulatory obligations (AML, KYC, transaction monitoring, sanctions screening, regulatory
reporting); buyer titles include Chief Compliance Officer, Head of Financial Crime,
MLRO, or Head of Risk; the company does not itself hold a licence but its customers are
all regulated; competitive set includes ComplyAdvantage, Onfido, Featurespace, Napier,
or NICE Actimize.
Business model: SaaS subscription to regulated entities. Apply B2B SaaS benchmarks
(Section B of SaaS Library) with fintech-specific adjustments: ACV typically £50K–£500K;
enterprise sales cycles 6–18 months; deep compliance review required before any go-live.
Key risk: regulatory requirement changes can accelerate or obsolete the product overnight;
a single false negative (missed criminal transaction) can trigger regulatory action on
the company's bank customers, creating reputational liability.

7. OPEN BANKING / API INFRASTRUCTURE
Signals: company enables bank account access, payment initiation, or financial data
aggregation via API; revenue is per-API-call or platform fee; operates under Open Banking
or PSD2/PSD3 framework; primary customers are other fintech companies or enterprise
businesses building financial features; competitive set includes TrueLayer, Plaid (US),
Tink (acquired by Visa), Token, or Yapily.
Business model: API call volume fees + platform access fees + data product fees.
Take rate equivalent: £0.05–£0.50 per API call for account data; £0.10–£1.00 per
payment initiation depending on volume tier.
Key risk: bank API quality and coverage in the UK/EU is inconsistent; banks have
incentive to degrade API performance for third-party access; regulatory mandate may
weaken under PSD3 if political appetite for open banking declines.

8. EMBEDDED FINANCE / BANKING AS A SERVICE (BaaS)
Signals: company provides banking infrastructure (IBAN issuance, card issuing, lending
rails) to non-financial companies as a white-label product; revenue is a mix of BaaS
platform fees and interchange from cards issued; primary customers are B2B SaaS
companies, marketplaces, and fintechs building financial products without a licence;
competitive set includes Railsbank (now Railsr), Solaris, Marqeta, Synapse, or ClearBank.
Business model: BaaS platform fee (£5K–£100K per month per customer) + interchange
on cards issued through the platform (0.2%–0.4% of card spend).
Key risk: regulatory compliance failures at a single customer can trigger FCA action
against the BaaS provider; a single large customer exiting the platform creates
revenue cliff; end-customer fraud is a shared regulatory liability.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. REGULATORY LICENCE RISK — OPERATING WITHOUT AUTHORISATION
SIGNAL: Company is processing payments, holding customer funds, or originating loans
in a jurisdiction where it does not hold the required regulatory licence. May describe
this as "operating under an exemption," "partnering with a licensed entity," or
"in the process of applying."
SEVERITY: CRITICAL
WHAT IT MEANS: The company can be shut down by the FCA or equivalent regulator with
24–48 hours notice. All revenue earned without a licence may be clawable. Investors
in unlicensed fintech face potential liability exposure.
VERIFICATION: Request the regulatory licence number (FCA registration number in the UK).
Search the FCA register directly. If the company says "we are in application," ask:
"What is the expected licence grant date and what is your business plan if it is delayed
by 6 months?" An uncertain answer is grounds for PASS.

02. BANKING PARTNER CONCENTRATION
SIGNAL: Company operates through a single banking partner (e.g., one EMI partner bank,
one BaaS provider, or one acquiring bank) that provides its core infrastructure.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: If the banking partner withdraws (due to its own regulatory issues, a
commercial decision, or a compliance concern about the fintech's customer base), the
company's operations can halt within 30–90 days. This is a single point of operational
failure, not a commercial risk — it is an existential risk.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "How many banking partners do you have? If [named partner] gave you
90 days notice tomorrow, what is your operational continuity plan?"

03. FRAUD RATE ABOVE INDUSTRY THRESHOLD
SIGNAL: Fraud losses stated or inferable above 0.10% of TPV for payments; above 5% of
accounts flagged for suspicious activity in neobanks; above 3% of policy count for
insurtech. Company may disclose fraud losses as "within acceptable range" without
providing the absolute figure.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Fraud losses above threshold indicate either inadequate controls
(attracting fraudsters) or a fundamentally fraudulent customer base (acquired cheaply
because it was not screened properly). Regulators will act on a company with material
fraud rates — FCA Consumer Duty requires firms to prevent harm.
VERIFICATION: Request fraud loss rate as % of TPV and as £ absolute for the last 4
quarters. A company that cannot produce this number has inadequate fraud monitoring.

04. CREDIT LOSS RATE ACCELERATION
SIGNAL: Net loss rate on the loan book has increased by more than 1.5 percentage
points in the last two quarters, or vintage analysis shows newer cohorts defaulting
faster than older cohorts at the same loan age.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The underwriting model is deteriorating — either because the company
expanded into a riskier customer segment to maintain growth, or because the model
is not predictive of actual credit behaviour at scale. A credit loss spiral can
consume equity capital faster than any other fintech failure mode.
VERIFICATION: Request vintage loss curves for each cohort quarter over the last 8
quarters. Overlay with underwriting criteria changes. If the company cannot produce
vintage analysis, the credit book is not being managed to institutional standard.

05. TAKE RATE COMPRESSION TREND
SIGNAL: Take rate (revenue as % of GMV) has declined for two or more consecutive
quarters while TPV has grown. Total revenue growth appears strong but the revenue
yield on each transaction is falling.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Either the company is discounting to win volume (price-driven growth
with deteriorating unit economics) or payment scheme changes (Visa/Mastercard
interchange updates, PSD2 caps) are compressing margins structurally. Revenue
growth cannot sustain against permanent take rate compression.
VERIFICATION: Calculate take rate for each of the last 6 quarters from TPV and net
revenue. If the trend is downward, ask: "What is your floor take rate at which the
business is still operationally viable? How close are you to that floor?"

06. AML / KYC COMPLIANCE GAPS
SIGNAL: Company has not described its AML programme, has not named its Money Laundering
Reporting Officer (MLRO), or describes KYC as a "friction reduction" initiative rather
than a core compliance function. May describe onboarding as "frictionless" without
explaining what KYC checks are in place.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: FCA requires all regulated entities to maintain adequate AML controls.
A fintech that has minimised KYC for growth purposes is either not in compliance with
its licence conditions or is attracting the criminal and fraudulent accounts that properly
screened competitors reject. The FCA's Skilled Persons reviews (Section 166) and
enforcement actions have been levied specifically for AML failures.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Who is your MLRO and what is their background? What is your
Suspicious Activity Report filing rate per quarter? What is your automated vs manual
KYC process?" An MLRO who cannot be named is a disqualifying flag.

07. REGULATORY CAPITAL APPROACHING MINIMUM
SIGNAL: Company's liquid capital is within 20% of its regulatory minimum requirement.
Company has not disclosed a plan to raise additional capital before the breach point.
May describe the position as "comfortable" without providing absolute figures.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A regulatory capital breach triggers immediate FCA notification,
potential business restriction, and mandatory remediation. The FCA can appoint a Skilled
Person at the firm's cost and restrict its ability to take on new customers. A capital
breach can destroy a fintech in weeks.
VERIFICATION: Request the current regulatory capital position vs minimum requirement
and the quarterly burn rate against the regulatory capital floor.

08. SINGLE PAYMENT SCHEME DEPENDENCY
SIGNAL: All card processing runs through a single scheme (Visa only or Mastercard only).
Or all open banking connectivity routes through a single bank API provider with no
fallback infrastructure.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Visa or Mastercard can change their technical requirements, pricing
schedule, or acceptance criteria in ways that materially affect the business. A company
with no scheme diversification has no negotiating power and no operational resilience.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Which payment schemes do you process across? Do you have redundant
open banking connectivity if your primary API provider has an outage?"

09. CUSTOMER FUNDS RECONCILIATION RISK
SIGNAL: Company holds customer funds (e-money or deposits) and cannot demonstrate
real-time or daily reconciliation between customer balances and segregated safeguarding
accounts. Any reconciliation break — even temporary — is a serious regulatory issue.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: FCA safeguarding requirements mandate that e-money institutions segregate
customer funds from their own funds at all times. A reconciliation break means customer
funds may not be protected in insolvency. This is the most serious operational compliance
failure for an EMI or neobank.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "How frequently do you reconcile customer funds to your safeguarding
account? What is your process when you identify a reconciliation break?"

10. FUNDING LINE RENEWAL RISK
SIGNAL: Lending company's primary funding facility is within 6 months of its renewal
date. The facility provider has not confirmed renewal terms. The company has no backup
facility and cannot continue originating at current pace without the primary line.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Funding line withdrawal is the most common cause of lending fintech
collapse — more common than credit losses. A lender without a committed funding facility
for at least 18 months ahead is operating with existential liquidity risk.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "When does your primary funding facility expire? What is your backup
funding plan? At what notice period can your lender withdraw the facility?"

11. INSURANCE CLAIMS MODEL UNVALIDATED AT SCALE
SIGNAL: Insurtech has grown GWP rapidly in the last 12 months but its claims model
was built on the first 12–18 months of data from a materially smaller book. The current
book size is 5–10x the size of the validation dataset.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Actuarial models trained on small datasets can misprice risk significantly.
A company writing £50M GWP whose pricing model was validated on £5M GWP data is
underwriting in largely uncharted territory. Loss ratios can spike sharply in the 12–18
months following a rapid scaling event.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "At what GWP size was your current pricing model validated against
actual claims data? How is your loss ratio trending in the most recent cohorts relative
to the model's predictions?"

12. INTERCHANGE REVENUE REGULATORY DEPENDENCY
SIGNAL: A neobank's primary revenue source is UK or EU interchange fees on debit or
credit transactions. Regulation has already capped UK debit interchange at 0.2% and
EU debit interchange at 0.2% under IFR. The company's financial model projects
interchange revenue at a rate that assumes the current cap is not further reduced.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Interchange is a politically and regulatorily sensitive revenue stream.
The UK and EU have already reduced it once; further reductions are a realistic risk.
A neobank with 70%+ of revenue from interchange and no credit or subscription revenue
is building on a potentially eroding foundation.
VERIFICATION: Calculate interchange as % of total revenue. If above 60%, ask: "What
is your plan if interchange is reduced by a further 50%?"

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE REGULATORY CLIFF
DESCRIPTION: A fintech company operates for 12–24 months under an exemption, e-money
licence from a partner bank, or regulatory tolerance while its own licence application
is in process. Revenue grows. The team assumes the licence will be granted. The FCA
rejects the application or grants it with conditions that require operational changes
that cost 12 months and £2M+ to implement. Revenue cannot grow during the remediation
period. Investors who funded on the assumption of a clean licence receive a company
that is commercially stalled and facing regulatory bills it was not budgeted for.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Licence application submitted more than 18 months ago with no
decision letter; FCA has issued Requests for Information more than twice; company
has changed external legal counsel mid-application; MLRO has resigned or been replaced
in the last 6 months; company describes the licence status as "expected shortly" without
a specific date.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any pre-licensed fintech where the licence is a condition of the
operational or revenue plan — i.e., the company cannot scale legally without it.

2. THE FRAUD SPIKE
DESCRIPTION: A payments or neobank company experiences rapid growth driven partly
by acquiring customers other regulated competitors rejected. Fraud controls are
calibrated for a smaller, better-screened book. At scale, fraud losses grow
non-linearly — criminals actively identify and target companies with weak controls.
The company faces a choice: tighten controls (which restricts growth) or absorb fraud
losses (which destroys economics). Investors discover the situation at a board meeting
where "fraud provision" appears as a new P&L line.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Customer acquisition cost is 40%+ below industry average for
the same segment (cheap acquisition often means scraping the segments better-screened
competitors rejected); fraud losses not disclosed separately in board reporting; customer
onboarding described as "instant" with no description of the underlying KYC checks;
chargebacks or disputes per 1,000 transactions rising quarter-on-quarter.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any consumer-facing payment or neobank company growing faster than 20%
MoM that has not disclosed its fraud loss rate in the submission.

3. THE CREDIT LOSS SPIRAL
DESCRIPTION: A lending company grows its loan book rapidly by relaxing underwriting
criteria. Initial cohorts perform well because they were conservatively underwritten.
Newer cohorts are looser. By the time the newer cohorts mature (12–18 months after
origination), loss rates are significantly above model predictions. The company must
simultaneously provision for elevated losses, tighten underwriting (reducing origination
volume), and raise equity to replace the consumed capital. Investors face a choice between
injecting further capital at distressed terms or accepting the company's failure.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Underwriting criteria have been changed (loosened) more than
twice in the last 18 months; average loan size has grown materially without a
corresponding improvement in the credit score distribution; vintage loss data shows
newer cohorts defaulting faster than older cohorts at the same loan age; the company
has raised equity capital to replace credit losses rather than to fund growth.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any lending company where the loan book has grown more than 3x in
18 months and the underwriting model has not been independently validated at the new scale.

4. THE BANKING PARTNER EXIT
DESCRIPTION: A neobank or embedded finance company builds its entire operational
infrastructure on a single banking partner. The partner bank is acquired, exits the
fintech-as-a-service market, receives its own regulatory sanction, or simply decides
the commercial arrangement is not worth the regulatory risk. It gives 90 days notice.
The fintech has 90 days to migrate all customer accounts, payment rails, and card
issuing to a new partner — a process that typically takes 9–18 months. The company
cannot serve new customers during the migration. Existing customers leave. The
company does not survive the migration period.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Single banking partner disclosed; partner bank has announced
strategic review of its BaaS business; news reports of the partner bank's regulatory
issues; fintech's contract with the banking partner has a termination clause of 90
days or less; the company has never tested a partial migration or built connectivity
with a second banking partner.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any neobank, BaaS consumer, or embedded finance company with a single
banking or infrastructure partner and no documented continuity plan.

5. THE TAKE RATE COMPRESSION DEATH SPIRAL
DESCRIPTION: A payments company competes primarily on price. To win enterprise clients,
it offers below-market take rates that are sustainable only at high volume. It raises
a Series B on projected TPV that assumes it can win large clients at those rates. The
large clients sign, the TPV grows, but the revenue per transaction is so low that gross
margin is structurally insufficient to fund the sales and engineering teams required
to serve the enterprise client base. The company is simultaneously too successful (TPV
growing) and commercially unsound (margin per transaction cannot support the cost base).
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Take rate is below 0.10% of TPV for an SMB-facing business;
large client contract has a pricing schedule that drops the effective take rate below
0.05% at volume milestones; gross margin below 40% on payment revenue; sales team
is offering pricing exceptions that are not in the standard rate card.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any payments company where a significant portion of TPV comes from
1–3 large contracts negotiated at rates materially below the standard pricing schedule.

6. THE REGULATORY SANDBOX ILLUSION
DESCRIPTION: A fintech company is built around a regulatory pilot, sandbox, or
temporary exemption that was granted by a regulator as an experiment, not as a
permanent policy position. The company grows within the sandbox and raises equity
capital. When the sandbox ends, the mainstream regulatory framework does not accommodate
the company's business model. The company must either fundamentally change its model
(destroying the product and economics it raised money for) or exit the market.
Common in: BNPL (built before BNPL regulation); crypto (built under FCA temporary
registration); AI-driven lending (built under model risk exemptions).
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Company describes its regulatory environment as a "pilot," a
"sandbox approval," or "operating under transitional provisions"; the underlying regulation
for the company's model has not been finalised; the company's legal counsel describes
its compliance as "good faith" rather than "fully compliant"; the submission contains
language like "we believe the FCA will permit this" rather than "we have confirmed with
the FCA that this is permitted."
WHEN TO APPLY: Any fintech operating in BNPL, crypto, AI-based underwriting, or any
other area where the regulatory framework has been described as "evolving."

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. REGULATORY LICENCE STATUS
QUESTION: "What is your exact regulatory status? Do you hold a licence directly, operate
under another company's licence, or rely on an exemption? If you are in application,
what stage is the application at and what is the FCA's expected timeline for a decision?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific FCA registration number; clear description of the licence type
and what it permits; if in application, a specific FCA case officer reference and written
evidence of progress.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're FCA-regulated" without specifying the licence type; "we operate
under a partner's licence" without naming the partner or describing the terms; "we're
in the process of applying" with no specifics.
REVEALS: Whether the company can legally continue its current operations and whether
regulatory risk is an existential threat or a managed compliance matter.

02. BANKING PARTNER STRUCTURE
QUESTION: "How many banking partners do you operate with? Who are they? What is the
contractual notice period if they decide to terminate? What percentage of your operational
infrastructure — settlement, card issuing, safeguarding — depends on each partner?"
STRONG ANSWER: Two or more live banking partners; longest notice period to terminate is
at least 12 months; active process to add a third partner; describes what happens
operationally if the primary partner gives notice.
WEAK ANSWER: Single banking partner; describes it as "a strong partnership" without
contractual detail; cannot confirm the termination notice period.
REVEALS: Whether the company has an existential single point of failure in its
infrastructure that is outside its direct control.

03. FRAUD RATE AND TREND
QUESTION: "What is your fraud loss rate as a percentage of TPV or total transaction
volume for each of the last four quarters? What is the trend? And what specific controls
have you added in the last 12 months?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific quarterly fraud rates; stable or declining trend; names specific
controls (machine learning fraud model, velocity checks, device fingerprinting); fraud
losses below 0.10% of TPV.
WEAK ANSWER: Describes fraud as "below industry average" without a specific figure;
cannot produce quarterly data; describes fraud controls as "industry standard."
REVEALS: Whether the company is managing fraud proactively or masking a growing problem
behind aggregate volume metrics.

04. CREDIT LOSS RATE BY VINTAGE
QUESTION: "For each loan cohort originated by quarter over the last 8 quarters, what is
the actual net loss rate at 12 months of age? How does this compare to your model
predictions at the time of origination?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific vintage loss curve for each quarter; loss rates below 4% at
12-month maturity for SMB; model predictions and actuals within 1 percentage point.
WEAK ANSWER: Reports a single blended loss rate across the entire book; cannot separate
vintages; describes the model as "performing within expectations" without specific figures.
REVEALS: Whether the underwriting model is predictive and whether newer cohorts are
performing differently from the cohorts that established the company's track record.

05. REGULATORY CAPITAL POSITION
QUESTION: "What is your current regulatory capital surplus above the minimum requirement?
At your current burn rate, when would you breach the minimum if you do not raise
additional equity? What is your buffer plan?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific capital surplus figure; runway to breach is more than 24 months
at current burn; has a committed facility or board-approved equity plan to maintain the
buffer.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're comfortably above the minimum" without a figure; cannot state
the breach point; describes it as "not a near-term concern."
REVEALS: Whether the company has modelled its regulatory capital requirement properly
and whether a capital breach is a realistic short-term risk.

06. TAKE RATE TRAJECTORY
QUESTION: "What is your take rate — revenue as a percentage of GMV or TPV — for each
of the last six quarters? What is driving the trend? What is the minimum take rate at
which your business model is financially viable?"
STRONG ANSWER: Stable or improving take rate; specific minimum floor with a clear
explanation of the cost structure that determines it; any compression explained by a
specific strategic decision (e.g., enterprise pricing) rather than structural market pressure.
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot produce a quarterly take rate history; describes pricing as "competitive"
without specifying the rate; suggests the take rate "has room to improve with scale."
REVEALS: Whether revenue growth is genuine or is TPV growth at deteriorating yield
that will not translate to profitable unit economics at scale.

07. AML / KYC PROGRAMME SPECIFICS
QUESTION: "Who is your MLRO? How many Suspicious Activity Reports did you file in the
last 12 months? What is your customer onboarding rejection rate for KYC failures? And
how do you monitor for transaction monitoring alerts?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names the MLRO and their background; specific SAR count; onboarding
rejection rate between 3–15% (lower suggests insufficient screening; higher suggests
customer acquisition is structurally challenged); automated transaction monitoring with
named vendor or proprietary system.
WEAK ANSWER: MLRO role "currently being filled"; no SAR count; describes KYC as
"automated" without specifics; onboarding rejection rate of 0% or not known.
REVEALS: Whether the company has a real compliance programme or whether it has
minimised compliance friction at the expense of regulatory compliance.

08. KEY PERSON RISK IN COMPLIANCE
QUESTION: "Is your MLRO or Chief Compliance Officer an approved person under FCA
Senior Managers and Certification Regime? What happens to your regulatory permissions
if they leave? Do you have succession coverage for the compliance leadership role?"
STRONG ANSWER: Named SMF16 (compliance oversight) or SMF17 (MLRO) approved person;
describes a documented succession plan; deputy MLRO named.
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot confirm whether compliance leadership is an FCA-approved person;
describes succession as "we'd hire quickly"; MLRO joined in last 3 months with no track
record in the role.
REVEALS: Whether a single compliance leadership departure could trigger a regulatory
event — the FCA requires approved persons to be in role and may restrict activities
if a key approved person leaves without a successor.

09. FUNDING LINE STRUCTURE (LENDING COMPANIES)
QUESTION: "What is the total size of your committed funding facility? When does it
expire? What is the advance rate and what covenants could trigger early repayment?
Do you have a backup facility? What is your origination plan if your primary funder
gives 90 days notice?"
STRONG ANSWER: Facility of at least 2x the current loan book; expiry at least 18 months
ahead; named backup facility or advanced discussions with a second funder; covenant
thresholds clearly understood and managed.
WEAK ANSWER: Facility size not disclosed; expiry within 12 months; no named backup
facility; describes the funder relationship as "strong" without contractual specifics.
REVEALS: Whether the lending business can continue operating if its primary funding
source changes its risk appetite or commercial terms.

10. INSURANCE CLAIMS MODEL VALIDATION
QUESTION: "At what GWP size was your current pricing model validated? Who validated it —
internally or by an independent actuary? How is the actual loss ratio in your most recent
cohorts tracking against model predictions? And what is your plan if actual losses
exceed model predictions by more than 10 percentage points?"
STRONG ANSWER: Independent actuarial validation at scale close to current GWP; specific
comparison of model predictions vs actual loss ratios by cohort; a documented response
plan for loss ratio deterioration.
WEAK ANSWER: "The model has been validated internally"; cannot specify at what GWP
size validation occurred; describes loss ratios as "performing well" without specific figures.
REVEALS: Whether the company is underwriting at scale with a validated risk model or
with a model that may be materially mispricing risk at current size.

11. REVENUE MODEL STRESS TEST (NEOBANK)
QUESTION: "If UK interchange regulation reduced debit interchange by a further 50%
tomorrow, what would that do to your annual revenue? What is your plan to replace
that revenue?"
STRONG ANSWER: Can quantify the interchange dependency; has a specific plan (credit
product revenue, subscription tier expansion, embedded lending) that would replace
it within 18 months; the plan is already in development.
WEAK ANSWER: "Interchange is only a portion of our revenue" without a specific figure;
describes a future product that would compensate but has no development timeline.
REVEALS: Whether the business is built on a structurally robust revenue mix or whether
it is exposed to a regulatory event that is not improbable.

12. PATH TO REGULATORY INDEPENDENCE
QUESTION: "If your banking partner exited and you needed to operate independently under
your own licence — or with a new banking partner — what would that require operationally
and financially? Have you modelled that scenario?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has modelled the scenario; knows the cost (typically £1–5M and 12–24
months for a new banking partner); has a documented operational continuity plan;
may have already initiated a relationship with a backup partner.
WEAK ANSWER: Has not modelled the scenario; describes the current partner relationship
as low-risk without addressing the hypothetical; "we'd cross that bridge if we came to it."
REVEALS: Whether the company's management team understands that their banking partner
is not a vendor — it is a lifeline — and whether they are managing that dependency
with the seriousness it deserves.

FCA AUTHORISATION TYPES AND TIMELINES:

Electronic Money Institution (EMI) — Full Authorisation:
  Permits: issuing e-money, providing payment services, safeguarding customer funds.
  Capital requirement: €350,000 minimum.
  Application timeline: 12–18 months typical from submission to decision.
  FCA approval rate: approximately 60–70% of submitted applications.
  Conditions commonly attached: restrictions on customer onboarding volume until
  operational controls are validated; requirement for a Skilled Person review.
  Key considerations for Alex: an EMI licence is the minimum viable licence for a
  neobank or consumer payments company. "Small EMI" registration (under £3M annual
  e-money in issue) does not permit holding customer funds in the same way.

Payment Institution (PI) — Full Authorisation:
  Permits: payment initiation, account information services, payment processing.
  Does NOT permit: issuing e-money or holding customer funds in segregated accounts.
  Capital requirement: €125,000 minimum.
  Application timeline: 9–15 months.
  Key considerations for Alex: a PI licence is insufficient for a company that wants
  to hold customer balances. If a company describes itself as "FCA-authorised" but
  holds customer funds under a PI licence, this is a compliance error.

Full Banking Licence (PRA + FCA dual-regulated):
  Permits: deposit-taking, lending, full banking services.
  Capital requirement: minimum £1M + ongoing risk-weighted assets calculation.
  Application timeline: 3–5 years from application to mobilisation licence.
  Post-mobilisation restrictions: banks operate under restrictions for up to 3 years
  before full authorisation is granted. Restricted licence limits deposit-taking.
  Key considerations for Alex: a company claiming a "banking licence application"
  that is less than 18 months old is at the very beginning of a 3–5 year process.
  Do not let management present a banking licence as a near-term milestone.

FCA Consumer Credit Authorisation:
  Permits: lending, credit brokering, debt collecting.
  Application timeline: 6–12 months.
  Key considerations for Alex: required for any consumer lending company. Not required
  for B2B or institutional lending. Check whether the lending company is authorised
  for the specific credit activities it is performing.

FCA Investment Management / Advisory Permissions:
  Permits: managing investments on behalf of clients, providing investment advice.
  Application timeline: 6–18 months depending on complexity.
  Key considerations for Alex: a wealthtech company managing money without these
  permissions is operating illegally. Always verify for wealthtech and robo-advisory deals.

OPEN BANKING — REGULATION AND OPPORTUNITY:

PSD2 Status (UK): The UK retained PSD2 post-Brexit under the Payment Services
  Regulations 2017. The FCA has been developing the next framework under the
  Edinburgh Reforms (2023) and consultation on Payment Services Regulation updates.
  Banks must provide FCA-registered AISPs and PISPs with API access.

PSD3 (EU — in force 2026): The EU is implementing PSD3, which strengthens open
  banking requirements, improves API quality standards, and extends SCA requirements.
  UK companies with EU operations should plan for PSD3 compliance by 2026.
  Key opportunity: PSD3 mandates higher-quality bank APIs across 27 EU member states —
  creates a structural tailwind for open banking infrastructure companies.

CONSUMER DUTY (FCA) — Effective July 2023:
  Requirement: All FCA-regulated firms must demonstrate they are delivering good
  outcomes for retail customers in four areas: products and services, price and value,
  consumer understanding, and consumer support.
  Impact on fintech: Consumer Duty significantly increases the compliance burden for
  neobanks, consumer lenders, and investment platforms. Firms must evidence outcomes
  — not just implement processes. The FCA has conducted enforcement actions for
  Consumer Duty failures.
  Alex flag: Any consumer-facing fintech that does not mention Consumer Duty in its
  compliance framework is operating with a significant FCA risk.

BNPL REGULATION (UK — Imminent):
  Status as of April 2026: BNPL regulation has been through multiple consultation
  rounds. The Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 includes powers to regulate BNPL.
  The FCA is expected to bring BNPL under Consumer Credit regulation, requiring:
  — Affordability checks before each BNPL transaction
  — FCA authorisation for BNPL providers
  — Mandatory credit agreement disclosures
  Impact on fintech: Any BNPL company built on a model that cannot withstand
  mandatory affordability checks faces a regulatory cliff when the rules land.
  Klarna, Clearpay, and Laybuy have all adjusted models in anticipation. New BNPL
  entrants face high regulatory uncertainty.
  Alex flag: PASS any BNPL company that cannot demonstrate its model is viable
  under the anticipated Consumer Credit Act framework.

CRYPTO ASSET REGULATION (UK — FCA):
  Current status: FCA crypto asset registration (under MLR 2017) has a ~70%+
  rejection rate for firms that have applied. FCA has published a roadmap for full
  crypto regulation under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 framework.
  The FCA's full crypto regulatory regime is expected 2025–2026.
  For stablecoins specifically: FCA and Bank of England have consulted on a payment
  stablecoin regime; systemic stablecoins (£10B+ in circulation) would be regulated
  by the Bank of England. Consumer stablecoins by the FCA.
  Alex flag: Any crypto company operating without FCA registration is operating
  illegally under UK money laundering regulations. Any crypto company operating under
  FCA registration should be treated with high regulatory scrutiny — not as a clean
  compliance status.

AI IN FINANCIAL SERVICES (FCA GUIDANCE):
  FCA Discussion Paper DP5/22 (2022) on AI in financial services established that:
  — Accountability: all AI decisions in regulated activities require a named human
    accountable person under SMCR.
  — Explainability: firms must be able to explain AI-driven decisions to customers.
  — Bias and fairness: AI models used in credit, insurance, or investment decisions
    must be tested for discriminatory outcomes.
  EU AI Act (in force 2024): AI systems used in credit scoring, insurance pricing,
  and financial services are classified as HIGH RISK under the EU AI Act.
  High-risk AI requirements: mandatory conformity assessment, human oversight,
  accuracy and robustness testing, and registration in the EU AI database.
  Alex flag: Any fintech using AI in credit underwriting, insurance pricing, or
  investment recommendation must demonstrate compliance with FCA AI guidance (UK)
  and EU AI Act high-risk requirements (EU operations). A company that describes its
  AI as "proprietary" without addressing explainability or fairness testing is
  carrying a material regulatory liability.

SANCTIONS COMPLIANCE:
  UK: Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (OFSI) maintains the UK sanctions
  list. All FCA-regulated firms must screen customers and transactions against OFSI.
  EU: European Commission sanctions list. Non-compliance carries criminal liability
  for directors.
  US: OFAC sanctions carry extraterritorial reach — a UK fintech processing USD
  transactions or serving US persons is subject to OFAC compliance obligations.
  Alex flag: Any fintech processing cross-border transactions that does not describe
  its sanctions screening programme (automated screening, escalation process, OFSI/OFAC
  coverage) is carrying an unquantified sanctions compliance risk. Ask specifically about
  exposure to high-risk jurisdictions and the frequency of sanctions list updates applied.
