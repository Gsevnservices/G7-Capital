NOTE ON PURPOSE:
Wrong calls are the most valuable training material in this library.
A correct YES or PASS confirms a pattern. A wrong call reveals a gap
in the analytical framework — a place where the right inputs were
available but the wrong conclusion was reached. Alex must study these
not to memorise outcomes, but to understand which signals were
available at the time and were either missed or discounted incorrectly.
The test for each wrong call: could a careful analyst, with only
the information available at the time of decision, have reached the
right conclusion? The answer is always yes. That is why it is a lesson.

WRONG CALL 011 — MISSED WIN: THE UNSEXY MARKET
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: MISSED WIN
PATTERN: Unsexy Market
SECTOR: Vertical SaaS — Waste Management and Environmental Compliance

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
A former operations director at a national waste management company
built a compliance and route management platform for UK skip hire,
hazardous waste, and liquid waste contractors. The product manages
waste transfer notes (a legal requirement under the Environmental
Protection Act), vehicle route optimisation, contractor license
tracking, and Environment Agency reporting in a single mobile-first
system. ARR: £480K. 34 customers (skip hire and hazardous waste
contractors with 10–80 vehicles). Average contract: £14K. NRR: 119%.
Annual churn: 8%. Growing 13% MoM for 14 months. No famous investors.
No press coverage. The deck uses photos of skips and industrial waste
vehicles. Raising £3M Series A at £11M pre-money.

THE DECISION MADE:
PASS. Reasoning given at the time: "The market is too fragmented and
operationally niche. Exit multiples in waste management services are
historically low. The customer profile (owner-operated skip hire
businesses) will be slow to adopt SaaS and difficult to expand.
No clear path to a large exit."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The Environment Agency announced mandatory digital waste tracking
(the Digital Waste Tracking Service) in 2022, with implementation
scheduled for 2024–2025. This regulation was publicly available
at the time of the decision. Every waste contractor in the UK —
approximately 34,000 registered waste carriers — will be legally
required to use a digital tracking system by the implementation date.
The company had already built the only purpose-built system compliant
with the forthcoming framework. The £480K ARR was built against
voluntary adoption; the regulation would make adoption mandatory
across a 34,000-contractor TAM. The submission mentioned the regulation
in a footnote. The analyst treated it as a future opportunity rather
than an immediate market-sizing catalyst. This signal was in the
submission. It was read and discounted.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The analyst applied the "unsexy market" aesthetic filter before
completing the market sizing. The skip hire photographs and the
owner-operator customer profile triggered an early PASS heuristic
based on category aesthetics. The regulatory catalyst in the footnote
was not evaluated against the market structure — it was noted and
moved past. The analyst failed to ask: "What happens to adoption
if this product is legally mandated?" The framework gap: regulatory
catalysts in footnotes must always be elevated to the primary analysis,
not left in footnotes.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
Whenever a submission mentions a regulation, legislative timeline,
or compliance requirement — even in a footnote — stop and evaluate
it as a potential mandatory adoption catalyst before assessing market
aesthetics. Ask: "If this regulation passes as described, what is
the addressable customer count that must adopt this product by law?"
If that number exceeds 1,000 paying customers at current ACV with
plausible adoption economics, the regulatory catalyst overrides
the market aesthetics assessment entirely.

OUTCOME:
The mandatory digital waste tracking regulation passed. Within 18
months of implementation, the company had 1,800 paying customers
and £2.8M ARR. A private equity firm backed them at £28M valuation.
The investors who passed at £11M pre-money watched a 2.5x step-up
they did not participate in. The exit four years later: £94M to a
waste management services group. 8.5x from the Series A price point.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a submission contains a mandatory regulatory catalyst in any
section — however peripheral — AND the company already has a compliant
product serving the regulated activity, do not evaluate the market on
current voluntary adoption. Calculate the legally mandated TAM. If
mandatory adoption economics justify a venture return, market aesthetics
are irrelevant. A skip hire company with a regulatory moat is more
defensible than a fintech without one.

WRONG CALL 012 — MISSED WIN: THE STRUGGLING SECOND-TIME FOUNDER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: MISSED WIN
PATTERN: Struggling Second-Time Founder
SECTOR: B2B SaaS — Insurance Claims Automation

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
Founder sold his first company — a workflow automation tool for HR
teams — for £18M after 4 years. His second company has been running
for 28 months. The product automates first-notification-of-loss
processing for mid-market insurance companies: when a policyholder
submits a claim, the system validates coverage, routes to the correct
adjuster team, and generates a provisional reserve estimate automatically.
ARR: £320K. 5 customers. Average contract: £64K. The company has
pivoted once (from a broader insurance operations product to this
specific claims intake workflow). Growth has been slow: 7% MoM for
the last 6 months. The deck is honest about the struggle. The founder
describes the pivot as "painful but necessary" and identifies specifically
why the prior product failed to find PMF (it required changes to the
insurer's existing core system; the claims intake workflow requires no
core system changes — it sits as a layer on top). Raising £3.5M at
£12M pre-money.

THE DECISION MADE:
PASS. Reasoning: "The metrics don't support a Series A. 7% MoM growth
and £320K ARR after 28 months suggests the market is not responding.
The pivot is concerning — it's the second major directional change.
The valuation does not reflect the risk."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The founder's root cause analysis of the pivot was available in the
submission and was specific, structural, and correct. The original
product required insurers to change their core claims management system
(a 12–18 month IT project). The new product required no core system
change — it sat above existing systems via API. This is not a direction
change based on investor pressure or market sentiment; it is a precise
architectural correction based on a buyer friction diagnosis that proved
accurate. The 5 customers signed under the new model all onboarded in
under 8 weeks (stated in the submission). Under the old model, 3 pilot
customers each took over 6 months to onboard and 2 churned during
implementation. The signal: onboarding velocity tripled under the new
architecture. This was available at the time. It was not weighted
correctly relative to the ARR and growth rate metrics.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The analyst applied the pivot fatigue heuristic (from the PASS framework)
too broadly. Not all pivots are created equal. The pivot fatigue pattern
applies when a founder abandons a customer base without a specific
structural reason. This founder identified a precise architectural
barrier (core system dependency), corrected it, and the onboarding
data confirmed the correction worked. The analyst pattern-matched
"pivot" to "uncertainty" without evaluating the quality of the
pivot analysis. Framework gap: the Type 4 ROOT CAUSE TEST exists
precisely for this situation. It was not applied. If it had been, the
specific vs asserted root cause distinction would have elevated this
to a Watch or Priority Review.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
When a repeat founder discloses a pivot, apply the Type 4 ROOT CAUSE
TEST immediately before evaluating the metrics. Determine: is the
root cause of the prior failure specific and structural (identifiable
from observable data) or asserted and attributional (self-serving
narrative without evidence)? If specific and structural, weight the
post-pivot operational data — onboarding time, implementation success
rate, early churn — more heavily than the ARR growth rate in the months
immediately following the pivot. A new product with 8-week onboarding
replacing a product with 6-month onboarding is a structurally significant
improvement, regardless of current ARR.

OUTCOME:
The company found PMF 6 months after the PASS decision. A tier-2 Lloyd's
market insurer signed a £200K contract. Three more followed within 4
months. ARR reached £1.2M within 8 months of the PASS. The company
raised a Series A at £22M — a 5x step-up from the passed valuation.
Exit 5 years later to a global insurance software group at £140M.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a repeat founder presents a pivot alongside a specific, structural
root cause analysis — and post-pivot operational data (not just revenue
data) shows measurable improvement in the identified friction point —
do not apply the pivot fatigue framework. Apply the Type 4 ROOT CAUSE
TEST. If the root cause is specific and the fix is verified by operational
data, give the company 3–6 more months of observation time rather than
a PASS at the current metrics.

WRONG CALL 013 — MISSED WIN: THE CONTRARIAN CATEGORY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: MISSED WIN
PATTERN: Contrarian Category
SECTOR: EdTech — Enterprise Skills Intelligence (B2B, not B2C)

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
October 2022. The consumer EdTech market has just collapsed: Byju's
valuation imploded, Chegg's stock dropped 50%, Coursera was under
pressure. Two ex-McKinsey partners who spent 10 years each in
workforce transformation built an enterprise skills intelligence
platform: it ingests a company's job descriptions, performance review
data, and learning management system records, maps actual skills
held by each employee against skills required for each role and
projected required skills in 3 years, and generates a board-level
workforce risk report. The product is B2B only: sold to CHROs and
Chief People Officers at companies with 2,000+ employees. ARR: £740K.
7 enterprise customers. Average contract: £106K. NRR: 126%.
No consumer exposure whatsoever. Raising £5M Series A.

THE DECISION MADE:
PASS. Reasoning stated internally: "EdTech sentiment is extremely
negative right now. LPs are asking questions about every EdTech
position. The market timing is wrong to lead a round in this category.
The consumer EdTech comparison will contaminate the Series B story."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The company was not EdTech. It was workforce analytics — a category
that overlaps structurally with HR technology, people analytics, and
enterprise risk management. Its buyers were CHROs and CFOs measuring
workforce capability risk, not individual learners paying subscription
fees. The submission clearly stated: "We do not sell to individuals.
We have no consumer revenue. Our buyer is the CHRO and our economic
buyer is the CFO." The company's competitive set — Workday, Gloat,
Eightfold AI — was not in the EdTech category. The EdTech category
collapse was caused by consumer subscription models with high CAC and
low retention. This company had B2B enterprise contracts with 126%
NRR. The category label "EdTech" was applied by the analyst as a
shortcut. The product was enterprise HR technology with a learning
intelligence layer. The distinction was in the submission.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The analyst used category label reasoning rather than business model
reasoning. "EdTech" triggered a negative prior based on recent headline
events (Byju's, Chegg, Coursera) without examining whether those events
were structurally relevant to this specific company. They were not.
Consumer EdTech failed because of consumer subscription economics.
This company had none of those characteristics. The cognitive error:
applying a category sentiment filter before completing a business model
classification. Framework gap: the initial screen should classify the
business model (B2B enterprise, B2C subscription, marketplace) before
applying category sentiment. A company with £106K ACV enterprise
contracts is not in the same market as a consumer EdTech subscription
at £20/month.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
Never apply category-level sentiment filters before completing a
business model classification. Before assigning a category label,
identify: (1) who is the buyer — an individual or an organisation?
(2) what is the pricing model — subscription, seat licence, or ACV
contract? (3) who are the direct competitors as named by the company?
If the buyer is an enterprise, the ACV is above £50K, and the named
competitors are enterprise software companies, the category label
is "enterprise B2B SaaS" regardless of what consumer adjacency the
technology superficially resembles. Apply category sentiment only
within the correct business model classification.

OUTCOME:
The company raised from a US growth fund 3 months later at the same
valuation. ARR reached £3.2M within 18 months. Workday acquired
the company for £180M — 11x the Series A post-money. The investors
who passed on category sentiment grounds received zero economic benefit
from the outcome they had helped build by passing.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a submission is categorised superficially in a market where
sentiment is negative, but the business model (buyer type, ACV, and
named competitive set) places the company in a structurally different
category, do not apply the category sentiment filter. Classify by
business model first: B2B enterprise with £50K+ ACV contracts is not
the same market as consumer subscriptions regardless of the technology
domain. Category sentiment is only relevant within the correct
business model category.

WRONG CALL 014 — MISSED WIN: THE BORING METRICS HIDING
                  EXCEPTIONAL RETENTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: MISSED WIN
PATTERN: Boring Metrics Hiding Exceptional Retention
SECTOR: B2B SaaS — Engineering Document Control for Construction

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
A former project manager on major UK infrastructure projects (Crossrail,
Tideway Tunnel) built a document control and revision management platform
for large construction projects. When a civil engineering project has
3,000 active drawings and 14 subcontractors, a change to Revision B
of a structural drawing must be propagated correctly to every party
who holds a copy. Errors cost £50,000–500,000 per incident. The product
automates this propagation and creates an immutable audit trail.
ARR: £380K. 11 customers (major contractors: Balfour Beatty, Laing
O'Rourke, Morgan Sindall). Average contract: £35K. NRR: 134%.
Growth rate: 16% MoM. No famous investors. No London office. Team
based in Bristol. Raising £2.5M Series A.

THE DECISION MADE:
PASS. Reasoning: "Growth rate of 16% MoM is below our threshold for
Series A (we require 20%+). ARR of £380K after 24 months is slower
than we'd expect for a product with this team quality. The construction
sector has historically low tech adoption rates and the sales cycle
will be long. We'd revisit at £600K ARR."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
NRR of 134% across 11 customers over 24 months. The submission included
a cohort table showing D90, D180, and D365 retention by customer cohort.
D365 retention across all cohorts: 100% (no customer had churned since
founding). The 134% NRR was driven by project expansion: when a
contractor used the product on one project and it saved them from a
significant revision error, they mandated it on every subsequent
project. The submission included a case study: one contractor started
with one project (£18K), expanded to 7 simultaneous projects (£126K)
over 18 months — a 7x revenue expansion from a single customer.
The growth rate of 16% MoM was slow because sales cycles were 3–4
months and each new customer was a major contractor requiring IT
security approval. But once a contractor signed, they never left
and they expanded substantially. This product did not have a growth
problem — it had a top-of-funnel constraint that would resolve as
the sales team scaled. The cohort table was in the submission.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The analyst applied a minimum growth rate threshold (20% MoM) as
a hard filter before examining retention quality. The threshold was
designed as a proxy for product-market fit — strong growth indicates
the market is pulling the product. But in enterprise markets with
long sales cycles, growth rate at small ARR bases is a function of
sales team size and deal cycle duration, not of PMF. A company with
100% D365 retention and 134% NRR has proven PMF more conclusively
than a company with 25% MoM growth and 85% annual retention. The
framework gap: growth rate thresholds should not be applied before
examining retention data. Retention is the primary signal; growth is
the accelerator.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
Never apply a minimum growth rate filter as a primary screen. Apply
growth rate as a secondary filter only after examining retention.
The correct sequence is: (1) evaluate NRR and cohort retention data
first; (2) if NRR is above 120% and annual retention is above 90%,
classify as strong PMF regardless of growth rate; (3) then evaluate
growth rate in the context of sales cycle length — a 3–4 month sales
cycle means a small team can close only 3–4 customers per month;
(4) if growth rate is below threshold but PMF is confirmed and the
bottleneck is identifiable (sales team size, sales cycle duration),
model what growth rate becomes with scaled sales investment and
evaluate the investment case on projected not current growth.

OUTCOME:
The company reached £600K ARR in the next 5 months without any new
investment, using only the seed capital remaining. They raised a
Series A at £18M — a 7x step-up from the valuation at which they
were passed. 100% D365 retention held through the Series A. Exit 4
years later to a major engineering software group at £85M.

PATTERN LESSON:
When NRR exceeds 120% and D365 cohort retention is above 90%,
do not apply a minimum growth rate filter. Exceptional retention in
an enterprise product with a documented long sales cycle is a stronger
PMF signal than high growth rate with moderate retention. The correct
model: confirm PMF via retention, then evaluate whether capital can
unlock growth by resolving the identified bottleneck. Growth rate
in the absence of retention data is meaningless; retention in the
absence of growth data is investable.

WRONG CALL 015 — MISSED WIN: THE REGULATORY WINDOW OPENER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: MISSED WIN
PATTERN: Regulatory Window Opener
SECTOR: FinTech — Open Banking Data Aggregation for Mortgage Lending

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
A team of two — a former FCA policy official and a bank open banking
lead — built a mortgage affordability verification platform using
PSD2 open banking data. Instead of requiring applicants to submit
3 months of bank statements (manual, slow, 40% of cases require
resubmission), the platform pulls live transaction data directly
from the applicant's bank via open banking API, categorises income
and expenditure automatically, and generates an FCA-compliant
affordability report in 4 minutes. ARR: £240K. 4 mortgage broker
customers. Average contract: £60K. Growing. Raising £3M Series A.
Very early stage. Limited traction data.

THE DECISION MADE:
PASS. Reasoning: "Too early. Only 4 customers. The open banking
ecosystem is still too fragmented — bank coverage is incomplete
and consumer consent rates are low. We'd revisit at Series A with
more evidence of broker adoption."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The FCA published Mortgage Market Review guidance in the same quarter
as this pitch explicitly encouraging lenders to use open banking
data for affordability assessments. More specifically: the FCA's
Consumer Duty regulation (effective July 2023) required mortgage
lenders to demonstrate they had taken reasonable steps to assess
affordability accurately — a duty that manual bank statement review
was increasingly failing to meet in FCA supervisory visits. The
submission disclosed that one of the 4 broker customers had adopted
the platform following a "supervisory letter" from the FCA raising
concerns about their manual affordability process. This single detail
— a real FCA supervisory action driving adoption — was available in
the submission. It indicated that regulatory pressure was converting
the theoretical market (brokers who would benefit from open banking)
into an urgent market (brokers who were receiving FCA letters about
their current process). The founding team's FCA policy background
gave them early sight of enforcement direction. This was in the deck.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The analyst evaluated the market based on current open banking
adoption rates (which were indeed fragmented in 2021) rather than
on regulatory enforcement trajectory. The "too early" conclusion
was correct as a market timing assessment in 2019. By 2021, the FCA
Consumer Duty and the supervisory letter pattern changed the timeline.
The founding team's regulatory background was treated as domain
expertise (positive signal) rather than as regulatory foresight
(primary investment signal). The analyst did not ask: "Why does
a former FCA policy official know this market is about to move?"
Framework gap: when a founder's background is regulatory, their
timing thesis requires regulatory due diligence, not just market
adoption analysis.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
When a founding team includes individuals with direct regulatory
experience (former regulator, former compliance director, former
policy official), treat their market timing claim as a regulatory
intelligence signal requiring specific evaluation. Ask: what specific
regulatory development is this team aware of that creates urgency?
Request the specific FCA guidance, legislation, or enforcement trend
they are referencing. If a current customer cites a regulator action
as the reason for adoption (as the supervisory letter did here),
treat this as a mandatory catalyst signal and apply the regulatory
catalyst framework: calculate mandatory or urgency-driven TAM rather
than voluntary adoption TAM.

OUTCOME:
Consumer Duty came into force in July 2023. Mortgage lenders and
brokers received FCA supervisory guidance requiring documented
affordability evidence. The company signed 42 new broker customers
in the 6 months following implementation. ARR reached £2.8M.
A US fintech infrastructure investor led a £12M Series A. Exit
to a mortgage technology platform at £75M — 25x the Series A
post-money for the investors who did invest.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founding team has direct regulatory experience AND a current
customer cites a specific regulatory enforcement action as their
reason for adoption, this is a regulatory window opener signal.
Do not evaluate market timing based on current voluntary adoption
rates. Apply the regulatory catalyst framework: identify the specific
regulation, map the enforcement timeline, calculate the population
of entities that will face mandatory compliance pressure within
24 months. If that population at current ACV generates a venture
return, the market is not too early — it is exactly on time.

WRONG CALL 016 — FAILED INVESTMENT: THE CHARISMATIC FRAUDSTER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: FAILED INVESTMENT
PATTERN: Charismatic Fraudster
SECTOR: B2B SaaS — Supply Chain Risk Management

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
The founder presented as a world-class operator: ex-Goldman Sachs
supply chain finance, followed by 5 years building risk systems for
a global logistics company. His platform monitors supplier financial
health in real time — integrating credit agency data, Companies House
filings, news sentiment, and payment behaviour signals to flag supplier
distress before it disrupts the buyer's production. ARR stated as
£1.4M. 11 customers including two named FTSE 250 companies. NRR: 131%.
Three references provided: a CPO, a Head of Procurement, and a supplier
risk director — all positive. The founder presented with exceptional
clarity and self-assurance. The deck was impeccable. Investment made:
£5M Series A.

THE DECISION MADE:
INVESTED. Reasoning: "Exceptional founder with deep domain credibility.
Strong ARR with meaningful enterprise names. Reference checks positive.
The product addresses a real post-COVID supply chain risk pain point.
Team has the right background."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
Three signals were available at the time of investment and were not
pursued with sufficient rigour. First: the three reference contacts
were all provided by the founder. Industry standard for reference
checking is to find at least one reference independently — someone
not provided by the founder, sourced from the investor's own network
or from LinkedIn connections at the customer companies. This was not
done. Second: the two named FTSE 250 companies appeared in the deck
but their procurement or supply chain heads were not part of the
reference list. The investor's network included two people at one
of the named companies. They were not contacted. Third: the ARR
figure of £1.4M was not verified against bank statements or audited
accounts. The contract schedule provided showed £1.4M but was a Word
document, not a data room export from an accounting system. The due
diligence checklist marked "contract schedule reviewed" — it had been
seen, but not verified against source data.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The founder's charisma and presentation quality compressed the
due diligence timeline. The investor team felt confident after the
founder meetings and treated the reference checks as confirmatory
(expecting positive results) rather than investigative (trying to
find disconfirming evidence). Provided references are coached
references — this is true even in non-fraudulent situations. The
cognitive bias: charisma reduces verification effort precisely
when it should increase it. A founder who is exceptionally
compelling, whose deck is flawless, and whose story is consistent
without any hesitation is a founder who has rehearsed the narrative.
That is either an excellent operator or a skilled deceiver. The
verification required to distinguish them is the same in either case.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
Alex's output must flag any investment where the following conditions
are simultaneously true: (1) the founder is described or presents
as exceptionally charismatic or polished; (2) all references are
founder-provided; (3) ARR is not verified against bank statements
or accounting system export; (4) named enterprise customers have
not been independently contacted. In the screening note, flag:
"VERIFICATION REQUIRED — charisma increases not decreases due
diligence rigor. Require: one independently sourced reference not
provided by founder; ARR verification against bank statements;
direct contact with named enterprise accounts before closing."
This flag does not mean PASS — it means the verification requirement
must be completed before investment.

OUTCOME:
18 months post-investment, the Series B process began. A prospective
Series B investor contacted the named FTSE 250 customers independently.
One confirmed they were a customer but at £80K, not the £300K in the
contract schedule. The other had not heard of the company. The ARR

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder demonstrates exceptional charisma, presentation polish,
and narrative consistency, this is an increased verification trigger,
not a confidence signal. Apply: require at least one independently

the investor's own network or LinkedIn contacts at named customers.
Require ARR verification against bank statements or accounting system
export, not a founder-prepared contract schedule. Charisma is not
a substitute for verification — it is a reason to verify more.

                  WAS ONE CUSTOMER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: FAILED INVESTMENT
PATTERN: Hockey Stick from One Customer
SECTOR: B2B SaaS — Energy Management for Commercial Real Estate

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
An energy management platform for commercial property: the product
connects to building management systems, meters, and HVAC controllers
to optimise energy consumption in real time, reducing energy bills
by an average of 18–23% with no capital expenditure required. ARR
growth was striking: £180K in January, £820K by July of the same
year — a 4.5x increase in 6 months. The deck showed a steep ARR
curve. NRR: 142%. 14 customers. Raising £6M Series A in August.

THE DECISION MADE:
INVESTED. Reasoning: "The hockey stick ARR growth is exceptional.
Energy efficiency is a compelling regulatory and commercial trend.
18–23% cost reduction with no CapEx is an easy enterprise sale.
NRR of 142% shows strong expansion."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The submission broke down ARR by customer in an appendix. The appendix
was reviewed but not analysed. If the 14 customer ARRs in the appendix
had been summed and compared to the total ARR figure, it would have
been apparent that one customer — a large commercial property fund
managing a 40-building portfolio — represented £520K of the £820K ARR.
That one customer accounted for 63% of total ARR. Furthermore, the
contract note for that customer showed a 90-day termination clause
(standard for pilot-phase property fund contracts, which can be
terminated if the energy savings underperform). The ARR figure was
real. The concentration was disclosed in the appendix. The termination
clause was in the contract note in the appendix. Neither was flagged
in the main analysis.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The hockey stick ARR chart produced a strong anchoring effect. The
analyst saw the curve, noted the 14 customers, and accepted the NRR
figure without disaggregating the ARR by customer. The appendix was
reviewed for completeness (all pages seen) but not for analytical
signal extraction (no customer-level analysis performed). The customer
appendix exists in most submissions for this purpose — to allow
customer-level concentration analysis. It was treated as a supporting
document rather than a primary analytical input. Framework gap: ARR
curves should never be accepted without disaggregation by customer.
Any ARR spike (defined as more than 40% ARR growth in a single quarter)
requires identification of the source before it is treated as evidence
of acceleration.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
When a submission shows an ARR spike — more than 40% growth in a
single quarter — flag immediately: "ARR spike requires source
identification." In the analysis, reconstruct the ARR by customer
using whatever data is available (customer appendix, case studies,
reference list). Calculate: what percentage of total ARR is
attributable to the largest single customer? If one customer exceeds
30% of ARR, apply the Customer Concentration Trap framework. Check
the contract for that customer for termination clauses, pilot status,
or non-standard renewal terms. If the customer has a 90-day or
shorter termination right, flag: "ARR CONCENTRATION RISK — [X]%
of ARR is in one account with [termination clause]. Underlying
ARR ex-concentration: [recalculated figure]."

OUTCOME:
The large property fund's energy savings in year one came in at 14%
— below the 18–23% headline claim, which was an average across all
buildings including newer, more efficient ones. The fund's asset
manager decided not to renew at month 11 and exercised the 90-day
termination clause. ARR dropped from £820K to £300K overnight.
The company rebuilt over 18 months but the Series B was raised at
a flat valuation to the Series A. Fund returned 1.1x.

PATTERN LESSON:
When ARR growth exceeds 40% in a single quarter, do not accept the
aggregate ARR figure. Disaggregate by customer. If one customer
represents more than 30% of ARR, apply the Customer Concentration
Trap framework and check the contract for termination rights. A
hockey stick caused by one contract is not a growth signal — it is
a concentration risk. Recalculate ARR excluding the concentrated
customer before evaluating the investment case.

WRONG CALL 018 — FAILED INVESTMENT: THE MARKET THAT
                  NEVER ARRIVED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: FAILED INVESTMENT
PATTERN: Market That Never Arrived
SECTOR: PropTech — Commercial Real Estate Tokenisation

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
A platform enabling fractional ownership of commercial real estate
through blockchain-based tokenisation: a £20M office building can
be divided into 200,000 tokens at £100 each, allowing retail
investors to own a fractional interest in a yield-generating asset
historically available only to institutional investors. The platform
handles token issuance, secondary trading, and yield distribution.
FCA-regulated (Electronic Money Institution licence held). ARR:
£280K from platform fees and token issuance. 3 properties tokenised.
£4.2M of investor capital deployed into tokens. Growing. The market
timing thesis: institutional adoption of tokenised real estate was
forecast by McKinsey, Deloitte, and the FCA itself to reach £1.4
trillion globally by 2027. Investment made: £4M Series A in 2021.

THE DECISION MADE:
INVESTED. Reasoning: "The regulatory framework is in place, the
platform works, and the institutional adoption forecasts are credible.
This is a first-mover position in a market that multiple credible
sources confirm will be significant within 5 years."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The market timing thesis was built entirely on analyst forecasts,
not on evidence of institutional demand. The submission included
no institutional LP or pension fund that had participated in a
tokenised real estate product. The three tokenised properties were
funded by retail investors, not institutions. The institutional
adoption forecasts cited McKinsey (2019), Deloitte (2020), and an
FCA discussion paper (2021) — all of which predicted adoption "within
5 years" without specifying any institutional buyer who had committed
to participating. A search of the public record at the time of
investment would have found: zero institutional property funds in the
UK with a published tokenisation strategy; zero pension fund trustee
board decisions approving tokenised assets; and no HMRC tax treatment
guidance for tokenised property (a prerequisite for institutional
adoption). The market timing evidence available at the time pointed
to analyst enthusiasm, not institutional readiness.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The investment committee treated credible analyst forecasts (McKinsey,
Deloitte) as evidence of market timing rather than as opinions about
market timing. There is a critical distinction: analyst forecasts
are hypotheses, not commitments. The question should have been:
"Who has committed capital to this market, not who has predicted it?"
Framework gap: apply the Prior Attempt Rule from Framework 3. Commercial
real estate tokenisation had been attempted 3 times in the prior 5 years
(Meridio, Harbour, RealBlocks — all early-stage attempts that failed
to achieve institutional adoption). Three prior attempts without a
single institutional commitment was a -3 timing confidence adjustment
that reduced the Market Timing score to 4–5/10. This calculation
was not performed.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
When an investment thesis depends on market adoption that has been
predicted by analysts but not yet commenced, apply two tests:
(1) Prior Attempt Rule — how many prior companies attempted this
market and why did they fail to achieve the predicted adoption?
Three or more prior attempts is a -3 timing confidence penalty.
(2) Institutional commitment test — has any institution that would
need to participate in this market (pension funds, sovereign wealth
funds, institutional REITs) made a public commitment, published
a strategy, or committed capital? Analyst forecasts from third
parties are not institutional commitments. If no institution has
committed, the market remains theoretical regardless of the
quality of the forecast.

OUTCOME:
By 2024, commercial real estate tokenisation remained a retail
investor product in the UK. No major pension fund or institutional
REIT had adopted tokenised real estate. HMRC published draft guidance
in 2023 but full clarity remained pending. The platform's retail
investor base grew modestly but institutional adoption — the thesis
on which the £4M was invested — did not materialise within the
investment horizon. The company was acqui-hired by a property
management software group for £3.2M — a 0.8x return.

PATTERN LESSON:
When an investment thesis depends on market adoption that analysts
have predicted but that has not yet commenced, apply the Prior
Attempt Rule and the institutional commitment test before accepting
the timing thesis. Analyst forecasts are not evidence of market
readiness — they are hypotheses. Three or more prior attempts without
institutional adoption is a -3 timing confidence adjustment regardless
of forecast quality. Require at least one institutional buyer commitment
(published strategy, capital allocation, or pilot) before accepting
that the market is ready.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: FAILED INVESTMENT
PATTERN: Coached Reference Customer
SECTOR: B2B SaaS — Mental Health Benefits Platform for Employers

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
An employer mental health benefits platform: companies pay a per-seat
licence and employees access therapy, coaching, and wellbeing content
through the platform. A B2B model targeting HR directors in companies
with 100–2,000 employees. ARR: £680K. 9 employer customers. Average
contract: £75K. NRR: 114%. Three reference calls conducted before
investment: (1) an HR Director at a 500-person tech company; (2) a
Head of People at a professional services firm; (3) a Chief People
Officer at a manufacturing business. All three gave glowing reviews.
Investment made: £4.5M Series A.

THE DECISION MADE:
INVESTED. Reasoning: "Reference checks were unanimously positive.
The product is mission-driven and addresses real employee wellbeing
need. ARR and NRR are solid. Team has the right HR and clinical
background."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
Two signals were available and not pursued. First: all three references
were provided by the founder and scheduled through the founder's
assistant. No independent reference was sought from any of the 9
named customers. The reference list provided by the founder included
the company names of the three referees. A LinkedIn search of the
three individuals at the time would have shown that two of the three
had personal connections to the founder predating the company's

company; the CPO had been at university with the founder's co-founder.
These connections were on public LinkedIn profiles. Second: the
9-customer ARR of £680K implied an average contract of £75K. Three
of the customers were named in the deck. The fourth customer reference
call was offered but declined (the investor felt three was sufficient).
Post-investment, it emerged that two of the three companies that gave
references were personal friends of the founder who had signed nominal
contracts at discounted rates. Their combined actual ARR contribution:
£62K. Actual arm's-length commercial ARR: approximately £350K.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The reference check process was treated as a confirmatory step rather
than an investigative one. Three positive references were accepted
without: (a) verifying the personal relationship history between
referees and the founding team; (b) independently finding one reference
not provided by the founder; or (c) requesting a fourth call from
the offered list. The LinkedIn check would have taken 15 minutes.
The relationship disclosures were on public profiles. The framework
gap: reference calls conducted without first mapping the personal
relationship between the referee and the founder are not independent
references — they are testimony of unknown provenance.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
Flag in the screening note whenever all references are founder-provided:

call, verify on LinkedIn whether the referee has a pre-existing
personal connection to any member of the founding team (prior
employer, university, prior company board). If any reference has
a pre-existing personal connection, treat their reference as a
character reference, not a commercial reference, and require
it to be supplemented by an independently sourced reference.
Independently sourced means: the investor identifies a customer
from the publicly named customer list and contacts them directly,
without founder introduction or scheduling.

Post-investment due diligence for a Series B 16 months later

could not raise. Bridge was provided. The company was acquired by

of the total capital invested.

PATTERN LESSON:
When all reference contacts are founder-provided, LinkedIn-verify
the relationship between each referee and the founding team before
the reference call. If any referee has a pre-existing personal
connection to any founder, that call is not an independent
commercial reference. Require at least one independently sourced
reference — identified and contacted directly by the investor,
not scheduled through the founder — before committing to investment.
Personal connections on LinkedIn profiles are public information.
There is no excuse for not checking.

                  NEEDED THE FOUNDER TO SELL IT
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TYPE: FAILED INVESTMENT
PATTERN: Founder-Dependent Sales Motion
SECTOR: B2B — Cybersecurity Consulting and SaaS (vCISO Platform)

WHAT THE DEAL LOOKED LIKE:
A former Chief Information Security Officer from a FTSE 100 company
built a platform and service for mid-market companies who need CISO-level
security leadership but cannot afford a full-time CISO at £200K+/year.
The product provides: a virtual CISO engagement (monthly retainer),
a security posture assessment platform (SaaS), and board-level reporting
tools. ARR: £1.1M. 8 enterprise customers. Average contract: £138K.
NRR: 122%. All 8 deals closed by the founder personally. Raising
£5M Series A. The deck specifically highlights the founder's ability
to "speak board language" and close enterprise security deals that
others cannot. Team of 9. No VP Sales yet — the founder intends to
hire one with Series A capital.

THE DECISION MADE:
INVESTED. Reasoning: "The ARR and NRR are strong. The founder's
ability to close enterprise deals demonstrates that the product
has a genuine enterprise value proposition. A VP Sales hire will
scale what the founder has built."

THE SIGNAL THAT WAS MISSED OR IGNORED:
The signal was in the deck. In the "Why We Win" section, the founder
wrote: "Our success in closing enterprise security deals comes from
my ability to engage at C-suite level and demonstrate a depth of
security expertise that typical sales representatives cannot replicate."
This sentence identified the problem it was meant to solve. It stated
explicitly that the close rate was attributable to the founder's
specific expertise and C-suite relationships — not to a product
that any competent enterprise salesperson could sell. The founder
intended this as a strength statement. It was a scalability warning.
A security platform sold on the basis of one individual's C-suite
trust and technical credibility is not a product with a repeatable
sales motion — it is a high-value consulting service packaged as SaaS.
The sentence was in the deck. It was treated as evidence of the
founder's credibility rather than as a scalability risk.

WHY THE WRONG CALL WAS MADE:
The analyst conflated founder sales execution with product-market
fit. All 8 deals being closed by the founder was noted but interpreted
as "founder is working hard" rather than "the sales motion is founder-
dependent." The key test — can someone other than the founder close
this deal? — was not asked. There was no evidence in the submission
of any deal attempt by a non-founder team member, any partner referral,
or any inbound lead that converted without founder involvement. The
decision to "hire a VP Sales" post-investment was accepted as a plan
without being stress-tested against the founder's own admission that
the close rate depended on specific personal expertise.

WHAT ALEX MUST DO DIFFERENTLY:
When all closed deals are founder-led and the submission attributes
sales success to specific founder qualities (relationship depth, domain
credibility, C-suite access), flag: "SALES REPEATABILITY UNPROVEN."
Before treating founder sales success as evidence of a scalable
commercial motion, require: evidence of at least one deal closed by
a non-founder team member; or a pipeline of inbound leads not sourced
from the founder's personal network that have progressed to commercial
conversation without founder involvement. If neither exists, note in
the screening output: "All revenue is founder-generated. VP Sales hire
required before Series A to demonstrate sales motion is repeatable
independent of founder. At current stage, this is a founder consulting
business with a SaaS layer, not a scalable enterprise SaaS business."
The question to ask explicitly: "Can your head of sales close this
deal without you in the room?" If the answer is no, the sales motion
is the founder.

OUTCOME:

enterprise outbound conversations in the first 6 months. Converted
0. Brought into 4 of the founder's pipeline deals as a supporting
presence. Converted 2. The conversion rate without the founder:
0%. With the founder: 50% (same as before). The VP Sales resigned
after 9 months, citing the inability to operate independently of
the founder. A second VP Sales was hired with the same outcome.
NRR held (the founder managed all renewals personally) but new

PATTERN LESSON:
When all revenue is founder-generated and the founder attributes
sales success to personal expertise or relationship depth that
a hired salesperson cannot replicate, require evidence of at least
one non-founder commercial win before treating the sales motion
as scalable. Do not accept a VP Sales hire plan as evidence of
future scalability — it is a hypothesis. The question is not
"will you hire a VP Sales?" but "has anyone other than you closed
a deal?" If the answer is no, the sales motion is the founder.
Growth-stage valuations require a growth-stage sales motion.
