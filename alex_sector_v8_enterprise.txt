SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Enterprise Software):
— Primary buyer is a procurement team, IT function, legal department,
  or C-suite — not a single business owner or individual contributor
— ACV is £100K or above, or the company explicitly targets Fortune 500,
  FTSE 100, government departments, NHS trusts, or global banks
— Sales cycle is 6 months or longer from first meeting to signed contract
— Contract includes custom legal terms, data processing agreements,
  enterprise SLA, or multi-year commitment structure
— Procurement involves security review, IT due diligence, legal sign-off,
  and/or compliance certification check before any commercial agreement

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A company targeting SMBs with a self-serve product is B2B SaaS, not
  Enterprise — apply the SaaS Library. Enterprise requires a named
  enterprise buyer and a bespoke procurement process.
— A company with enterprise logos as customers but ACV below £20K is
  a mid-market SaaS product, not enterprise — the procurement dynamics
  are fundamentally different.
— A company selling to enterprise via a reseller channel without direct
  enterprise engagement is a channel business, not an enterprise business —
  the sales cycle, implementation risk, and NRR profile all differ.
— A Vertical SaaS company selling to enterprise should apply both
  this library and the B2B SaaS Library. Use this library for the
  procurement, implementation, and contract risk assessment.

THE MOST IMPORTANT ENTERPRISE QUESTION ALEX MUST ASK FIRST:

IS THIS A LAND-AND-EXPAND MODEL OR A FULL-DEPLOYMENT MODEL?

LAND AND EXPAND: Initial contract is a beachhead — one department,
one use case, one geography. The value proposition is to prove ROI
at small scale before expanding to the full organisation. Win the
initial deal, prove value, then sell upward and outward.
Financial characteristic: initial ACV is small (£50–200K); expansion
ACV potential is large (£500K–£5M+). NRR should be 120–150%+ if
the product delivers on its promise.
Risk: if the product does not deliver within 6–12 months at the
beachhead site, expansion is blocked and the contract is at risk
of non-renewal at year 1.

FULL DEPLOYMENT: The initial contract is a full-organisation deployment.
All users, all features, full implementation from day one.
Financial characteristic: initial ACV is large (£500K–£5M+); expansion
comes from adding modules, geographies, or new use cases.
Risk: implementation failure at full-deployment scale is catastrophic —
there is no beachhead fallback. A failed full deployment can result in
a £1M+ loss of ARR, public reputational damage, and legal exposure.

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. ENTERPRISE RESOURCE PLANNING (ERP) AND CORE SYSTEMS
Signals: product manages core business operations — finance, HR, payroll,
supply chain, or manufacturing; the word "system of record" appears;
buyer title includes CIO, CFO, or CHRO; implementation is measured in
months not weeks; the competitive set is SAP, Oracle, Workday, or legacy
on-premise software.
Key characteristic: longest sales cycles in enterprise software (12–24
months typical); highest implementation complexity; highest switching costs
once live (replacing an ERP is a multi-year programme); ACV typically
£500K–£5M for mid-market; £1M–£10M+ for global enterprise.
TAM: large (global ERP market £50B+) but highly segmented by vertical.
Key risk: implementation failure. An ERP that fails to go live on schedule
triggers customer escalation, executive relationship risk, and contract
dispute. Most enterprise ERP startups underestimate implementation cost
by 50–100% in early contracts.
Cross-reference: if the company sells specifically to financial services
(finance ERP) or healthcare (clinical operations), also apply the
Fintech or Healthtech Library.

2. SECURITY AND COMPLIANCE
Signals: product addresses cybersecurity (endpoint protection, SIEM,
identity management, threat detection, GRC, vulnerability management);
buyer title includes CISO, Head of Information Security, or Chief Risk
Officer; product requires access to network traffic, identity systems,
or sensitive data; sales cycle involves a security proof of concept
(PoC) or technical evaluation period.
Key characteristic: security budget is non-discretionary — in a downturn,
CISOs cut headcount before cutting security tooling. However, procurement
is highly technical: a CISO will not buy without a technical PoC, a
penetration test review, and a security architecture sign-off. Sales cycles
are 6–12 months for endpoint and identity; 12–18 months for SOC/SIEM platform.
NRR benchmark: STRONG >120% (security sprawl drives expansion). Logo churn:
STRONG <5% annual (switching security tooling is operationally risky).
Key risk: a single major security breach at a customer attributed to the
product can destroy the business overnight. Cyber liability and indemnity
clauses in enterprise contracts must be carefully constructed.
Cross-reference: if product sells into financial services (FCA-regulated firms)
or government (MOD, NCSC supply chain), also apply Fintech or note
that government procurement applies additional vetting requirements.

3. DATA AND ANALYTICS INFRASTRUCTURE
Signals: product is a data warehouse, data lakehouse, data catalogue,
analytics platform, or BI tool; buyer title includes Chief Data Officer,
Head of Data Engineering, or VP Analytics; pricing includes compute
usage, data volume, or query consumption; competitive set includes
Snowflake, Databricks, Palantir, or legacy business intelligence vendors
(MicroStrategy, Cognos, Qlik).
Key characteristic: very high implementation cost — migrating enterprise
data pipelines requires months of engineering work and creates structural
lock-in once complete. A company that successfully onboards an enterprise
data platform customer has switching costs that compound over time.
Usage-based pricing is common; committed ARR and consumption ARR must
be separated.
Key risk: the data infrastructure market is dominated by well-funded
incumbents. A startup challenging Snowflake or Databricks must identify
a specific underserved segment (e.g. regulated data for financial services,
real-time analytics for retail, healthcare-specific data governance).

4. DEVELOPER TOOLS AND DEVOPS
Signals: product is used by engineering teams — CI/CD pipeline, container
orchestration, observability, monitoring, incident management, internal
developer platform, code review, or security scanning; initial adoption is
bottoms-up (engineers discover the product before procurement is involved);
a free tier, open-source version, or free trial exists; pricing converts
from individual use to enterprise contracts.
Key characteristic: product-led growth (PLG) to enterprise is the primary
motion. Engineers adopt the product individually or in small teams; usage
grows; IT or procurement eventually formalises the relationship into an
enterprise contract. Win rate in formal enterprise procurement is higher
than cold enterprise sales because the product is already embedded.
Key risk: PLG-to-Enterprise Transition Failure. See Section D. A developer
tool company that hires an enterprise sales team before shipping enterprise
features (SSO, RBAC, audit logs, compliance reporting) will burn the pipeline
without closing deals.

5. COMMUNICATION AND COLLABORATION
Signals: product enables enterprise communication, document management,
knowledge management, project coordination, or enterprise search;
buyer titles include Head of IT, CIO, or Chief People Officer; pricing
is per-seat with volume tiering; competitive set includes Microsoft 365,
Google Workspace, Slack, Confluence, Notion, or SharePoint.
Key characteristic: high user count but low ACV per seat (£5–25 per seat
per month). Enterprise value is driven by seat count, not per-user
pricing power. A company selling at £10/seat/month to a 10,000-seat
enterprise generates £1.2M ARR per customer — significant, but requires
selling at C-suite level and displacing established tools.
Key risk: Microsoft and Google are the structural incumbent in this
category. Any collaboration product must answer the Incumbent Displacement
Question: why will a CIO rip out Microsoft Teams or Google Workspace for
this? Without a compelling answer (category-defining feature, regulatory
requirement, or specific workflow the incumbents cannot address), the
competitive dynamics are unfavourable.

6. SUPPLY CHAIN AND OPERATIONS
Signals: product addresses supply chain visibility, procurement automation,
warehouse management, demand planning, or logistics optimisation;
buyer title includes Chief Supply Chain Officer, VP Procurement, or COO;
ROI is quantifiable (percentage reduction in procurement cost, inventory
reduction, or delivery time improvement); implementation involves data
integration with ERP and logistics systems.
Key characteristic: ROI is the enterprise sales argument — not features,
not innovation, but measurable financial return on investment. The
sales process requires the vendor to model ROI with the customer's own
data before procurement is approved. This creates a detailed discovery
phase but also creates strong buy-in when ROI is proven.
Key risk: implementation complexity is very high. Supply chain software
must integrate with multiple upstream and downstream systems (ERP, WMS,
carrier APIs, supplier portals). Integration failures delay go-live and
trigger penalty clauses. A startup without a strong professional services
team will promise 6-month implementations and deliver 18-month ones.

7. VERTICAL ENTERPRISE SOFTWARE
Signals: product is built specifically for one industry — financial services
(trading platforms, regulatory reporting, loan origination), retail
(merchandise planning, price optimisation), manufacturing (quality management,
MES), or government (case management, benefits administration, digital services);
buyer is an industry-specific executive; competitive set includes legacy
on-premise software vendors (Temenos, Finastra, JDA, CGI) or in-house built
systems; product requires deep domain knowledge to configure and use.
Key characteristic: deep domain expertise is both the moat and the
bottleneck. A vertical enterprise software company can achieve dominant
market position (>30% share) in its vertical — but the buyer universe is
finite and often smaller than it appears. A company with 150 potential
enterprise customers worldwide cannot achieve venture-scale returns unless
ACV is very large (£1M+) or there are adjacent verticals accessible.
Apply the Enterprise Only Trap test (PASS Deal 024 from the Training Library):
if realistic enterprise customer count is below 300, ACV must be £500K+
to justify a £50M+ Series A valuation.

IMPORTANT NOTE ON ENTERPRISE METRICS:
Enterprise software companies grow differently from SMB SaaS. Sales cycles
are long — 6–18 months — which means ARR at early stages is less predictive
than pipeline quality and ACV trajectory. Alex must weight pipeline health,
win rate, and ACV more heavily than ARR at pre-seed and seed stage. At
Series A and beyond, NRR is the primary quality signal: enterprise software
should have the highest NRR of any SaaS sub-category (120–145%+) because
land-and-expand is the core growth model.

PROFESSIONAL SERVICES MARGIN DECOMPOSITION — REQUIRED AT ALL STAGES:
Enterprise companies frequently blend software and services revenue into a single
gross margin figure. Alex must always request decomposed margins.

Software margin targets (by stage):
  Seed:        STRONG >70% | ACCEPTABLE 60–70% | CONCERN <60%
  Series A:    STRONG >75% | ACCEPTABLE 65–75% | CONCERN <65%
  Series B:    STRONG >80% | ACCEPTABLE 70–80% | CONCERN <70%

Professional services margin targets:
  STRONG >20% (highly productised implementation)
  ACCEPTABLE 0–20% (near-cost delivery — acceptable if strategic)
  CONCERN <0% (services running at a loss — subsidising customer acquisition)
  FAIL: ongoing services loss without a credible path to >0% margin

Blended gross margin — context adjustment table:
  Prof services 15% of revenue: software margin = (blended × 1.15) − (15% × services margin)
  Prof services 30% of revenue: software margin = (blended × 1.30) − (30% × services margin)
  Use this to verify whether the software business stands alone at venture-grade margins.

VALUATION BENCHMARKS — Enterprise Software (2025–2026 market):
Seed:      10–20x ARR | Premium for >3 Fortune 500 reference customers: 18–30x
Series A:  10–20x ARR | Premium for NRR >125%: 18–28x
Series B:  12–22x ARR | Premium for NRR >130% and Rule of 40 >45: 20–35x
Enterprise premium vs SMB SaaS: enterprise commands a 15–25% multiple premium
at the same ARR and growth rate due to higher NRR, lower logo churn, and
greater switching cost certainty.
Note: Revenue quality matters more than growth rate in enterprise. A company
at £5M ARR with 130% NRR and 3% logo churn commands a higher multiple than
a company at £8M ARR with 105% NRR and 15% logo churn.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. SALES CYCLE CONSISTENTLY EXCEEDING 18 MONTHS
SIGNAL: Stated average sales cycle across the current pipeline is above 18 months;
or founder acknowledges that deals routinely stall in legal, security, or procurement
for more than 6 months after the business buyer has approved the purchase.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The company does not have a repeatable enterprise sales motion. A
sales cycle above 18 months on average means the company cannot reliably plan its
ARR additions. Pipeline dating becomes unreliable. Forecast accuracy collapses.
It also signals that the product may not be compellingly urgent to the buyer —
enterprise buyers will delay non-critical software procurement indefinitely.
VERIFICATION: Ask for the last 5 closed contracts: date of first meeting, date of
signed contract. Calculate average. Also ask: "What is the most common reason deals
stall between verbal commitment and signed contract?" Legal, security, and budget
approval stalls each have different remediation strategies.

02. IMPLEMENTATION FAILURE RATE ABOVE 10%
SIGNAL: More than 10% of signed customers either failed to go live, went live more
than 100% over the stated implementation timeline, or escalated to executive or
legal dispute during implementation.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The product is either too complex to implement at the current
level of professional services maturity, or the pre-sale scoping process is
setting unrealistic expectations. A single failed enterprise implementation can
destroy a reference customer relationship, trigger contract penalties, and consume
10x the implementation cost in dispute management. At early stages, one failed
implementation out of five customers is a 20% failure rate.
VERIFICATION: For each signed customer: "Is the product fully live and in active
use?" Any customer not live after 12 months from contract signing warrants
detailed explanation.

03. PROFESSIONAL SERVICES ABOVE 40% OF TOTAL REVENUE
SIGNAL: Professional services revenue (implementation, customisation, training,
managed services) represents more than 40% of total reported revenue.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The business is partially or substantially a services business,
not a software business. Professional services typically carry 0–25% gross margin
vs 70–85% for software. A company with 40%+ services revenue and a blended 60%
gross margin has a software gross margin that is mathematically plausible only
if the services margin is near-zero. The implied software business may be much
smaller than the headline revenue suggests.
VERIFICATION: Require revenue split and margin by type for the last 12 months.
Calculate: what is the standalone ARR excluding all professional services revenue?
What is the software-only gross margin? Is the services team growing faster
than the software revenue?

04. SINGLE CUSTOMER ABOVE 20% OF ARR
SIGNAL: One customer represents more than 20% of total ARR; or the top 3 customers
represent more than 50% of total ARR at Series A or later.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Revenue concentration risk. If this customer churns, does not
expand, or renegotiates downward at renewal, the company misses its ARR plan by
more than 20%. For an early-stage company, a single large customer can distort
product roadmap, sales cycle benchmarks, NRR figures, and all unit economics.
The NRR calculation is dominated by one account — 130% NRR with one customer
driving 30% of ARR says nothing about whether the other customers are healthy.
VERIFICATION: Request ARR by customer for top 5. Ask: "If your largest customer
did not renew, what would your NRR be?"

05. NO SOC 2 TYPE II OR ISO 27001 FOR A PRODUCT HANDLING SENSITIVE ENTERPRISE DATA
SIGNAL: Company sells to enterprises — particularly financial services, healthcare,
or government — and is handling sensitive customer or employee data, but has not
completed SOC 2 Type II certification or ISO 27001 accreditation.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: In 2025, SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 are not optional for enterprise
software that handles sensitive data. Any Fortune 500, FTSE 100, regulated financial
institution, NHS trust, or government department requires these certifications before
procurement can be approved. Without them, the sales cycle cannot close — the deal
stalls at the security review stage indefinitely.
SOC 2 Type II: takes 9–15 months from initiation to report issuance.
ISO 27001: takes 6–12 months for initial certification audit.
A company at Series A without these certifications is 12–18 months away from
being enterprise-procurable. This is a growth ceiling, not a minor gap.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Do you have SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001? If not, when did the
process start and when is certification expected?"

06. G-CLOUD OR CCS LISTING ABSENT FOR A PUBLIC SECTOR PRODUCT
SIGNAL: Company claims a significant portion of its pipeline or target market is
UK public sector (government departments, NHS, local authorities, education) but
is not listed on G-Cloud (Digital Marketplace) or Crown Commercial Service frameworks.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: G-Cloud listing is effectively mandatory for selling cloud software
to UK public sector. Government departments and NHS trusts must procure software
via approved frameworks or run a full public procurement process (OJEU or Find a
Tender — months of additional work). Without G-Cloud, the pipeline claim is not
credible. The public sector deals described may never close without framework access.
VERIFICATION: Search the Digital Marketplace for the company name. Ask: "Are you
listed on G-Cloud 13 or G-Cloud 14? If not, are you in the application process?"
G-Cloud applications open twice per year. Not yet listed is a 6–18 month gap.

07. FOUNDER-LED SALES AT SERIES A WITH NO ENTERPRISE VP SALES IN POST
SIGNAL: The company has reached Series A with enterprise ACV above £100K but
all sales are still closed by the founders, with no enterprise-experienced VP Sales
hired and actively in post.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Enterprise sales is not a founder skill set that translates to a
repeatable team motion. Founders close early enterprise deals through personal
credibility, technical depth, and network relationships. A VP Sales must build
a process, pipeline methodology, and forecasting framework that works for a team.
Without this hire, the company cannot scale beyond 5–10 customers because the
founders are the bottleneck. Series A funds are deployed to prove repeatability —
that requires a professional enterprise sales leader.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Who closed your last 5 contracts?" If the answer is the CEO
or CTO every time: "When will you hire VP Sales, and what is your target profile?"
A company that has tried and failed to hire VP Sales has a talent or compensation
problem — investigate further.

08. PIPELINE CONVERSION RATE BELOW 15%
SIGNAL: Fewer than 15% of opportunities that enter the formal sales pipeline
result in a signed contract; or win rate in competitive bake-offs is below 15%.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Either the ICP is mis-defined (the company is selling to buyers
who will never purchase), the product is not differentiated enough to win
competitive evaluations, or the sales team lacks the enterprise skills to navigate
complex procurement. A below-15% win rate means the company is losing 85% of the
deals it invests 6–18 months of sales cycles pursuing. This has direct consequences
for sales team burn efficiency and growth predictability.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What is your win rate in competitive evaluations?" and
"What are the most common reasons you lose?" Loss analysis reveals whether the
problem is product, price, sales execution, or reference customer scarcity.

09. NET NEW ARR DRIVEN PRIMARILY BY UPSELL WITH STALLING NEW LOGO COUNT
SIGNAL: NRR is 120%+ and looks strong, but new logo additions have stalled at
the same count quarter-over-quarter. Expansion ARR is masking the absence of
new customer acquisition.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: The company has captured its early adopter cohort and is expanding
within those accounts successfully, but is not adding new enterprise logos at a
rate consistent with its ARR growth target. This is a precursor to the Land
Without Expand failure (Section D Pattern 5 reversed — Expand Without Land).
Eventually, the expansion within existing accounts plateaus and the company needs
new logos to sustain growth — but the new logo engine is not working.
VERIFICATION: Request new logo count by quarter for the last 6 quarters. Plot
new logo adds vs total ARR growth. If new logos have been flat for 2+ quarters
while ARR grew 30%+, the expansion is masking acquisition weakness.

10. IMPLEMENTATION BACKLOG CREATING CHURN RISK
SIGNAL: The professional services team has more customer implementations in
backlog than it can deliver in the next 90 days at its current capacity; or
a customer that has been in implementation for more than 2x the contracted
implementation timeline is threatening not to renew.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: An implementation backlog means customers are waiting to go live
on a product they have already paid for. Customer satisfaction deteriorates during
implementation delays. The buying champion who signed the contract becomes a
detractor. Other departments that were planned to expand adoption go cold. By
the time the product goes live, renewal is already at risk.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "How many customers are currently in implementation? What is
the average time-to-go-live for the last 5 implementations vs your contracted SLA?"
If there is a gap between contracted and actual, ask: "How are you managing the
customer relationship during the delay?"

11. CUSTOMER REFERENCE UNWILLING TO SPEAK PUBLICLY
SIGNAL: Company has 5+ paying enterprise customers but cannot produce a single
reference customer willing to speak on a call, appear in a case study, or be
named on the website.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Reference customers are the primary sales tool in enterprise
software. An enterprise buyer will not proceed past the PoC stage without
speaking to a current customer at a peer organisation. If no customer is willing
to be a reference, one of three things is true: the product is deployed in a
sensitive context (government, regulated finance) where disclosure is constrained;
the customers are not happy with the product or the relationship; or the accounts
are in implementation and not yet productive.
VERIFICATION: Ask directly: "Can you provide three customer references — senior
executives who can speak about business outcomes?" If references are offered only
under strict NDA or only by email, investigate the underlying relationship quality.

12. CONTRACT AUTO-RENEWAL RATE BELOW 85%
SIGNAL: Fewer than 85% of enterprise contracts renew without requiring active
re-sell; or the company does not track auto-renewal as a distinct metric from NRR.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Auto-renewal rate measures product embeddedness. If a customer
renews automatically — without renegotiation, competitive evaluation, or escalation —
it signals that the product is deeply integrated into their operations. An
auto-renewal rate below 85% means more than 1 in 7 contracts require active
retention effort. At scale, this consumes CS and sales capacity and signals that
the switching cost is lower than expected for enterprise software.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your renewals come up for active
re-evaluation vs auto-renewing at contract end?" If the company does not track
this, it is managing renewals reactively — flag as a CS maturity concern.

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE IMPLEMENTATION CRISIS
DESCRIPTION: The product is technically sound and the customer was genuinely
committed to the purchase. But the implementation takes 18 months instead of the
contracted 6 months. The customer's buying champion is replaced mid-implementation.
The new executive inherits a project that is over budget and behind schedule, with
no business value delivered yet. At month 18, the executive escalates to their
CEO. The vendor's CEO flies in. Legal disputes over penalty clauses begin.
By month 24, the customer does not renew. The implementation cost — in PS team
time, travel, and management distraction — has exceeded the contract value.
One failed implementation at an early-stage company consumes 20–30% of the
company's operating capacity for 6–12 months.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Implementation timeline stated in the pitch deck is below
3 months for any mid-market or enterprise ERP, supply chain, or security platform
(these are optimistic by definition); professional services team is smaller than
the customer success team; no dedicated implementation project manager role;
implementation methodology documented only at a high level; no change management
support offered to the customer's internal team.
WHEN TO APPLY: ERP, supply chain, data platform, and security platform companies
at seed and Series A with fewer than 3 successful live implementations as reference.
Any company where the implementation timeline has never been stress-tested against
a complex enterprise customer.

2. THE SERVICES TRAP
DESCRIPTION: Early enterprise customers require significant customisation before
they will go live. The company builds a professional services team to deliver this
customisation. The team grows from 2 to 10 to 25 people. Revenue grows impressively —
but 45% is now professional services. The software gross margin is 78%, but the
blended margin is 52% and declining. The company cannot raise its next round at
a software multiple because the financial profile looks like a services business.
The team is too large to cut, because the service contracts require the headcount.
The company is trapped between its software valuation ambition and its services
reality.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Professional services headcount growing faster than
engineering headcount for two consecutive quarters; services revenue as percentage
of total revenue has increased quarter-on-quarter for 3+ quarters; each new
enterprise customer requires a scoping document with a services line item;
product team is routinely asked to build customer-specific features that are
not in the core roadmap.
WHEN TO APPLY: Enterprise software companies at Series A and B where professional
services headcount exceeds 30% of total company headcount, or services revenue
has crossed 35% of total revenue.

3. THE SINGLE CHAMPION DEPENDENCY
DESCRIPTION: The company builds a strong relationship with one executive champion
at each enterprise account. This champion pushed the purchase through procurement,
defends the budget, and drives adoption internally. When the champion leaves —
which happens on average every 18–24 months at enterprise level — the new
executive has no attachment to the vendor. The renewal conversation starts from
scratch. The new executive uses the renewal as an opportunity to revisit the
decision. The vendor's customer success team has no other senior relationships
in the account. Three champions leave in the same quarter. Three renewals
are suddenly at risk.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Account mapping shows only one named executive contact
per customer; CS activity logs show meetings only with the original champion;
no multi-stakeholder engagement documented in 12+ months; expansion conversations
have always been initiated by the same person; the champion has left a previous
employer where the vendor also had a contract (pattern of champion-dependency);
champion mentions they are considering a role change.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any enterprise software company with ACV above £150K and NRR
that has not been tested through a full customer champion turnover cycle. Most
dangerous in Series A to B transition when the customer base is small enough
that individual champion departures are material.

4. THE PROCUREMENT PARALYSIS
DESCRIPTION: The product is technically superior to the incumbent. The business
buyer is committed. But the deal cannot close. IT raises a security concern —
the vendor does not have SOC 2 Type II. Legal raises a data residency issue —
the vendor's SaaS is hosted in US data centres only. Procurement raises a
supplier compliance issue — the vendor has not completed the enterprise's supplier
onboarding process. The security review starts — the vendor's penetration test
is from 2022. Each issue is solvable individually, but simultaneously they create
6 months of additional delay. By the time all issues are resolved, the business
buyer's budget has been reallocated, the champion has left, or the competitive
vendor has closed the account.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Sales cycle has exceeded 12 months with active business
buyer engagement; deal log shows the same compliance or security question appearing
across multiple named accounts; product has no data residency option (US-only
hosting); DPA template is non-standard and requires customer legal negotiation
on every deal; no security questionnaire response library exists; company is
not listed on G-Cloud for public sector deals.
WHEN TO APPLY: Companies that have closed their first 3–5 enterprise deals
through founder relationships and are now attempting to scale to enterprise
accounts they have no prior relationship with. The founder-relationship override
masks the underlying procurement readiness problem until the first non-relationship
sale is attempted.

5. THE LAND WITHOUT EXPAND
DESCRIPTION: The company wins initial enterprise contracts at £80–150K ACV with
a beachhead use case. The product delivers value in the beachhead. But the
expansion motion does not activate. One year later, the contract renews at the
same value. Two years later, still the same. NRR is 97–102% — not contracting,
but not expanding. The land-and-expand model that justified the initial investment
thesis is not working. The company's growth is entirely dependent on new logo
acquisition — which is slow and expensive in enterprise. Without expansion ARR,
the company needs to sell twice as many new logos every year to maintain growth,
which doubles the sales cost and CAC.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: NRR has been between 98–105% for two consecutive years
despite strong customer satisfaction scores; product usage data shows adoption
concentrated in the original beachhead department with no adjacent department
engagement; expansion pipeline at existing accounts is less than 20% of total
pipeline; the company's case studies all describe the original beachhead use case
and never describe a multi-department or enterprise-wide deployment;
professional services team is not structured to support expansion scoping.
WHEN TO APPLY: Enterprise companies at Series A and B where the investment thesis
explicitly includes land-and-expand as the primary NRR driver. If NRR has not
crossed 115% by 24 months after Series A, the expansion motion must be
investigated immediately.

6. THE COMPETITIVE DISPLACEMENT AT RENEWAL
DESCRIPTION: The company wins initial contracts against legacy software vendors
(on-premise, 20-year-old architecture). As the product matures and gains market
visibility, the category attracts well-funded competitors or a large incumbent
decides to build a competing product. At renewal, the customer receives an
aggressive competitive pitch from Salesforce, ServiceNow, or a PE-backed
consolidator — with a 30% price discount and an integration with software the
customer already uses. The company cannot match the competitive offer on price
or integration breadth. It loses 3 of its 10 largest accounts in the same renewal
cycle. ARR drops 25% in a single quarter.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Category has attracted a named large competitor (Salesforce,
ServiceNow, Microsoft, or a PE-backed rollup) in the last 12 months; company's
differentiation argument is primarily on feature superiority rather than
integration depth, switching cost, or data lock-in; contract structure does not
create meaningful switching cost (no proprietary data format, no deep API
integration, no multi-year minimum); renewal conversations are starting to include
requests for pricing reviews and competitive benchmarking.
WHEN TO APPLY: Series B and later companies selling into a category that has
recently attracted large strategic or PE acquirer interest. Check whether any of
the company's named enterprise customers are also customers of the named
competitor. If yes, competitive displacement risk is elevated at every renewal.

ACQUISITION MULTIPLES BY SUB-CATEGORY (strategic acquirer / PE acquirer):

ERP / Core Systems:          Strategic 8–18x ARR | PE 5–10x ARR
  Premium driver: >30% market share in a vertical or >500 enterprise logos.
  PE note: Vista Equity Partners and Thoma Bravo actively acquire vertical ERP
  at 6–10x ARR and apply operational improvement playbooks.

Security / Compliance:       Strategic 12–30x ARR | PE 7–15x ARR
  Premium driver: high NRR (>130%), expanding TAM (threat categories growing),
  and non-discretionary budget status. Security commands the highest multiples
  in enterprise software due to mission-critical nature.

Data / Analytics:            Strategic 10–25x ARR | PE 6–12x ARR
  Premium driver: proprietary data assets or pre-trained models; deeply
  embedded data pipelines (high switching cost). Snowflake IPO at 75x ARR
  set the ceiling — not a 2025 benchmark.

Developer Tools / DevOps:    Strategic 10–30x ARR | PE rarely acquires
  Premium driver: large developer user base (GitHub-style distribution),
  network effects, or unique infrastructure position. PE rarely acquires
  PLG-native products because the growth model requires product investment
  over operational efficiency.

Communication / Collab.:     Strategic 6–14x ARR | PE 4–8x ARR
  Premium driver: deep enterprise workflow integration and high seat penetration.
  Microsoft and Google dominate — a strategic acquirer in this category is
  most likely a horizontal SaaS platform (Salesforce, ServiceNow) adding
  communication capability to a workflow product.

Supply Chain / Operations:   Strategic 8–16x ARR | PE 5–10x ARR
  Premium driver: integration depth with major ERP systems (SAP, Oracle);
  quantifiable ROI data across multiple enterprise logos.

Vertical Enterprise:         Strategic 8–20x ARR | PE 5–12x ARR
  Premium driver: >25% market share in vertical; strong NRR (>120%);
  proprietary data that is unavailable outside the platform.
  PE note: Thoma Bravo, Vista, and Francisco Partners all run active vertical
  enterprise consolidation strategies. A strong vertical enterprise business
  with defensible market position often has a higher PE exit probability
  than a strategic exit.

ENTERPRISE MULTIPLE PREMIUM vs SMB SaaS:
Enterprise software commands a 15–25% ARR multiple premium over SMB SaaS
at equivalent growth rates because:
(a) NRR is structurally higher (120–140% vs 105–120% for SMB)
(b) Logo churn is structurally lower (<5% vs 10–20% for SMB)
(c) Switching costs are quantifiably higher (multi-year implementation lock-in)
(d) Revenue predictability is higher (multi-year enterprise contracts)

NAMED STRATEGIC ACQUIRERS:

Salesforce: Horizontal enterprise workflow, CRM adjacent, AI-native enterprise.
  Typical range: £200M–£10B. Has paid 15–25x ARR for category-defining assets
  (Slack £21B, Mulesoft £6.5B, Tableau £14B). Focuses on integrations with
  its existing enterprise CRM and platform customer base.

ServiceNow: Enterprise workflow automation (IT, HR, finance, legal, procurement).
  Typical range: £100M–£2B. Pays 10–20x ARR for workflow tools that can be
  embedded in its Now Platform and sold to its existing enterprise customer base.

SAP: ERP-adjacent, supply chain, financial compliance, manufacturing operations.
  Typical range: £100M–£3B. Acquires to extend core ERP into adjacent workflows.
  Actively acquiring AI-native tools that layer onto SAP ERP customer data.

Oracle: ERP, HR, finance, data, cloud infrastructure.
  Typical range: £100M–£5B. Acquires vertically integrated enterprise platforms
  that extend its existing suite. Will pay a premium for regulated-industry
  vertical software (financial services, healthcare, government).

Microsoft: Developer tools, security, collaboration, AI-native enterprise.
  Typical range: £200M–£10B+. The most active large enterprise software acquirer.
  Has strategic interest in developer toolchain (GitHub acquisition), security
  (CrowdStrike partnership precedent), and AI-native enterprise (OpenAI investment).
  Will pay a substantial premium for products with significant developer
  or enterprise IT mindshare.

Workday: HR tech, workforce management, financial planning, ERP.
  Typical range: £50M–£1B. Acquires to extend workforce and financial platform
  coverage. Focus on adjacent HR workflow, talent intelligence, and financial analytics.

CrowdStrike: Cybersecurity — endpoint, identity, threat intelligence, SOC.
  Typical range: £50M–£1B. Acquires to extend the Falcon platform across

Palo Alto Networks: Network security, cloud security, SOC, AI security operations.
  Typical range: £100M–£2B. Running a platform consolidation strategy —

Thoma Bravo (PE): Vertical SaaS, security, compliance, financial software.

Vista Equity Partners (PE): Vertical SaaS, ERP, HR tech, financial software.
  Typical range: £100M–£3B enterprise value. Similar to Thoma Bravo. Pays
  5–10x ARR. Applies Vista's operational playbook (standardised processes,

Francisco Partners (PE): Enterprise software, security, infrastructure.
  Typical range: £50M–£2B enterprise value. Active consolidator. Will acquire

Broadcom (Symantec, Carbon Black): Enterprise security and infrastructure.

  Note: Broadcom's VMware acquisition strategy (extract maintenance revenue,

IPO COMPARABLES — Enterprise Software:

Veeva Systems (2013):   ARR at IPO ~£130M | YoY growth 95% | IPO multiple: 18x ARR
  The vertical enterprise software IPO benchmark. Life sciences vertical dominance.

Workday (2012):         ARR at IPO ~£270M | YoY growth 89% | IPO multiple: 22x ARR
  HR and finance ERP. Demonstrated that cloud ERP could displace SAP.

  Workflow automation. Grew from IT service management to horizontal enterprise platform.

Palantir (2020):        ARR at IPO ~£1.1B | YoY growth 47% | IPO multiple: 25x ARR

(blended gross margin <60%) is unlikely to achieve a software-grade IPO multiple.

SECTION F — FOUNDER MEETING QUESTIONS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. AVERAGE SALES CYCLE LENGTH AND TREND
QUESTION: "What is your average sales cycle from first meeting to signed contract?
And has that number changed over the last 12 months?"
STRONG ANSWER: States a specific number with data (e.g. "7.5 months average
across our last 12 closed deals, down from 9 months 12 months ago"). Cites
the specific bottleneck — legal, procurement, security — and describes what
process was built to address it. Trend is stable or declining.
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot state an average. Describes it as "it varies" without
a range. Or states it is improving without citing data. Or states that some
deals close in 3 months without addressing why others take 18+.
REVEALS: Sales process maturity. Whether the company understands its own
go-to-market engine. Whether the trend line supports the ARR growth rate
in the financial model.

02. IMPLEMENTATION TIMELINE AND FAILURE RATE
QUESTION: "For your last 5 customers that went live — what was the contracted
implementation timeline vs the actual time to go live? And have any customers
experienced implementation problems serious enough to affect the renewal?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has data on all 5. Time to live is within 20% of contracted
timeline for 4 of 5. Any exception is explained with a specific root cause
that has been remediated. No customers have cited implementation as a
renewal concern.
WEAK ANSWER: "Implementations have generally gone well." Cannot name
specific go-live dates. One or more customers are "still in implementation."
Deflects implementation timeline questions by moving to customer satisfaction.
REVEALS: The single most important operational risk in enterprise software.
Implementation failure is how enterprise software companies die at seed and
Series A. This question must be answered with specifics.

03. PROFESSIONAL SERVICES AS PERCENTAGE OF REVENUE
QUESTION: "What percentage of your revenue last quarter was professional services
— implementation, customisation, training, managed services — vs pure software
licence or SaaS subscription?"
STRONG ANSWER: Below 25%, declining as a percentage as the company productises
implementation. Can articulate the plan to reduce services dependency (better
onboarding tooling, implementation partners, product-led implementation).
WEAK ANSWER: Above 40%, or growing as a percentage. Cannot clearly separate
the two revenue streams. Describes services revenue as "strategic" without
a plan to productise.
REVEALS: Whether the company is a software business or a services business
in disguise. Gross margin quality. Whether the business can scale without
proportionally growing a headcount-intensive services team.

04. LARGEST SINGLE CUSTOMER AS PERCENTAGE OF ARR
QUESTION: "Who is your largest single customer, and what percentage of your
current ARR do they represent?"
STRONG ANSWER: Below 15%, with a clear plan to grow the total base so no
single customer exceeds 10% within 18 months. The customer has multi-year
contract, is actively expanding, and is a named reference.
WEAK ANSWER: Above 25%, with this customer described as "strategic" or
"a great reference" without acknowledging the concentration risk. Defensive
when the question is pressed.
REVEALS: Revenue concentration risk. Whether the ARR and NRR metrics are
representative of the broader customer base or are dominated by one relationship.

05. SECURITY CERTIFICATIONS IN PLACE
QUESTION: "Do you have SOC 2 Type II certification or ISO 27001? If not, when
did the process start and when do you expect to receive certification?"
STRONG ANSWER: SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001 in place. Can name the audit firm.
Can state the scope of the Type II report. Is in the process of adding the other
certification. Has Cyber Essentials Plus for UK government/NHS sales.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're working on it." Started the process more than 12 months ago
without a certification date. Or describes SOC 2 Type I (observation period only —
not the same as Type II). Or says enterprise customers have waived the requirement
(unlikely; more likely the requirements were not surfaced in the sales process).
REVEALS: Procurement readiness for enterprise. Whether the company is genuinely
enterprise-grade or has closed early deals through founder relationships that will
not replicate at scale.

06. PROCUREMENT FRAMEWORK LISTINGS
QUESTION: "Are you listed on G-Cloud, any Crown Commercial Service frameworks,
or any NHS procurement frameworks? If you're targeting public sector, which
frameworks are you on?"
STRONG ANSWER: G-Cloud 13 or 14 listing in place. Named CCS framework if relevant
(e.g. Technology Products and Associated Services). NHS DPSP completion or DSP
Toolkit aligned for health-related products. Has a named public sector sales
channel (reseller, framework partner, or direct).
WEAK ANSWER: "We're planning to apply." Not listed. Describes public sector
pipeline without understanding that procurement requires a framework route.
Or lists US government procurement (FedRAMP) in a UK-focused pitch — wrong market.
REVEALS: Whether the public sector pipeline is real. G-Cloud is the front door
to UK public sector cloud procurement. Without it, the pipeline claims cannot close.

07. VP SALES HIRE STATUS AND TRACK RECORD
QUESTION: "Who is your VP Sales? How long have they been in role? What was
their last role, and what size enterprise sales team did they build there?"
STRONG ANSWER: VP Sales has been in role 6+ months. Previous role was VP Sales
at a comparable-stage enterprise software company that successfully scaled from
£2–10M ARR. Has already changed the pipeline management methodology. Can name
the 3 enterprise logos they personally influenced to close since joining.
WEAK ANSWER: VP Sales hired within the last 3 months (too new to evaluate impact).
Or founder is still closing all enterprise deals and VP Sales is "supporting."
Or VP Sales background is mid-market SaaS with no enterprise experience above
£100K ACV. Or the role has not been filled and the founder is trying to fill it
with equity-only compensation.
REVEALS: Whether the company can build a repeatable enterprise sales engine or
remains founder-dependent. This is the single most important people question at
Series A in enterprise software.

08. PIPELINE COVERAGE RATIO
QUESTION: "What is your current pipeline coverage — total pipeline value vs your
ARR target for the next 12 months? And how are you defining pipeline (what stage
does a deal have to reach to be included)?"
STRONG ANSWER: Pipeline coverage above 4x ARR target. Pipeline definition is
precise — only deals with a signed NDA and formal evaluation underway are included.
Can break pipeline by stage (discovery, PoC, legal, signed). Weighted pipeline
above 2x ARR target.
WEAK ANSWER: Pipeline includes "conversations," "warm introductions," and
"interested leads" — all at the same probability as a deal in formal procurement.
Cannot state coverage ratio clearly. Or coverage ratio is below 3x — insufficient
to achieve the ARR plan if win rate is below 30%.
REVEALS: ARR forecast reliability. Whether the growth plan is achievable. Whether
the founder understands enterprise pipeline management or is reporting activity
as pipeline.

09. COMPETITIVE WIN RATE AGAINST NAMED ALTERNATIVES
QUESTION: "In competitive bake-offs — where you know you were evaluated against
at least one other vendor — what is your win rate over the last 12 months?
And who do you most commonly lose to?"
STRONG ANSWER: Win rate above 35% competitive. Names the 2–3 competitors most
commonly encountered. Has a clear view on why the company wins (differentiator)
and why it loses (price, integration breadth, brand). Has case studies that
address the most common competitive comparison.
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot state a win rate. Describes competition as "legacy software"
without naming current-generation competitors. Claims to rarely face competition
(unlikely in any meaningful enterprise software category). Or win rate is below
20% with no clear plan to improve it.
REVEALS: Market position. Whether the product is genuinely differentiated or is
winning through price alone (unsustainable) or founder relationships (non-scalable).
Loss analysis is as important as win rate.

10. CUSTOMER REFERENCE QUALITY
QUESTION: "Can you provide three reference customers — senior decision-makers,
ideally C-suite or VP level — who are willing to speak with us about the business
outcomes they achieved? Are any of them willing to be named publicly on your website
or in case studies?"
STRONG ANSWER: Immediately provides 3 names and contact details. At least 2 are
C-suite at recognisable enterprise organisations. At least 1 is publicly named
on the website or in a press release. References proactively describe business
outcomes in quantifiable terms.
WEAK ANSWER: References available "subject to NDA." All references are at manager
or director level, not C-suite. References are willing to speak only about
product satisfaction, not business outcomes. No customer is willing to be publicly
named. Or the company has 10+ customers but cannot produce 3 references.
REVEALS: The deepest signal of enterprise customer health. Reference willingness
is the product market fit test for enterprise. If customers will not advocate
publicly, they are not getting the value required to justify an expansion
investment or renewal.

11. LAND AND EXPAND — ACV GROWTH WITHIN EXISTING ACCOUNTS
QUESTION: "What is the average ACV 12 months after initial contract, vs the
initial ACV? What is your expansion motion — how do customers expand within
the platform?"
STRONG ANSWER: ACV 12 months post-signing is 30–60% higher than initial ACV.
Can name 3 customers who expanded from beachhead to multi-department. Has a
structured QBR process that identifies expansion opportunities every 90 days.
Expansion ARR is 25–40% of total net new ARR.
WEAK ANSWER: ACV is flat or declining 12 months post-signing. "Customers expand
when they're ready." No structured expansion programme. Expansion ARR is less than
10% of total net new ARR, meaning all growth comes from new logos.
REVEALS: Whether the land-and-expand model is working. This is the most important
NRR driver in enterprise software. If land-and-expand is not working, the growth
model requires 2x the new logo volume to maintain the same ARR trajectory.

12. G-CLOUD AND PUBLIC SECTOR FRAMEWORK ACCESS
QUESTION: "For your public sector pipeline — what framework route do these deals
close through? Are you on G-Cloud? Have you completed NHS DSPT or Cyber Essentials
Plus for the health or government accounts in your pipeline?"
STRONG ANSWER: Listed on G-Cloud 13 or 14. Names specific framework lots. Has
closed at least one public sector deal through G-Cloud. NHS DSPT complete for
any health trust customers. Cyber Essentials Plus in place for government/MOD.
Has a named reseller or framework partner for accounts where direct listing is
insufficient.
WEAK ANSWER: Not listed on any framework. Public sector pipeline described as
"direct relationships" or "introductions." Has not completed NHS DSPT or Cyber
Essentials. Is unaware that UK public sector procurement requires a framework
route for cloud software.
REVEALS: Whether the public sector pipeline will ever close. A company that does
not understand procurement frameworks cannot close government or NHS business
at any meaningful scale, regardless of product quality.

UK GDPR AND DATA PROCESSING FOR ENTERPRISE SOFTWARE

Enterprise software vendors processing personal data on behalf of enterprise
customers act as data processors under UK GDPR (retained post-Brexit).
Requirements:
— Data Processing Agreement (DPA) required with every enterprise customer
  that involves processing personal data. Standard DPAs must comply with
  UK GDPR Article 28. A non-standard DPA triggers lengthy legal negotiation
  and is a major procurement delay.
— Data residency: enterprise buyers — particularly financial services, healthcare,
  and government — often require UK or EU data residency. A US-only SaaS
  deployment blocks procurement. UK and EU region hosting options must be
  available for enterprise-grade products.
— Data subject access requests (DSAR) and deletion: enterprise contracts must
  specify the vendor's process for responding to DSARs and implementing deletion
  requests within the 30-day UK GDPR window.
— Lawful basis: enterprise AI products using customer data for model training
  require explicit data processing terms and often separate consent clauses.
  Any AI product trained on enterprise customer data without explicit permission
  is a material legal risk.
Assessment implication: A company without a standard UK GDPR-compliant DPA template
cannot close enterprise deals in the UK. Ask for the DPA template.

NIS2 DIRECTIVE — IMPLICATIONS FOR ENTERPRISE SOFTWARE VENDORS

The EU's NIS2 Directive (Network and Information Security, transposed 2024)
imposes cybersecurity requirements on "managed service providers" and "digital
infrastructure providers" operating in the EU. UK equivalent: the Network and
Information Systems (NIS) Regulations (UK NIS).
Requirements for software vendors:
— Vendors selling to NIS2-regulated operators (energy, transport, health, finance,
  water) must meet supply chain security requirements imposed by those operators.
  Enterprise buyers will pass NIS2 supply chain requirements through to their
  software vendors via contract terms.
— Incident notification: vendors must notify customers of security incidents
  affecting their software within 24 hours under NIS2. This requires a formal
  incident response process.
Assessment implication: A security or infrastructure software vendor selling to
EU-regulated sectors without NIS2 supply chain compliance is a procurement
blocker at enterprise level in those sectors. Ask whether the company has
reviewed its NIS2 supply chain obligations.

CYBER ESSENTIALS AND CYBER ESSENTIALS PLUS — UK GOVERNMENT REQUIREMENT

Cyber Essentials: the UK government's baseline cybersecurity certification scheme,
administered by NCSC. Required for any contract involving the handling of certain
sensitive government data. Takes 2–4 weeks to certify.
Cyber Essentials Plus: includes an independent technical assessment in addition
to the self-assessment. Required for government contracts handling more sensitive
information, including MOD supply chain and many NCSC-aligned contracts.
Takes 4–8 weeks from initiation.
Assessment implication: Any enterprise software company targeting UK government,
MOD supply chain, or defence-adjacent enterprise customers without Cyber Essentials
Plus cannot win those contracts. It is a binary procurement requirement.
Cyber Essentials Plus is also increasingly required by large financial services
firms and NHS trusts as a vendor security baseline.

G-CLOUD 13 / G-CLOUD 14 — ROUTE TO UK PUBLIC SECTOR

G-Cloud is the UK Crown Commercial Service (CCS) framework for cloud software,
hosting, and support services. It is the primary procurement route for UK
central government departments, NHS, local authorities, schools, and other
public sector bodies to buy cloud software.
Structure: Lot 1 (cloud software/SaaS), Lot 2 (cloud platforms), Lot 3 (cloud hosting).
Enterprise software companies selling SaaS to public sector should be listed on Lot 1.
Listing process: G-Cloud applications open twice per year (typically March and September).
Applications require completion of a service definition, pricing schedule, and standard
supplier questionnaire. Takes 4–8 weeks to complete. Most applications are approved
if the product is genuinely cloud-delivered.
G-Cloud deals: buyers search the Digital Marketplace and can directly award contracts
up to £2M without a full competitive tender process. Above £2M, buyers run a
further competition within the framework.
Assessment implication: Without a G-Cloud listing, UK public sector SaaS sales
cannot close below £2M in any efficient procurement timeline. A company claiming
significant NHS or government pipeline without a G-Cloud listing has a pipeline
that cannot convert without a framework route. Verify listing at
find-a-supplier.service.gov.uk.

NHS DIGITAL SECURITY AND PROTECTION TOOLKIT (DSPT) AND CLINICAL SAFETY

NHS DSPT: the mandatory annual self-assessment for organisations that have access
to NHS patient data or NHS systems. Required for any software sold to NHS trusts
that connects to clinical systems, patient records, or NHS infrastructure.
Clinical Safety: under DCB0129 (software developer standard), any software used
in clinical settings must complete a clinical safety case. Under DCB0160, NHS
provider organisations must manage clinical safety of software they deploy.
Assessment implication: Any healthtech enterprise software company selling to NHS
trusts that involves patient data or clinical workflow must have DSPT completion
and DCB0129 clinical safety case documentation. Without these, NHS procurement
cannot proceed. See also the Healthtech Sector Library for full NHS framework detail.

FCA OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE — IMPLICATIONS FOR FINSERV ENTERPRISE SOFTWARE VENDORS

The FCA's Operational Resilience policy (PS21/3, effective March 2022, fully
implemented March 2025) requires FCA-regulated firms to ensure their critical
business services remain within impact tolerances during operational disruptions.
This extends to their software vendors and technology suppliers.
Requirements imposed on enterprise software vendors by finserv buyers:
— Business Continuity Plan (BCP) and Disaster Recovery (DR) documentation provided
  to the regulated firm customer.
— SLA uptime guarantees with contractual penalties that meet the customer's
  regulatory impact tolerance (typically 99.9% or higher for critical systems).
— Supply chain mapping: regulated firms must map their technology supply chain
  and identify critical third parties. A software vendor categorised as a
  "critical third party" by the FCA is subject to direct FCA oversight.
Assessment implication: A fintech or financial services enterprise software company
without formal BCP/DR documentation and FCA-compliant SLA terms cannot close
deals with FCA-regulated banks or asset managers. Ask for the SLA terms and
BCP summary.

DORA — DIGITAL OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE ACT (EU)

DORA (EU Regulation 2022/2554) entered into force January 2025 and applies to
all financial entities in the EU, including their ICT (information and communications
technology) third-party service providers.
Requirements for software vendors selling to EU financial institutions:
— Contractual requirements: DORA mandates specific contract clauses for ICT
  service providers — including uptime SLAs, audit rights, sub-contractor disclosure,
  incident notification timelines (within 4 hours), and exit provisions.
— Regulatory oversight: ICT third-party providers deemed "critical" by the European
  Supervisory Authorities (EBA, EIOPA, ESMA) are directly supervised under DORA.
— Sub-contractor chain: if an enterprise software vendor uses AWS, Azure, or GCP,
  the cloud provider is in the sub-contractor chain and must be disclosed to the
  regulated customer.
Assessment implication: Any enterprise software vendor selling into EU financial
services (banks, insurers, asset managers, payment institutions) post-January 2025
must have DORA-compliant contract templates. A UK company selling to EU finserv
customers without DORA-compliant terms cannot close those contracts. Given that
many UK enterprise software companies target EU finserv, this is a material
commercial readiness issue.

ISO 27001 AND SOC 2 TYPE II — ENTERPRISE PROCUREMENT REQUIREMENTS

ISO 27001: International standard for information security management systems.
Globally recognised. Required by many enterprise procurement teams, particularly
in financial services, healthcare, and government. Certification is issued by
an accredited third-party certification body (UKAS-accredited in the UK).
Certification cycle: annual surveillance audits + full recertification every 3 years.
Timeline to initial certification: 6–12 months from programme initiation.

SOC 2 Type II: US-origin standard (AICPA) now widely required in UK and European
enterprise procurement, particularly by US-headquartered enterprise buyers or
companies with US investor requirements. Type I = controls are designed.
Type II = controls have operated effectively over a period (minimum 6 months).
A Type II report is the enterprise-grade requirement. Type I is not sufficient
for most enterprise procurement teams.
Timeline: 9–15 months from programme initiation to Type II report.

Assessment implication: An enterprise software company at Series A or beyond
without at least one of ISO 27001 or SOC 2 Type II is not enterprise-procurable
by the vast majority of Fortune 500, FTSE 100, or regulated-industry buyers.
This is not a future goal — it is a current blocker on the sales pipeline.

EU AI ACT — HIGH-RISK AI CLASSIFICATION FOR ENTERPRISE SOFTWARE

The EU AI Act (entered into force August 2024, phased implementation 2025–2027)
classifies certain enterprise AI applications as "high-risk" AI systems with
mandatory conformity assessment requirements.
High-risk categories relevant to enterprise software:
— AI systems used in employment decisions (recruitment, performance evaluation,
  task allocation) — affects HR tech and workforce management software.
— AI systems used in creditworthiness assessment or credit scoring.
— AI systems used in critical infrastructure management (energy, water, transport).
— AI systems used by public authorities for decision-making affecting citizens.
— AI systems used in the administration of justice or democratic processes.
Obligations for high-risk AI systems:
— Conformity assessment before placing on the EU market.
— Registration in the EU AI Act database.
— Technical documentation, risk management system, data governance requirements.
— Human oversight mechanisms embedded in the product.
— Transparency obligations to deploying enterprises (deploy-level obligations).
Timeline: High-risk AI system obligations apply from August 2026.
Assessment implication: Enterprise software companies building AI-native products
in any of the high-risk categories and targeting EU enterprise buyers must
begin EU AI Act compliance preparation now. A company that reaches Series B
in 2026 without understanding its EU AI Act classification is carrying material
compliance risk in its largest addressable market.

PROCUREMENT FRAMEWORKS — ADDITIONAL ROUTES TO MARKET

Crown Commercial Service (CCS) frameworks: beyond G-Cloud, CCS runs frameworks
for specific categories. Technology Products and Associated Services (TPAS)
covers hardware and associated software. Professional Services frameworks cover
implementation consulting. Enterprise-scale software deals above £2M often
run through CCS frameworks rather than G-Cloud.

NHS frameworks: NHS Supply Chain does not cover software. NHS England procurement
runs through NHS Shared Business Services (NHS SBS) frameworks and NHS
collaborative procurement hubs. NHS trusts can also use G-Cloud directly.
For large NHS-wide software deployments, NHS England may run a separate
procurement outside any standard framework.

MOD / Defence frameworks: Ministry of Defence procurement for software uses the
Defence Digital framework and the MOD Commercial function. Requires security
clearance (SC or DV) for personnel handling classified information, DECS
(Defence Equipment and Support) supplier registration, and Cyber Essentials Plus.
ITAR and export control implications apply to any software touching defence
capability — see the Deep Tech Sector Library for full export control framework.

Local authority procurement: OJEU (now Find a Tender) above £213,477 threshold
for most contracts. Many local authorities also use G-Cloud for cloud software.
Below the threshold, direct award is possible with documented market testing.
