ALEX — RED FLAG LIBRARY
CATEGORY 2: TEAM RED FLAGS
Version 1.0. Built: April 2026.
15 flags. Apply mechanically to every deal submission.
These flags are observable from a submission without a meeting.
Two analysts applying these rules reach the same conclusion.
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION RULE:
For every deal submission, Alex checks all 15 flags before completing
the screening note. Flags are not discretionary — if the signal is
present, the flag is raised. Flags are then weighed in aggregate.
One CRITICAL flag = automatic deep investigation required.
Two or more HIGH flags = note this in the Risk Assessment section.
CRITICAL flag + confirmed by verification = strong PASS presumption.
Team flags interact with financial and market flags — a team flag alone
rarely disqualifies; a team flag combined with a financial flag is more
serious than either in isolation.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T01: FOUNDER BACKGROUND UNVERIFIABLE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Key credentials claimed in the submission — specific employer names, role titles,
revenue figures managed, deals closed, or qualifications — cannot be independently
confirmed through LinkedIn, company websites, Companies House, press coverage,
academic institution records, or other public sources. The gap between the claimed
background and the publicly verifiable record is material (e.g. claims to have been
VP Sales at a named company but that person does not appear in the company's LinkedIn
employee history, press mentions, or alumni network).

DETECTABLE SIGNALS IN SUBMISSION:
— Employer names are vague or unverifiable ("a major US tech company," "a global bank")
  without named organisations
— Role titles claimed are unusually senior relative to the founder's age and graduation
  date, with no verifying press coverage or professional endorsement
— Academic qualifications listed at institutions that do not award the claimed degree
— Exit or revenue figures from prior ventures that cannot be cross-referenced against
  any public record of the company

WHAT IT MEANS:
An unverifiable background is not necessarily fraudulent — some founders worked in
roles that left no public record, particularly in government, defence, or early-stage
companies. However, it creates two risks: (1) the claimed expertise that underpins
the market insight or domain advantage is not real, removing the primary differentiator
the team is claiming; (2) a founder willing to exaggerate credentials in a pitch will
exaggerate elsewhere — the character signal is material. The most serious form: a
claimed exit or successful prior company that cannot be verified, presented as evidence
of execution capability, which is the primary justification for the team score.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A specific credential is cited as the primary reason the team is qualified to build
this company — "we are uniquely positioned because our CEO ran [X division] at [Y
company]" — and that credential cannot be confirmed through any public source after
a reasonable search effort (20+ minutes of structured verification).

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The unverifiable element is peripheral — a supporting credential rather than
the primary expertise justification — and the rest of the background is fully
verifiable and consistent.
(b) The founder proactively explains the verification gap (e.g. "my NHS work is
not publicised for patient data reasons — I can provide my manager as a reference")
and offers a specific named reference for direct confirmation.
(c) The credential in question is a junior early-career role where no public record
would be expected — the gap is not suspicious in context.

VERIFICATION:
Search LinkedIn for the founder's full name + named employer. Search Companies House
for prior company directorships. Search Google for the founder's name + named employer
+ named role. Search academic institution alumni records where a degree is claimed.
If a named prior company is stated: verify the company existed, check the founder's
tenure dates against the company's founding and exit dates, and confirm the claimed
revenue or headcount against any press coverage or public filing.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a key credential underpins the team's right to win in a specific market, verify
it independently before weighting the team section of the screening note — unverifiable
credentials are treated as unconfirmed, not as confirmed.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T02: SERIAL FOUNDER WITH UNDISCLOSED PRIOR FAILURES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The founder's LinkedIn profile, Companies House record, or Crunchbase profile reveals
one or more prior companies that were dissolved, placed into administration, or quietly
wound down — none of which are mentioned in the submission. Timeline gaps of 12+
months between roles that are not explained by the current company's founding date
suggest undisclosed ventures.

DETECTION METHOD:
Search Companies House for the founder's name under "Officers" — this returns every
UK company they have been a director of, including dissolved entities. Cross-reference
Crunchbase and AngelList. Compare the founding dates of any discovered prior companies
against the timeline presented in the submission. Any company dissolved within 3 years
of founding that is not mentioned in the submission is a flag.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Prior failure is not disqualifying — many of the best founders failed before succeeding,
and the lessons from a failed company are often the most valuable preparation for
building the next one. The flag is the non-disclosure, not the failure. A founder
who proactively says "my last company failed for X reason — here is what I learned
and why this company is different" demonstrates self-awareness and honesty. A founder
who omits prior failures entirely is either not self-aware or deliberately concealing
information from an investor they are asking to trust them with capital. The non-disclosure
also raises the question of what else is not being mentioned — it is a character signal
that extends beyond the specific omission.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The prior company was dissolved within the last 24 months AND the circumstances of
dissolution are not disclosed AND the current company is in the same sector or
targeting the same customer type as the failed company — suggesting the failure was
not a learning event but a repeated attempt at a fundamentally flawed model.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The prior failure is proactively disclosed in the submission with a specific
account of what went wrong and what structural change in the current company
addresses that failure mode.
(b) The prior company was dissolved due to external circumstances (co-founder
departure, market timing, COVID impact) with clear evidence rather than product
or execution failure.
(c) The prior company is not a failure — it was a small side project or freelance
vehicle dissolved after a few months when the founder took a full-time role — the
Companies House record exists but the economic reality is not a meaningful failure.

VERIFICATION:
Run a Companies House officers search for every founder named in the submission.
List all directorships including dissolved entities. For each dissolved company
not mentioned in the submission, search for press coverage, Crunchbase entries,
or LinkedIn descriptions that explain the outcome. Present the findings directly:
"We identified a prior company, [X], dissolved in [year] — can you tell us about
that venture and why it did not continue?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When Companies House reveals a dissolved prior company not mentioned in the submission,
treat the non-disclosure as the primary flag — request the founder's account of the
venture before assessing the business itself.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T03: FOUNDING TEAM ALL FROM SAME PROFESSIONAL BACKGROUND
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
All co-founders share the same primary professional background — all engineers from
the same company, all McKinsey consultants, all academics from the same department,
all ex-bankers from the same team — with no complementary skill set visibly present
in the founding team to cover the other core functions of building a company.

SPECIFIC COMBINATIONS THAT TRIGGER THIS FLAG:
— All founders are engineers: no commercial, sales, or domain expertise in founding team
— All founders are domain experts (e.g. all doctors, all lawyers): no technical or
  commercial execution capability in founding team
— All founders are ex-consultants: strong analysis, weak product and sales execution
— All founders are ex-bankers: financial modelling capability, no engineering or GTM

WHAT IT MEANS:
Building a company requires three distinct capabilities: building the product
(technical), selling the product (commercial), and knowing what to build (domain).
A team that is strong in one and absent in the other two is not a founding team —
it is a group of smart people with one shared skill set attempting to operate
in territory they have not demonstrated they can navigate. The missing capabilities
will either be hired at high cost and late (by which time the product may be going
in the wrong direction) or not hired at all (in which case one function of the
company will be permanently under-resourced). The most common consequence: all-engineer
teams build products nobody wants to buy, and all-commercial teams commission products
that cannot be built on schedule.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
All founders share the same background AND the business requires expertise in the
missing area as a critical path dependency — for example, an all-engineer team
building enterprise software for financial services buyers where regulatory domain
knowledge and sales credibility with CISOs are essential to close the first deal.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The missing capability is explicitly addressed by a named senior hire already
in place at or above VP level — the team gap exists at founding team level but
not at leadership level.
(b) The business model in its early stage genuinely requires only one capability —
a deep tech company at TRL 3–5 needs engineering depth above all else; commercial
expertise is appropriately staged later.
(c) One founder has genuinely diverse experience (e.g. an engineer who spent
3 years in a commercial role before returning to engineering) and the apparent
homogeneity is surface-level.

VERIFICATION:
Read each co-founder's full LinkedIn profile, not just the headline. Identify
the primary function they performed in each role. Map the founding team against
three axes: product/technical, commercial/sales, domain/market. Identify which
axis is unrepresented. Ask: "Who owns the commercial/technical/domain function
in the founding team today, and what is their background in that area?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When all founders share the same professional background, identify the missing
function and ask how the company plans to fill it before assessing the team score —
the answer reveals whether the founders understand their own limitation.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T04: KEY TECHNICAL ROLE UNFILLED AT SERIES A
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
A company at Series A stage — defined as £1M+ ARR or an announced Series A round —
does not have a named CTO or VP Engineering in the founding team or senior leadership,
or the person holding that role has fewer than 3 years of engineering leadership
experience and has never built or managed a team of more than 5 engineers.

WHAT IT MEANS:
The Series A is the stage at which a company begins scaling its engineering team —
typically from 3–6 engineers to 10–20 within 18 months. This transition requires a
technical leader who can: (a) architect the product for scale rather than for the
next customer; (b) hire and manage an engineering team without the founder's daily
involvement; (c) maintain engineering quality and velocity as headcount increases.
Without an experienced technical leader at Series A, one of three things happens:
the CEO micromanages engineering (slowing both product and commercial execution);
the engineering team makes architectural decisions that require expensive rebuilds
at Series B (technical debt spiral); or the best engineers leave because there is
no technical leader to learn from and no clear engineering culture. The last failure
mode is the most costly — top engineers choose employers based heavily on the
quality of their technical leadership.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company is at Series A AND the CTO role is described as "being recruited" AND
the current engineering team is above 8 people AND the product has experienced
visible performance or reliability issues cited in customer feedback — the
leadership gap is already causing product quality risk.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) One of the co-founders is a genuinely experienced technical leader — former
CTO of a company that scaled from 5 to 50 engineers, or a staff engineer at a
top-tier technology company — even if their title is CEO or CPO rather than CTO.
(b) The company is a deep tech or hardware company where "CTO" is replaced by
a Chief Scientist or Head of Engineering who has the relevant domain expertise,
even if their management experience is limited.
(c) The company is at the very early end of Series A (£1–2M ARR) and the engineering
team is still below 5 people — the scaling challenge has not yet arrived and the
hire is appropriately staged.

VERIFICATION:
Identify the most senior technical person in the company from the submission's team
slide or LinkedIn. Find their full employment history. Identify the largest engineering
team they have previously led and the stage of company at which they did so. Ask:
"Who owns the technical architecture and the engineering roadmap today? And what
is the largest engineering team they have previously built from scratch?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When no experienced technical leader is present at Series A, model the engineering
scaling risk explicitly — the absence of technical leadership is a compounding cost
that gets more expensive to fix at every subsequent funding stage.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T05: EXCESSIVE EARLY DILUTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The founding team collectively owns less than 50% of the company at seed stage
before the current funding round — detectable when the cap table is disclosed
in the submission, or when the founding story involves multiple pre-seed investment
rounds, convertible notes, or significant advisor equity grants that collectively
dilute the founders below the threshold.

DETECTION METHOD:
If the cap table is not disclosed: a company that has raised more than £1M at
pre-seed, has 3+ investor names on the cap table, and is now raising seed suggests
meaningful dilution. If advisors hold more than 3% collectively this is also a
signal. If the founder's percentage holding is stated below 50% at seed this
triggers the flag directly.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Founders who are below 50% combined equity at seed face a compounding motivation
problem. After a seed round (typically 15–20% dilution) they may own 40% or less.
After Series A (20–25% dilution) they may own 30% or less. By Series B, the founders
may own so little of the company that the financial return required to make the
venture meaningful to them personally is so high it is unlikely to materialise —
and the practical reality of running a company they do not control (below 50% is
effectively minority ownership) affects decision-making, retention, and recruiting.
The secondary concern: excessive dilution at early stages often reflects poor
negotiation, desperation for capital (which raises questions about why early capital
was hard to raise), or a pattern of giving equity as a substitute for management.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Combined founder ownership is below 40% at seed stage AND more than 5% of the cap
table is held by advisors with no clear commercial deliverable attached AND the
company is seeking a Series A from institutional investors — because institutional
VCs typically require 70–80% founder ownership at seed entry for the long-term
dilution model to work. Below 40% combined at seed means founders may be below
20% by Series B, which is often below the threshold for sustained founder motivation.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Dilution below 50% reflects a deliberate strategy — a corporate spin-out where
the parent company holds equity as part of the founding structure, or a company
where a key industry partner took early equity in lieu of a commercial partnership fee.
(b) One co-founder holds significantly more than 50% while others hold less —
the combined figure is below 50% but one founder is well-incentivised.
(c) The company has already completed a seed round and the cap table reflects
post-seed dilution — the flag applies to pre-money seed cap table, not post-round.

VERIFICATION:
Request the current cap table showing all shareholders, share classes, and percentages.
Ask: "What percentage does each founder own today, before this round? And what
will their ownership be post-round and post-option pool creation?" If the cap table
is withheld at this stage, note this — early-stage companies that do not share
their cap table with investors in diligence are unusual.

PATTERN LESSON:
When founders collectively own less than 50% at seed stage, model their ownership
trajectory through Series A and B and assess whether residual ownership at exit
provides sufficient motivation for the level of commitment the company requires.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T06: NO VESTING SCHEDULE DISCLOSED FOR FOUNDING TEAM
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The submission does not disclose vesting terms for the founding team — either because
the deck has no cap table information and no mention of vesting, or because when
prompted, the founders describe equity as fully vested or already issued without
a time-based or milestone-based vesting schedule attached to founder shares.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A vesting schedule — typically 4 years with a 1-year cliff — is the primary
structural mechanism that prevents a co-founder departure from being catastrophic.
Without vesting, a co-founder who leaves after 6 months retains 100% of their
equity, which creates three simultaneous problems: (1) the departing founder holds
a large equity stake in a company they no longer contribute to, which suppresses
the equity available to replace them and alienates the remaining team; (2) future
investors will require a resolution before investing — the vesting gap must be
negotiated retrospectively with the departed founder, which is expensive and often
contentious; (3) the remaining founders and employees see the departure and its
consequence, which damages morale. The absence of vesting at a company that has
not yet raised institutional capital suggests the founders did not receive basic
legal advice — which raises questions about the rigour applied to other contractual
and structural decisions.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has 2 or more co-founders AND no vesting schedule is in place for any
of them AND the company is seeking a Series A from institutional investors — because
every institutional investor will require vesting to be established before closing,
and if vesting must be retrofitted onto a fully-issued cap table, it requires each
co-founder's consent and creates legal complexity that delays and sometimes destroys
the round.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company has only one founder — vesting is less critical without co-founder
departure risk, though employee equity vesting is still required.
(b) The founders confirm that vesting schedules exist but were not disclosed in the
submission — the schedules should be shared as part of standard diligence.
(c) The company is genuinely pre-incorporation and is setting up legal structure as
part of the fundraise — the absence of vesting is a timing issue, not a structural
omission, provided the founders commit to standard terms before close.

VERIFICATION:
Request the shareholder agreement and any founders' service agreements. Confirm: the
vesting start date for each co-founder, the cliff period, the total vesting period,
and whether vesting accelerates on a change of control. Ask: "If a co-founder left
tomorrow, how much equity would they retain?" The answer should be: "Only the vested
portion — the rest returns to the pool." Any other answer means vesting is not in place.

PATTERN LESSON:
When vesting terms are not disclosed in a multi-founder company, treat this as a
CRITICAL structural question and require written confirmation of vesting schedules
before any investment committee discussion — retrofit vesting after a co-founder
departure is orders of magnitude harder than establishing it before.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T07: ADVISOR EQUITY WITHOUT DELIVERABLES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The submission lists an advisory board whose collective equity holdings represent
5% or more of the fully diluted cap table — and no specific deliverable, committed
time, or milestone is attached to any individual advisor's role. Advisors are
described in general terms ("strategic advisor," "industry veteran," "former CEO of X")
without any statement of what they are actively contributing or have already delivered.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Advisor equity grants are intended to incentivise specific contributions: an introduction
to a strategic partner, a regulatory navigation commitment, a customer reference, or
a board-level governance input. When advisors collectively hold 5%+ with no attached
deliverable, the equity is not incentivising anything — it was granted to signal
credibility to investors rather than to obtain specific value. This creates two problems:
(1) The 5%+ is dilution that costs every future investor and every employee —
the economic cost is real even if the advisors are not providing active value in return.
(2) The advisor roster is a credentialing exercise, not a capability extension —
which means the team is trying to substitute name recognition for operational capability
it does not possess internally. The most common pattern: advisors who took equity in
return for a single warm introduction that happened 18 months ago and have not been
actively involved since.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Advisors collectively hold more than 8% of the fully diluted cap table AND the company
is at Series A AND no advisor is described with a specific past deliverable or active
current commitment AND the advisor equity is presented in the deck as evidence of
the team's strength — the credentialing substitution problem is most serious when
the underlying team has verifiable gaps.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Each advisor's equity is below 0.5% individually and 3% collectively — small
grants in exchange for occasional guidance are standard and not a structural concern.
(b) One or more advisors hold equity above 0.5% and a specific, verifiable
contribution is disclosed — "introduced us to our first 3 enterprise customers"
or "providing weekly guidance on our FDA 510(k) submission."
(c) The advisor equity is on a vesting schedule tied to ongoing participation —
unvested equity that lapses if the advisor disengages is not a dilution problem.

VERIFICATION:
Request the advisor equity schedule: each advisor's name, equity percentage, vesting
terms, and the specific contribution for which the equity was granted. For each
advisor holding more than 0.25%, ask: "What has this advisor done for the company
in the last 6 months?" If the answer is "they're available to advise when we need
them," the equity is not being earned and should be treated as sunk dilution.

PATTERN LESSON:
When advisors hold more than 5% collectively, request a contribution log for each
advisor — if no specific contribution is identifiable, treat the equity as wasted
dilution and reduce the team score accordingly.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T08: FOUNDER PREVIOUSLY REMOVED BY BOARD — UNDISCLOSED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Public record — press coverage, Companies House resignation date that does not
align with stated tenure, LinkedIn date discrepancy, or investor community knowledge —
indicates that a founder left a prior company earlier than their stated departure,
under circumstances suggesting they were removed or asked to step down rather than
voluntarily departing. This is not disclosed in the submission.

DETECTION METHOD:
Compare the founder's stated departure date from a prior company against Companies
House officer resignation records for that company. A discrepancy of 3+ months
between the two, combined with a LinkedIn departure description that does not match
the company timeline (e.g. "left to pursue new venture" but the current company was
not incorporated until 12 months after departure), warrants investigation.
Check for press coverage of the prior company during the departure period for any
news that would explain the timing.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A board-initiated founder removal is a serious event that carries specific risk signals
for a new investment: (1) the founder may have management or interpersonal behaviours
that caused the board to lose confidence — patterns that are likely to recur;
(2) the founder's shareholders, co-founders, or employees at the prior company may
have negative firsthand experience that is relevant to the current investment thesis;
(3) the non-disclosure of a board removal — especially when the departure timing is
clearly unusual — is a transparency failure. A founder who has been removed by a
board and proactively discloses it with a clear account of what happened and what
they learned is providing important information. A founder who conceals it is asking
an investor to discover it during diligence and draw their own conclusions without
the founder's framing — which is always worse for the founder.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Evidence of a prior board removal exists AND the founder has not disclosed it AND
the reason for removal, when researched, relates to financial mismanagement, investor
relations failures, or dishonesty — behaviours that are directly relevant to
the trust required in a new investor-founder relationship.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The founder proactively discloses the removal in the submission with a specific,
credible account of the circumstances — disagreement with investor direction, a
co-founder conflict, or a board that wanted a different CEO profile for a later stage.
(b) The removal occurred more than 7 years ago, the founder has since built and
exited another company successfully, and there is no pattern of repeated removal.
(c) The circumstances of removal are verifiably external — company ran out of capital,
merger required a different CEO, regulatory event forced a leadership change.

VERIFICATION:
Search Companies House for the prior company and check the director resignation date
for the founder. Cross-reference with their LinkedIn departure date. Search press
archives for the prior company name + founder name during the departure period.
If discrepancies are found, ask directly: "Can you walk us through your departure
from [company] — our records suggest you left earlier than you have described."
The response to the direct question is itself a data point.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder's Companies House resignation date does not align with their stated
departure from a prior company, investigate the circumstances before forming a view
on the team — the most important character signal is how the founder responds to
the direct question, not the event itself.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T09: KEY PERSON CONCENTRATION IN A NON-FOUNDER EMPLOYEE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
A non-founder employee — a head of sales, lead engineer, head of customer success,
or technical architect — is responsible for a majority of commercial relationships,
technical IP, or customer retention, with no succession plan or institutional backup.
Detectable when: customer names appear alongside a specific employee (not founder)
in case studies; one engineer is described as "the person who built X" without
co-authorship; or the submission describes an employee as "critical" or "irreplaceable"
without describing how they would be replaced.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Key person concentration in a non-founder employee creates a risk that is in some
ways more acute than founder dependency — founders have equity incentives and usually
strong conviction about the mission; a non-founder employee can leave for a competitor
at standard notice (typically 1–3 months) and take their institutional knowledge,
customer relationships, or technical skills with them. The most damaging scenarios:
(1) The lead engineer who holds the architectural knowledge of a complex technical
system departs — replacement requires 6–12 months of onboarding, during which
product development slows or stops; (2) the head of sales who owns 80% of the active
pipeline departs — pipeline confidence drops and committed ARR becomes uncertain;
(3) the head of customer success who is the primary relationship at 5 enterprise
accounts departs — renewal risk at those accounts immediately increases.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A single non-founder employee is responsible for more than 30% of active pipeline,
more than 40% of customer relationships by ARR, or is the sole possessor of critical
technical architecture knowledge AND the company does not have a documented succession
or knowledge transfer plan AND the employee's equity stake is below standard market
rate (creating an elevated attrition incentive).

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The key person is a co-founder or has co-founder-equivalent equity (above 3%)
and strong mission alignment — the retention incentive is structurally embedded.
(b) The company has documented processes, technical specifications, and customer
playbooks that mean the departure of the individual would be painful but survivable
within 3 months.
(c) The key person is early in a long vesting schedule with significant unvested
equity — the financial cost of departure is high and the retention risk is lower
than the title suggests.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "If [named employee] left tomorrow, what would be the impact on the business
in the next 90 days? Who else knows what they know?" A founder who says "we'd be
fine" about a described-as-critical employee is either not taking the question
seriously or has built better redundancy than the submission implies. Either way,
the specific answer is a data point about operational resilience.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a non-founder employee is described as critical to commercial relationships
or technical architecture, assess their equity stake, vesting status, and tenure —
low equity plus short remaining vesting is a key person departure signal that
should be scored as HIGH risk regardless of the current relationship quality.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T10: REFERENCE CUSTOMERS ALL FOUNDER-CONNECTED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Every named reference customer in the submission has a direct prior relationship
with the founding team — former employers, co-founders' business contacts, family
businesses, or companies where a founder served as an advisor or board member —
with no customer who came through an arm's-length commercial sales process.
Detectable by cross-referencing named customer companies against founders' LinkedIn
profiles, prior employers, and advisory roles.

WHAT IT MEANS:
The first 3–5 customers of a startup are almost always founder-connected — this is
expected and not a flag in isolation. The flag triggers when all named reference
customers are founder-connected and the company is presenting them as evidence of
product-market fit, demand validation, or commercial traction. Founder-connected
customers are not evidence of arm's-length commercial demand — they are evidence
of the founder's network and their willingness to support a friend's company.
Three specific distortions this creates: (1) pricing may be below market rate
because the buyer is being friendly; (2) the sales process was not a commercial
evaluation — the product was adopted as a favour and may not be used actively;
(3) these customers cannot be asked to speak objectively to future buyers —
their reference is coloured by the personal relationship.
If the company is at Series A with all founder-connected customers, it has not
yet proven it can sell to someone who did not previously know the founder.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
All 5 or more named customers are verifiably founder-connected AND the company is
at Series A AND no arm's-length commercial win can be identified in the last 24 months
AND the submission presents these customers as evidence of product-market fit. The
combination of all four conditions means commercial traction has not been demonstrated.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is at pre-seed or seed stage and all customers being founder-connected
is expected — the flag is a watch item for the next stage, not a current disqualifier.
(b) One or more founder-connected customers has provided a documented, arm's-length
product evaluation and has chosen to continue paying above an agreed minimum — the
relationship may have introduced the connection but the purchase decision was commercial.
(c) A new customer in the last 6 months came through an inbound or outbound process
with no prior founder relationship — the pattern is breaking, even if only once.

VERIFICATION:
Cross-reference every named customer against every founder's LinkedIn profile,
Companies House directorships, and advisory roles. For any match, ask: "How did
you first make contact with [customer]? Who made the buying decision and what
evaluation process did they run?" An arm's-length buyer will have run a comparison,
reviewed alternatives, and made a decision based on product merit — not a favour.

PATTERN LESSON:
When all named customers are verifiable as founder-connected, treat commercial
traction as undemonstrated and look for the earliest stage at which an arm's-length
customer acquisition occurred — the absence of any arm's-length win by Series A
is a PMF concern, not a team concern.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T11: TEAM GROWTH OUTPACING REVENUE GROWTH
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Headcount has grown at a faster rate than revenue for 3 or more consecutive quarters —
observable when the submission discloses both headcount history and ARR history,
or when the founding story describes significant hiring rounds that preceded revenue
milestones. Calculable as: headcount growth rate % − revenue growth rate % = the
overhire indicator. A positive number means the company is hiring ahead of revenue;
a persistently positive number over 3 quarters means the gap is structural.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Hiring ahead of revenue is a deliberate strategy in some growth contexts — a company
building an enterprise sales team ahead of a pipeline it expects to close, or scaling
engineering capacity to build a product that will generate revenue in 9 months.
The problem arises when headcount growth consistently outpaces revenue growth because:
(1) revenue per employee is declining — the team is becoming less productive, not more;
(2) burn multiple is increasing — each unit of new ARR is costing more capital to acquire;
(3) the company has hired to a growth forecast that has not materialised, creating a
cost base that cannot be sustained at the actual revenue level. The consequence is
always the same: a correction event — either a layoff that damages culture and morale,
or a fundraise at a punitive valuation to buy time for revenue to catch up.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
Headcount has grown more than 40% in the last 12 months AND ARR has grown less than
20% in the same period AND burn multiple is above 3x AND the company is raising
its next round based on a growth trajectory that requires the higher headcount
to deliver — the capital efficiency assumption of the model is already broken.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) Headcount growth preceded revenue by design and the pipeline can explain the lag —
a company that hired 10 enterprise salespeople in Q1 and is showing revenue conversion
from those hires beginning in Q3 is executing a predictable model, not overhiring.
(b) Headcount growth is in engineering for a product launch that occurs within
6 months — the revenue impact of the engineering capacity is staged but identifiable.
(c) The headcount includes a significant number of recent hires who have not yet
reached full productivity — ramp time for enterprise sales is 6–9 months, and the
headcount growth is inflated relative to the productive contribution during ramp.

VERIFICATION:
Request the headcount by function for the last 6 quarters alongside ARR for the same
periods. Calculate revenue per employee quarter-on-quarter. A declining revenue per
employee trend is the most concise summary of the problem. Ask: "What is your revenue
per employee today compared to 12 months ago? And what is your target revenue per
employee at the end of this funding period?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When headcount has grown faster than revenue for 3 or more consecutive quarters,
calculate revenue per employee and burn multiple across the same period before
accepting the company's explanation for the growth investment — the trend line
is the most reliable predictor of the next quarterly result.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T12: VP SALES HIRED BEFORE PRODUCT-MARKET FIT IS DEMONSTRATED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
A VP Sales or Head of Sales has been hired and assigned a revenue target before
the company has demonstrated repeatable sales without the founder — defined as
3 or more arm's-length commercial wins closed by a non-founder salesperson. The VP
Sales hire was made while the founder was still the primary closer and had not yet
documented the sales process in a repeatable form.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A VP Sales is a scaling hire, not a building hire. Their job is to take a repeatable
sales process and build a team around it — not to discover the sales motion from scratch.
A VP Sales hired before PMF is demonstrated will try to apply the playbooks they
know from their prior company to a sales motion that does not yet exist — creating
three common failure modes: (1) they hire junior salespeople who fail because there
is no defined ICP, pitch, or process to guide them; (2) they hit targets in month 1–3
on their personal network, mask the underlying process problem, and the team quota
collapses in month 4–6; (3) they correctly identify that the company does not have
PMF, and leave within 9 months — which costs the company 6 months of their salary,
an option grant, and the time to recruit their replacement. The VP Sales hire before
PMF is one of the most commonly cited early hiring mistakes in post-mortem analyses
of failed startups.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A VP Sales has been hired AND has been in role for more than 6 months AND has not
yet closed a single arm's-length commercial deal without the founder AND the company
is presenting the VP Sales as evidence of commercial maturity in the fundraising process.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The VP Sales has domain expertise that is genuinely additive to the sales process
at this stage — for example, a CISO-turned-sales-leader at a cybersecurity company,
where their credibility opens doors that no standard sales hire could — and their
first deals are closing.
(b) The VP Sales was hired with an explicit brief to work alongside the founder to
document and systematise the sales process before scaling — a process-building role,
not a team-scaling role.
(c) The company has 5+ arm's-length commercial wins and the VP Sales hire is within
the last 3 months — the timing is right, the execution is yet to be assessed.

VERIFICATION:
Ask: "Of your last 10 customer closings, how many were led by the VP Sales without
the CEO in the room? And what is the VP Sales' quota attainment for the last two
quarters?" A VP Sales who has been in role 6+ months and has not yet closed a
deal without the founder is not adding the value the title implies.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a VP Sales is described as a key recent hire, ask for their quota attainment
and the number of deals they have closed without the founder — time in role without
a closed deal signals either wrong hire or pre-PMF stage, and both require the
same follow-up.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T13: GEOGRAPHIC TEAM SPLIT WITHOUT CLEAR RATIONALE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The founding team is split across 2 or more countries or cities — detectable from
LinkedIn profiles showing different locations for co-founders, or from submission
language referencing "our team in London and New York" or "our UK and US offices"
at a stage (seed or early Series A) where geographic separation has no clear
commercial or operational justification.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Geographic splits in early-stage founding teams are almost always explained by one
of three things: a personal circumstance (a co-founder who cannot or will not relocate),
a co-founder relationship that was formed across locations (online, academic conference),
or a deliberate US/UK dual-market strategy. The first two are risk signals; the third
is a strategy that must be evaluated on its own merits.
The operational cost of geographic splitting at early stage is consistently
underestimated: (1) collaboration is asynchronous and slower — product and commercial
decisions take longer to make when the team is not in the same room; (2) culture
is harder to build — the team naturally splits into geographic sub-groups with
different norms and information flows; (3) the company often ends up with a
"real" headquarters where the senior team spends most of their time and a secondary
location that gradually becomes the home of whoever cannot or will not relocate —
creating a two-tier team structure that damages morale and retention.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founding team is split across more than 2 geographies AND the company has fewer
than 10 employees AND no clear market or operational reason exists for the geographic
distribution AND the submission describes the split as "we're a remote-first company"
without addressing the coordination cost — at this stage, remote-first is not a
strategy, it is an accommodation.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) One co-founder is in the primary customer geography (e.g. US) and one is in
the development geography (e.g. UK or Eastern Europe) — a deliberate GTM and talent
cost arbitrage strategy with a clear plan for how it will be consolidated as the
company scales.
(b) The geographic split is temporary — a co-founder is relocating within 6 months
and the split is a transition artefact.
(c) The company is genuinely remote-native (developer tool, open-source, or PLG
product) where geographic presence is not a core operational input and the team
has demonstrated effective distributed collaboration.

VERIFICATION:
Check each co-founder's current LinkedIn location. Ask: "Where does the founding
team spend the majority of their working time? And is there a plan to consolidate
to one location?" The answer reveals whether the split is a strategic choice or
a personal accommodation that has been reframed as strategy.

PATTERN LESSON:
When co-founders are in different countries without a stated market rationale,
ask directly about the collaboration cadence and any plan to consolidate — the
answer distinguishes between a deliberate two-market strategy and a co-founder
relationship that is being managed across geographies by circumstance.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T14: FOUNDER ACADEMIC TITLE WITHOUT COMMERCIAL TRACK RECORD
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: MEDIUM (context-dependent — LOW in deep tech, HIGH in consumer or GTM-led)

SIGNAL:
The founder leads with academic credentials — Professor, Reader, Principal Investigator,
PhD, post-doctoral researcher — as their primary qualification for building the company,
and the submission contains no evidence of prior commercial execution: no prior company
built, no commercial role in industry, no revenue generated outside academia. The
founder's LinkedIn or submission bio is dominated by academic publications, grants,
and research positions rather than commercial achievements.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Academic excellence and commercial execution are distinct skill sets that overlap less
than founders and investors often assume. A Professor with 50 peer-reviewed publications
and a £3M research grant portfolio has demonstrated: the ability to identify a novel
scientific problem, secure non-commercial funding from research bodies, manage a
small team of PhD students and postdocs, and publish findings that survive peer review.
None of these skills directly maps to: identifying a customer willing to pay for the
problem's solution, selling that solution at a price that funds the company, managing
a commercially driven team under financial pressure, or making decisions under
uncertainty without the institutional support of a university.
This flag is context-dependent: in deep tech, quantum, biotech, and advanced materials,
academic founding is not a risk — the technology is the product and the founder's
academic depth is the primary differentiator. In consumer, SaaS, fintech, and
marketplace businesses, it is a HIGH risk because the primary challenge is
not technical — it is commercial.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder is an academic founding a consumer or SaaS business AND has no prior
commercial role AND has not hired or partnered with a commercially experienced
co-founder or COO AND is seeking a Series A with less than £500K ARR — the commercial
execution gap is the critical path dependency and it is unaddressed.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is a deep tech spinout where the technology is the core differentiator
and academic depth is the primary credential — apply the Deep Tech framework.
(b) The academic founder has a commercially experienced co-founder who owns the GTM
function with a verifiable track record.
(c) The academic founder has prior commercial experience that is not the headline
of the submission — a Professor who spent 5 years in industry before returning
to academia has relevant commercial reference points even if they are not leading
the bio.
(d) The company has already demonstrated commercial execution at seed stage —
£500K+ ARR from arm's-length customers is evidence that the founder can sell,
regardless of academic background.

VERIFICATION:
Search for the founder's full career history including industry roles before or during
academia. Ask: "Have you ever been responsible for commercial revenue — either in a
prior company or in an industry role — and if so, what was the context?" The question
probes for commercial execution experience that may not be visible in an academic-led
bio. If the answer is no, assess whether the commercial gap is addressed by co-founder
or hire.

PATTERN LESSON:
When the founder is primarily academic and the business is commercial rather than
deep tech, assess whether a commercially experienced co-founder or operator is
present before weighting the team score — academic excellence is the right foundation
for the technology but not for the sales motion.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG T15: NO VERIFIABLE REFERENCE FROM NAMED PRIOR EMPLOYER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Team
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
A founder claims a senior role — VP, Director, Head of, or equivalent — at a named
prior employer that is verifiable as a real organisation, but no public record
(LinkedIn endorsements, press mentions, company publications, conference speaker
bios, or professional directory listings) confirms the founder held that specific
role at that seniority level at that organisation during the stated tenure period.

DETECTION METHOD:
Search LinkedIn for the named employer's employee history and look for the founder's
profile in their former employees. Search the named company's press releases and
blog posts for the founder's name during the stated tenure. Search Google for
the founder's name + named employer + role title. Search the named employer's
website for historical content that would reference the founder at their stated level.
A complete absence of any corroborating reference for a senior role at a major
organisation after a 15-minute structured search is a flag — not proof of fabrication,
but sufficient to require direct verification.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Senior roles at recognisable employers are verifiable — a VP at Goldman Sachs,
a Director at Google, or a Head of Product at Monzo will appear in press coverage,
conference agendas, LinkedIn endorsements, or internal publications in most cases.
The complete absence of any corroborating evidence for a senior role is either
explained by an unusual circumstance (classified government role, NDA-bound private
equity position, extremely small company where the title was internally meaningful
but externally invisible) or by embellishment. The distinction matters because:
(1) if the role was at the claimed seniority and scale, it is the primary evidence
that the founder can operate at that level — and if it cannot be verified, neither
can the claim; (2) a founder who overstates a prior role will overstate other things.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The unverifiable senior role is cited as the primary justification for why the
founder is uniquely qualified to build this specific company AND no alternative
evidence of comparable capability exists (no prior company, no visible product,
no industry recognition) AND the founder is evasive when asked to provide a
reference from the named employer.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The founder immediately offers a named reference at the prior employer who can
confirm the role — the gap is a public record issue, not a credential issue.
(b) The role was at a company or in a context where public record would not be
expected — government advisory role, early-stage startup, or private-market firm
where external visibility is structurally low — and the explanation is coherent.
(c) The role in question is peripheral to the investment thesis — a supporting
credential rather than the primary justification for the founder's ability to
execute in this market.

VERIFICATION:
Ask directly: "Could you provide the name and contact details of a manager or peer
from your time at [named employer] who could speak to your role and scope?" An honest
founder with a genuine senior role will immediately provide a name. Hesitation,
deflection ("they've moved on"), or the offer of a self-selected junior colleague
rather than a manager is a flag requiring escalation. The reference call itself —
even if not conducted at this stage — is a standard diligence step.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a senior prior role is the primary credential justifying the team score and
cannot be independently verified after a structured search, request a specific named
reference from that role before completing the team assessment — the response to
the request is itself a signal about transparency.
