SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Marketplace):
— Revenue is a percentage of GMV (Gross Merchandise Value) or a transaction fee on
  value exchanged between two or more parties the company did not produce itself
— Company connects a supply side (sellers, providers, workers, landlords) with a
  demand side (buyers, customers, hirers, tenants) and takes a fee for the connection
— Neither side of the platform owns the underlying product, service, or asset being
  transacted — the company facilitates, not manufactures
— Network effects are cited as the primary moat: more supply attracts more demand,
  more demand attracts more supply

CRITICAL DISTINCTION — MARKETPLACE vs RESELLER vs SaaS:
A marketplace does not own inventory. If the company buys stock and resells it, it is
a retailer — not a marketplace. Revenue recognition differs fundamentally.
A company that charges suppliers a SaaS subscription to list on its platform without
taking a transaction fee is a SaaS business with marketplace-like supply, not a marketplace.
Test: does the company's revenue scale with the volume of transactions between third
parties on its platform? If yes — marketplace.

THE FUNDAMENTAL MARKETPLACE EQUATION:
GMV × Take Rate = Gross Revenue
Gross Revenue − Payment Processing − Supply Acquisition − Demand Acquisition = Contribution Margin
Contribution Margin per Transaction must be positive before fixed cost leverage applies.
A marketplace with negative contribution margin per transaction cannot scale to profitability
regardless of GMV growth. This is the first and most important check for every marketplace deal.

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A company that takes on delivery risk (owns the last-mile fulfilment) is a hybrid
  logistics business, not a pure marketplace — apply a blended gross margin test
— A labour marketplace where the company employs the workers (not contracts them) is
  a staffing company; if workers are independent contractors, it is a marketplace
— A financial marketplace that warehouses loans on its own balance sheet is a lending
  company (apply Fintech Library); a marketplace that connects borrowers to lenders
  without balance sheet risk is a true financial marketplace

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. B2C MARKETPLACE
Signals: supply side is businesses or professional sellers; demand side is individual
consumers; revenue is transaction fee or take rate on consumer spend; key metrics
are GMV, consumer repeat purchase rate, and average order value; competitive set
includes Amazon, eBay, Airbnb, Deliveroo, or a category-specific incumbent; growth
metrics reference app downloads, consumer DAU/MAU, and supply listing count.
Business model: take rate 10–30% of GMV (varies by category). Consumer marketplaces
with physical delivery face COGS in the 5–15% of GMV range for payment and logistics.
Key risk: consumer demand is fickle — retention requires habitual use or strong
incentive. Supply quality is the primary driver of demand retention; poor quality
control destroys NPS faster than any competitive threat.

2. B2B MARKETPLACE
Signals: both supply and demand are businesses; transactions are typically larger and
less frequent than B2C; buyer is a procurement function or operations team; seller is
a manufacturer, distributor, or service provider; competitive set includes Faire,
Alibaba B2B, or industry-specific procurement platforms; key metrics include GMV,
average transaction size, buyer repeat rate, and time to second purchase.
Business model: take rate 8–20% of GMV; may also charge suppliers a listing fee or
subscription in addition to the transaction fee.
Key risk: B2B buyers at scale will build direct procurement relationships with top
suppliers — the marketplace becomes disintermediated once buyers and sellers trust
each other. Switching costs must be built into the platform to prevent this.

3. LABOUR / SERVICES MARKETPLACE
Signals: supply side is individual workers, freelancers, or service providers; demand
side is employers, clients, or businesses; company facilitates the hire and may manage
payment, contracts, and compliance; key metrics are GMV (total fees facilitated),
repeat hire rate, time-to-match, and worker retention; competitive set includes
Toptal, Fiverr, Malt, Workmotion, or industry-specific platforms.
Business model: take rate 15–30% of total fees; may include employer subscription for
access to vetted talent pool.
Key risk: high-quality workers are acquired by the employer and transacted off-platform
for repeat engagements once the relationship is established. The disintermediation rate
for labour marketplaces is structurally high unless the platform provides ongoing value
in compliance, invoicing, or benefits administration.

4. FINANCIAL MARKETPLACE
Signals: platform connects borrowers with lenders, investors with opportunities, or
insurance buyers with underwriters; company does not warehouse financial risk on its
own balance sheet (if it does, apply Fintech Library); revenue is an origination fee,
platform fee, or basis points on the value arranged; key metrics are volume arranged,
default rate of arranged loans, and investor/lender retention; competitive set includes
Funding Circle, Seedrs, Wise for Business, or a specific financial product platform.
Business model: origination fee 1–5% of loan or investment value; may charge ongoing
servicing fee on the outstanding balance.
Key risk: if the platform takes on even partial balance sheet risk, it crosses into
regulated lending activity requiring FCA authorisation. Regulatory boundary is critical
and must be confirmed before assessment.

5. REAL ESTATE / PROPERTY MARKETPLACE
Signals: platform connects buyers, sellers, landlords, or tenants with properties;
supply side is agents, landlords, or developers listing properties; demand side is
buyers or renters searching; revenue is agent subscription, listing fee, or lead
generation fee rather than a transaction take rate (direct transaction fees on
property sales are rare in UK models); key metrics are listing count, demand-side
engagement (search-to-enquiry rate), and agent retention; competitive set includes
Rightmove, Zoopla, OnTheMarket, or a vertical-specific property platform.
Business model: primarily agent subscription (£200–£1,000/month per branch) plus
premium listing and advertising products. Note: unlike most marketplaces, property
platforms in the UK charge the supply side (agents) regardless of transaction completion.
Key risk: listing count creates a strong network effect but Rightmove's monopoly position
(80%+ of UK buyer enquiries) makes it nearly impossible to displace with a generalist
property marketplace. New entrants must find an under-served segment (commercial,
new build, overseas, rentals) or add a service layer (conveyancing, mortgages).

6. HEALTHCARE MARKETPLACE
Signals: platform connects patients with healthcare providers, or healthcare buyers
with suppliers; supply side is clinicians, clinics, or hospitals; demand side is
patients or healthcare purchasers; revenue is a booking fee, per-appointment commission,
or supplier listing fee; key metrics are booking conversion rate, provider retention,
repeat patient booking rate, and patient NPS; competitive set includes Doctify, Healios,
Dispensed, or a specialist clinical platform.
Business model: booking commission 10–20% of appointment value; may include provider
subscription for practice management features.
Key risk: applies the Clinical Champion Dependency (Healthtech Library PASS Flag 07)
at the marketplace level — platform adoption depends on a small number of high-volume
providers. Also: patient data governance requirements (DSPT, GDPR special category)
apply to any platform handling appointment or clinical data.

7. HORIZONTAL PLATFORM MARKETPLACE
Signals: platform transacts across multiple categories; supply and demand are not
category-specific; network effects are cross-category (a buyer in category A discovers
category B); competitive set includes eBay, Gumtree, Vinted, or Facebook Marketplace;
key metrics are GMV per category, category expansion rate, and cross-category buyer activity.
Business model: take rate 5–15% of GMV; classified ads revenue; premium seller subscriptions.
Key risk: horizontal marketplaces face competition from category-specific vertical
marketplaces that offer better curation and supply quality in each individual category.
Growth in GMV can mask the fact that the platform is losing its most valuable categories
to specialists while retaining lower-value long-tail inventory.

SECTION B — STAGE-SPECIFIC METRIC BENCHMARKS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Format: STRONG (top quartile) | ACCEPTABLE (median) | CONCERN (flag) | FAIL (disqualifying)

NOTE: Marketplace benchmarks centre on GMV, take rate, and contribution margin —
not ARR and NRR. NRR is not applicable to transactional marketplaces. Retention is
measured on both the supply side (seller/provider retention) and demand side (buyer
repeat purchase rate). Both sides must be measured separately — a marketplace can
lose buyers while retaining suppliers and appear healthy until demand collapses.

GMV BENCHMARKS BY STAGE:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PRE-SEED — Establishing liquidity in first geography:
GMV:               STRONG >£500K/month | ACCEPTABLE £100–500K/month | CONCERN <£100K/month
  Note: at pre-seed, GMV absolute level matters less than GMV growth rate and
  whether liquidity has been achieved in a single geography (see Liquidity Threshold below).
Take rate:         STRONG >15% | ACCEPTABLE 8–15% | CONCERN <8% | FAIL: not yet defined
Supply count:      STRONG >200 active | ACCEPTABLE 50–200 | CONCERN <50 | FAIL: <20
Demand (buyers):   STRONG >1,000 active monthly | ACCEPTABLE 200–1,000 | CONCERN <200
Fill rate:         STRONG >60% of searches result in transaction | ACCEPTABLE 35–60% | CONCERN <35%
MoM GMV growth:    STRONG >20% | ACCEPTABLE 10–20% | CONCERN 5–10% | FAIL <5%

SEED — Proving liquidity and unit economics:
GMV:               STRONG >£2M/month | ACCEPTABLE £500K–2M/month | CONCERN <£500K/month
Net revenue:       STRONG >£300K/month | ACCEPTABLE £50–300K/month | CONCERN <£50K/month
  (Net revenue = GMV × Take rate, minus payment processing)
Contribution margin (net revenue minus variable supply + demand acquisition):
                   STRONG >30% of net revenue | ACCEPTABLE 10–30% | CONCERN 0–10% | FAIL <0%
Take rate:         STRONG >18% | ACCEPTABLE 10–18% | CONCERN <10% | FAIL: declining QoQ
Demand retention (buyers who transact again within 90 days of first purchase):
                   STRONG >50% | ACCEPTABLE 30–50% | CONCERN 15–30% | FAIL <15%
Supply retention (active suppliers retained month-over-month):
                   STRONG >85% | ACCEPTABLE 70–85% | CONCERN 55–70% | FAIL <55%
MoM GMV growth:    STRONG >15% | ACCEPTABLE 8–15% | CONCERN 3–8% | FAIL <3%

SERIES A — Scaling in primary geography; beginning second geography:
GMV:               STRONG >£5M/month | ACCEPTABLE £2–5M/month | CONCERN <£2M/month
YoY GMV growth:    STRONG >120% | ACCEPTABLE 70–120% | CONCERN 35–70% | FAIL <35%
Net revenue:       STRONG >£800K/month | ACCEPTABLE £200–800K/month | CONCERN <£200K/month
Gross margin (net revenue minus payment processing COGS):
                   STRONG >70% | ACCEPTABLE 55–70% | CONCERN 40–55% | FAIL <40%
Contribution margin: STRONG >35% of net revenue | ACCEPTABLE 20–35% | CONCERN <20% | FAIL <0%
Demand repeat rate (% buyers transacting 3+ times in last 12 months):
                   STRONG >40% | ACCEPTABLE 25–40% | CONCERN 10–25% | FAIL <10%
Supply NPS:        STRONG >50 | ACCEPTABLE 30–50 | CONCERN 10–30 | FAIL <10
Demand NPS:        STRONG >60 | ACCEPTABLE 40–60 | CONCERN 20–40 | FAIL <20
Disintermediation rate (estimated off-platform transactions as % of known matches):
                   STRONG <5% | ACCEPTABLE 5–10% | CONCERN 10–20% | FAIL >20%

SERIES B — Multi-geography; monetisation expansion:
GMV:               STRONG >£20M/month | ACCEPTABLE £8–20M/month | CONCERN <£8M/month
YoY GMV growth:    STRONG >80% | ACCEPTABLE 40–80% | CONCERN 20–40% | FAIL <20%
Take rate:         STRONG stable or improving | CONCERN declining for 2+ consecutive quarters
Gross margin:      STRONG >72% | ACCEPTABLE 60–72% | CONCERN 50–60% | FAIL <50%
Contribution margin: STRONG >40% of net revenue | ACCEPTABLE 25–40% | CONCERN <25%
Active geographies: STRONG ≥3 cities/regions with positive contribution margin each
Supply concentration: STRONG: top 10 suppliers <20% of GMV | CONCERN: top 5 >35% | FAIL: top 3 >50%
Demand concentration: STRONG: top 10 buyers <15% of GMV | CONCERN: top 5 >30% | FAIL: top 3 >40%

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TAKE RATE BENCHMARKS BY SUB-CATEGORY:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

B2C Marketplace (physical goods):        12–20% of GMV (Amazon: ~15%; eBay: ~12%)
B2C Marketplace (services/experiences): 15–30% of GMV (Airbnb: ~15%; Deliveroo: 25–30%)
B2B Marketplace:                         8–18% of GMV (Faire: 15–25% net of promotions)
Labour / Services Marketplace:           15–30% of total fees (Fiverr: 20%; Toptal: margin embedded)
Financial Marketplace (loan origination): 1–5% of loan value; 0.5–2% annual servicing
Property Marketplace:                    Subscription model — take rate concept does not apply
Healthcare Marketplace:                  10–20% of appointment value
Horizontal Platform:                     8–15% of GMV; plus classified listing fees

FAIL condition: take rate below the category minimum is only acceptable if the company
  has an explicit plan to reach category minimum within 12 months and can demonstrate
  why suppliers accept a below-market fee (e.g., volume guarantee, superior tools).

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
LIQUIDITY THRESHOLD — MINIMUM VIABLE MARKETPLACE:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A marketplace is self-sustaining in a geography when: a buyer searching for a
product or service can find a suitable match within a reasonable time frame, transact,
and return again without the platform needing to subsidise the transaction to occur.

Minimum liquidity threshold signals by sub-category:
B2C goods marketplace:        >500 active SKUs in the primary category; >200 active buyers/month;
                              fill rate >50% of searches.
B2C services (e.g., delivery):>200 active service providers in a city; order fill rate >85%;
                              average wait time within category expectation (<30 min for food).
Labour marketplace:           >300 verified active workers in primary skill; time-to-match
                              under 48 hours for 70% of job postings.
B2B marketplace:              >100 active suppliers across the primary category; >50 active
                              buyers placing orders monthly; average re-order within 60 days.
Property marketplace:         >1,000 active listings in target geography; >500 buyer searches/day.

FAIL condition: company has launched a second geography before achieving the liquidity
  threshold in its first geography. This is the geographic overextension failure pattern —
  it creates two half-built marketplaces instead of one dominant one.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
VALUATION BENCHMARKS — Marketplace (2025–2026 market):
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Pre-seed / Seed:  1–4% of annualised GMV | OR 10–25x net revenue | premium for liquidity evidence
Series A:         2–6% of annualised GMV | OR 8–20x net revenue | premium for positive contribution margin
Series B:         3–8% of annualised GMV | OR 6–15x net revenue | premium for multi-geography liquidity
Premium driver:   Positive contribution margin in all active geographies is the single
  most powerful valuation driver. A marketplace with negative contribution margin at
  Series B trades at a significant discount regardless of GMV scale.
Note: Property marketplace (subscription model) values on revenue multiple: 8–20x ARR.
  Rightmove at IPO (2006): £500M market cap on £40M revenue = 12x revenue.
  Auto Trader (2015 IPO): £2.4B on £240M revenue = 10x revenue.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. SUPPLY CONCENTRATION — TOP SUPPLIERS DOMINATE GMV
SIGNAL: The top 5 suppliers account for more than 35% of total GMV. Any single supplier
accounts for more than 15% of GMV. Company may present this as evidence that top suppliers
love the platform — it is actually a structural fragility.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Marketplace power is inverted when a supplier is large enough to threaten
departure. A supplier representing 15%+ of GMV knows it can extract pricing concessions,
lower take rates, or preferential placement by threatening to exit. More critically, if
that supplier leaves — due to building its own direct channel, joining a competitor, or
simply receiving a better commercial offer — the marketplace loses a disproportionate
share of GMV and the associated demand-side NPS collapse that follows.
VERIFICATION: Request GMV attribution by supplier for the top 10 suppliers and calculate
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) concentration score. Ask: "What is the contractual
relationship with your top 3 suppliers? Are they exclusive to your platform or multi-homed?"

02. DEMAND CONCENTRATION — TOP BUYERS DOMINATE GMV
SIGNAL: The top 5 buyers account for more than 30% of GMV (B2B marketplace) or any
single consumer accounts for more than 10% of GMV (unusual and a structural concern
for any B2C marketplace).
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Same inversion applies on the demand side. A B2B buyer representing
10%+ of GMV can negotiate directly with suppliers, bypassing the platform once the
relationship is established. The marketplace has effectively spent its supply acquisition
cost to introduce the buyer to the supplier — and will receive no ongoing benefit if
they transact off-platform.
VERIFICATION: For B2B marketplaces specifically, request cohort GMV breakdown by buyer
size quartile. If the top quartile generates more than 60% of GMV, the marketplace
is a B2B lead generator for enterprise buyers, not a true marketplace.

03. DISINTERMEDIATION RATE RISING
SIGNAL: The company discloses, or an investigation reveals, that a material percentage
of matches made on the platform result in transactions off the platform. Rate has
increased in the last two quarters. The company may describe this as an unverifiable
behaviour without acknowledging it as a rising trend.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Disintermediation is the terminal disease of marketplaces. Once supply
and demand find each other, the platform's value is primarily in discovery — not in
the ongoing relationship. If buyers and sellers can find each other via the platform
and then transact directly at lower cost (by avoiding the take rate), the rational
economic choice for both parties is to do so. A rising disintermediation rate means
the platform is providing discovery value but failing to create sufficient lock-in for
the ongoing relationship.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Do you track off-platform transactions between parties who first
met on your platform? What is your estimate of the off-platform rate? What product
features or contractual terms do you have in place to prevent or disincentivise this?"

04. TAKE RATE COMPRESSION — DECLINING REVENUE YIELD ON GMV
SIGNAL: Take rate (net revenue as % of GMV) has declined for two or more consecutive
quarters. GMV continues to grow but net revenue growth is slower than GMV growth.
Company focuses investor reporting on GMV growth without flagging the take rate decline.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Take rate compression in a marketplace means either the company is
discounting to win volume (pricing-driven growth with deteriorating unit economics)
or supplier power has increased to the point where suppliers are forcing lower fees.
Both are dangerous: the first destroys unit economics; the second signals that the
marketplace's network effect is weaker than the supply side's bargaining power.
VERIFICATION: Calculate take rate for each of the last 6 quarters from GMV and net
revenue. If declining, ask: "What is causing the take rate decline? Is it deliberate
(to win volume) or structural (supplier negotiation)? What is your floor take rate?"

05. GEOGRAPHIC LIQUIDITY TOO THIN TO BE SELF-SUSTAINING
SIGNAL: Company operates in multiple cities or regions but no single geography has
reached the liquidity threshold (see Section B). Each city has enough supply and demand
to generate some transactions but not enough to self-sustain without ongoing subsidies
or marketing spend.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A marketplace with thin liquidity in many geographies is burning supply
and demand acquisition cost simultaneously across all markets without building a dominant
position in any of them. This is the least capital-efficient way to build a marketplace
and typically results in all geographies being abandoned as the company runs out of
money, or the company retreating to one geography after burning two rounds of capital.
VERIFICATION: For each geography, ask for monthly GMV, active supply count, active demand
count, and fill rate. Calculate which, if any, geographies have reached the liquidity
threshold. A company with 6 active cities and no city at self-sustaining liquidity has
a structural problem, not a scaling story.

06. SINGLE CITY OR REGION DEPENDENCY
SIGNAL: More than 60% of GMV is generated in a single city or region. The company
describes itself as a national or European marketplace but the operational reality is
a city-specific business.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: A marketplace with 60%+ of GMV in one city is operationally a local
marketplace with aspirations of national scale. The TAM is correspondingly smaller.
If the primary city faces competitive attack (a well-funded competitor launches in
that city), the entire business is threatened. Geographic concentration creates fragility
identical to customer concentration in a SaaS business.
VERIFICATION: Request GMV attribution by geography for the top 5 cities/regions.
Calculate the Herfindahl-Hirschman concentration score. Ask: "What is your plan to
reduce geographic concentration and over what timeline?"

07. CHICKEN-AND-EGG UNSOLVED IN SECOND CITY
SIGNAL: Company has achieved liquidity in its first geography. It has launched a second
geography but after 9 months the second geography has less than 30% of the supply
density and less than 20% of the demand volume of the first geography at the same stage.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Solving the chicken-and-egg problem in the first city often relies on
the founder's personal network, a specific anchor supply partner, or a supply-side
seeding strategy that was unique to that geography. Replicating the seeding strategy
in a new city without those unique factors is systematically harder. A company that
cannot demonstrate it has solved the chicken-and-egg problem in its second geography
independently of its first-city playbook does not have a scalable expansion model.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Walk me through how you launched [second city]. What was the specific
supply seeding strategy? How does it differ from how you launched [first city]?
What metrics will you use to determine if [second city] is working?"

08. SUPPLY ACQUISITION COST RISING FASTER THAN DEMAND
SIGNAL: Cost to acquire each new active supplier is rising quarter-on-quarter. The
trend is not correlated with supply quality improvement — it is simply becoming more
expensive to attract suppliers.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Rising supply acquisition cost signals that the marginal supply pool
is harder to attract — either because the easy-to-acquire supply (founder network,
existing platform, inbound referrals) has been exhausted, or because a competitor is
bidding for the same supply. Either scenario means the marketplace's supply moat is
weaker than its demand-side growth might suggest.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What is your cost per newly active supplier by acquisition channel
for each of the last 4 quarters? Which channels are becoming more expensive and why?"

09. OFF-PLATFORM TRANSACTION RATE ABOVE THRESHOLD
SIGNAL: Company's own estimate of off-platform transaction rate (buyers and sellers
transacting without using the platform after meeting on it) is above 10%. Or the
company has never measured it.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: An off-platform transaction rate above 10% indicates the platform
is not providing sufficient additional value beyond the initial match to justify its
take rate for repeat transactions. The take rate is the cost of disintermediation
avoidance — and if 10% of pairs are choosing not to avoid it, the take rate is too
high relative to the platform's ongoing value delivery.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Have you estimated the rate at which supply and demand transact
off your platform after meeting on it? What data do you have for this? What product
or commercial features do you use to prevent off-platform transactions?"

10. NO SUPPLY EXCLUSIVITY IN COMPETITIVE CATEGORY
SIGNAL: In a category where competitors are actively recruiting the same supply (e.g.,
freelancers on multiple labour platforms, restaurants on multiple delivery platforms),
the company has no exclusivity agreements, no structural supply lock-in, and no
proprietary tool that makes the platform meaningfully stickier than alternatives.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Multi-homed supply — supply that operates on two or more competing
platforms simultaneously — is structurally available to the highest-paying or most
convenient demand. A marketplace without at least partial supply lock-in is dependent
on demand liquidity alone to retain supply. If a competitor achieves higher demand
density, supply switches en masse. This is the fastest form of marketplace collapse.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your supply is exclusively on your platform?
What incentives or tools create supply stickiness? How many of your top 50 suppliers
are also active on [named competitor platform]?"

11. REGULATORY RISK TO CORE MARKETPLACE MODEL
SIGNAL: Company's core marketplace model is built on a worker, housing, lending, or
content model that is under active regulatory review — e.g., gig economy worker
classification, short-term rental restrictions, unlicensed financial intermediation,
or algorithmic pricing for housing.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A regulatory reclassification of workers as employees (as happened in
Spain, France, and the UK's Uber ruling) fundamentally changes the cost structure of
a labour marketplace. A city restricting short-term rentals (as Berlin, Barcelona, and
New York have done) directly reduces the supply available to property marketplaces.
A financial marketplace facilitating lending without regulatory clarity is the Regulatory
Sandbox Illusion pattern from the Fintech Library applied to marketplace economics.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Which specific regulatory changes could materially affect your
marketplace model? What is your regulatory risk assessment? Have you received any
regulatory correspondence or inquiries about your model?"

12. NEGATIVE UNIT ECONOMICS ON SUPPLY ACQUISITION
SIGNAL: Cost to acquire a new active supplier, amortised over that supplier's expected
tenure on the platform, exceeds the total contribution margin expected from that supplier
over their lifecycle. The marketplace is destroying value on every new supplier it adds.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Negative supply-side unit economics is a structural failure, not a
growth investment. Unlike demand-side subsidies (which are sometimes justified during
a liquidity-building phase), paying more to acquire supply than that supply will ever
return is not a scalable strategy — it is a cash consumption spiral dressed as growth.
VERIFICATION: Calculate: average supply acquisition cost (by channel) ÷ average monthly
contribution margin per supplier × average supplier tenure in months. If this ratio
is negative or below 1.0x, unit economics are structurally broken. Ask: "What is your
LTV:CAC for suppliers by acquisition channel?"

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE LIQUIDITY COLLAPSE
DESCRIPTION: A marketplace achieves a fragile liquidity state in its primary geography —
enough transactions to appear healthy in aggregate, but not enough redundancy to absorb
supply or demand exits. A competitor launches in the same geography and wins the top
25% of supply by offering better commercial terms. Demand NPS immediately deteriorates
because the best supply is no longer available. Lower demand NPS causes the marginal
buyers to reduce purchase frequency. Reduced demand causes more supply to leave in
search of better utilisation on the competitor platform. The marketplace enters a
downward spiral — each supply exit reduces demand quality, which causes more supply
to exit. This is a liquidity collapse: a non-linear, rapid deterioration of platform
quality that does not stabilise until the marketplace has lost most of its volume.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Top-decile supply represents more than 40% of GMV (meaning the
loss of 10% of suppliers would cause 40% GMV decline); supply NPS is trending down
while demand NPS appears stable (demand does not yet know the best supply is unhappy);
a well-funded competitor has launched in the primary geography; take rate has recently
increased, making the competitor's lower take rate more attractive to supply; no
supply exclusivity or lock-in mechanism exists.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any marketplace where a well-funded competitor has entered the primary
geography and where supply has no contractual or structural lock-in to the platform.

2. THE GEOGRAPHIC OVEREXTENSION
DESCRIPTION: A marketplace achieves early traction in its first city and raises a
Series A. Under investor pressure to demonstrate national or European scale, it launches
in 4–6 new cities simultaneously. Supply and demand acquisition cost in each new city
is budgeted at the same level as the first city — but the first city benefited from
founder relationships, press coverage, and organic referral that does not transfer.
Each new city requires 3x the budgeted acquisition spend to reach the same supply and
demand density. 12 months later, the company has 7 cities with half the liquidity
threshold in each, a burn rate that is 3x the Series A budget, and none of the cities
close to self-sustaining. It raises a bridge round at a flat valuation to consolidate
into 2 cities. The 5 abandoned cities have been seeded with supply and demand who now
have a bad experience of the platform and are unlikely to re-engage when the company
tries to return.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Company has launched more than 3 geographies before any single
geography has achieved positive contribution margin; the launch timeline for each new
city is 6–8 weeks (insufficient to build real liquidity); the board deck shows a city
count increasing while no city-level P&L has been shared; supply acquisition cost per
city is increasing rather than decreasing with each new city launch (no playbook scaling).
WHEN TO APPLY: Any marketplace at Series A or B that has launched or is planning to
launch more than 3 geographies before its first geography is contribution-margin positive.

3. THE DISINTERMEDIATION SPIRAL
DESCRIPTION: A marketplace achieves strong early GMV growth. Investigation reveals that
a growing percentage of buyers and sellers are using the platform for discovery and then
transacting directly to avoid the take rate. The company receives less and less revenue
per match made. In response, it increases investment in features to drive repeat platform
transactions — but because supply and demand can communicate directly (via the information
exchanged in the initial platform transaction), the features add marginal value. The
platform's take rate is a tax on ongoing transactions between parties who no longer need
the platform for the relationship. GMV measured on-platform stagnates while off-platform
volume grows. The company is increasingly a directory that charges for initial discovery
but cannot monetise the ongoing relationship.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Labour marketplaces: repeat hire rate is high but platform repeat
hire rate is lower than inferred total repeat hire rate from worker capacity utilisation;
B2B marketplaces: average transaction size is increasing while transaction frequency per
buyer-seller pair is decreasing (first transaction is large, then they negotiate direct);
company cannot produce an off-platform transaction rate estimate; buyers and sellers are
allowed to exchange contact details within the platform interface without friction.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any labour or B2B marketplace where transactions are high-value and
relationship-dependent — the higher the value of a single transaction, the stronger
the economic incentive to avoid the take rate for the next one.

4. THE TAKE RATE TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A marketplace with thin unit economics decides to improve its financials
by raising its take rate from 12% to 18% — a 50% revenue increase for the same GMV.
The take rate increase makes the platform economically worse for suppliers and slightly
worse for buyers (through price increases). The top-decile suppliers, who have sufficient
volume and direct relationships to operate independently, immediately begin routing
demand off-platform. The marginal supply, who do not have enough volume to sustain
off-platform operations, stay on the platform but become less engaged. GMV falls 20%
as the top suppliers reduce platform activity. Net revenue is flat at best — the take
rate increase was partially offset by GMV decline. The company is now in a worse position:
lower supply quality, lower demand NPS, and supply that is actively looking for
lower-cost alternatives.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Take rate has been increased by more than 20% in the last 12
months; top-decile supply has no long-term contract or lock-in; supply utilisation rate
(% of available supply capacity being used) has declined since the take rate increase;
supply NPS has declined while demand NPS has not yet deteriorated (the lag between
supply dissatisfaction and demand impact is typically 3–6 months).
WHEN TO APPLY: Any marketplace considering or having recently implemented a take rate
increase where supply has no contractual commitment to the platform.

5. THE SUPPLY QUALITY COLLAPSE
DESCRIPTION: A marketplace grows supply aggressively to meet demand growth targets.
Onboarding standards are relaxed — from a manual vetting process to a self-serve
registration with automated checks. In the short term, supply count doubles and fill
rate improves. Buyers are matched faster. But quality drops: the new supply cohort
performs worse than the original supply (lower quality products, slower delivery, worse
service). Demand NPS falls 15 points over 6 months. Repeat purchase rate declines.
The cost to acquire new demand increases because word-of-mouth referral (the primary
organic demand channel for most marketplaces) weakens as existing buyers stop
recommending the platform. The company is in a demand-side spiral driven by a supply-side
quality decision that was optimised for fill rate rather than buyer experience.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Supply onboarding time has decreased by more than 50% in the
last 12 months; supply approval rate has increased from below 60% to above 85% (without
a corresponding improvement in the quality of applicants); demand NPS is declining
despite fill rate remaining stable; first-order repeat rate (buyers who purchase a
second time after their first order) is declining in recent cohorts.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any marketplace that has materially increased its supply onboarding
velocity without a documented change in the quality of the applicant pool.

6. THE PLATFORM DEPENDENCY TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A marketplace is built on top of another platform — using Instagram for
supply discovery, WhatsApp for buyer-seller communication, Google Shopping for demand
acquisition, or an app store for distribution. The host platform changes its algorithm,
pricing, or API terms. The marketplace's customer acquisition cost doubles overnight,
or its primary communication channel is cut off, or its product is removed from the
app store. The company built a business on infrastructure it does not control and
treated an external platform's policies as a constant rather than a variable.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: More than 50% of demand acquisition comes from a single external
platform (Meta, Google, Apple, Amazon); the marketplace's communication layer runs on
a third-party API (WhatsApp Business API, Twilio) without fallback; supply onboarding
or discovery depends on scraping or accessing data from a platform that has not provided
a commercial API agreement; the company's response to "what happens if Google changes
its algorithm?" is "we have a good relationship with our Google account manager."
WHEN TO APPLY: Any marketplace where a single external platform's policies account for
a critical element of demand acquisition, supply onboarding, or buyer-seller communication.

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. LIQUIDITY METRICS IN THE PRIMARY GEOGRAPHY
QUESTION: "In your primary city or region, what is your current active supply count,
active demand count, and fill rate — the percentage of buyer searches or requests that
result in a completed transaction? How have each of these metrics trended over the
last six months?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific numbers for all three metrics; fill rate above 50% and stable
or improving; supply count growing proportionally with demand; trends are upward.
WEAK ANSWER: Describes the primary geography as "performing well" without specific
numbers; cannot separate primary geography metrics from blended platform metrics; fill
rate not tracked.
REVEALS: Whether the marketplace has actually achieved self-sustaining liquidity or
whether it is in a permanently subsidised, pre-liquidity state in every geography.

02. DISINTERMEDIATION RATE AND TREND
QUESTION: "What is your estimate of the rate at which buyers and sellers who meet on
your platform transact off the platform for subsequent transactions? How do you measure
this? Has the rate changed in the last 12 months? What specific mechanisms do you have
in place to prevent or reduce off-platform transactions?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific estimate (even a range is acceptable) based on a methodology
(survey data, repeat transaction gap analysis, worker utilisation vs platform volume);
describes specific product or contractual mechanisms (messaging restrictions, payment
escrow, quality guarantees only for on-platform transactions) that reduce off-platform
incentive.
WEAK ANSWER: "We know some transactions happen off-platform but it's not significant";
cannot quantify the rate; describes off-platform transactions as "not measurable";
has no specific mechanism to reduce it.
REVEALS: Whether the company understands its biggest structural risk and is managing it
proactively, or whether it is choosing not to measure it because the answer would be uncomfortable.

03. TAKE RATE SUSTAINABILITY AND FLOOR
QUESTION: "What is your current take rate and how has it changed over the last eight
quarters? What is the minimum take rate at which your business model is financially
viable? Have you tested supply price sensitivity — do you know how your top suppliers
would respond to a take rate increase of 5 percentage points?"
STRONG ANSWER: Stable or improving take rate with a specific history; names a floor take
rate and explains the cost structure that determines it; has tested price sensitivity
on a cohort and can describe the supply response.
WEAK ANSWER: "Our take rate is in line with the market"; cannot produce quarterly
take rate history; "we have room to increase it as we grow" without testing supply
response; does not know where the floor is.
REVEALS: Whether the take rate is a managed, strategic variable or an unexamined
assumption — and whether the company has pricing power over its supply side.

04. SUPPLY ACQUISITION COST AND TREND
QUESTION: "What is your cost to acquire a new active supplier, broken down by acquisition
channel, for each of the last four quarters? Which channels have the best LTV:CAC for
suppliers? How has the blended supply acquisition cost trended?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific cost by channel; identifies the highest-ROI supply acquisition
channel; blended supply CAC is stable or declining as the playbook matures.
WEAK ANSWER: Provides a blended number without channel breakdown; "most supply comes
organically" without a specific rate; cannot confirm whether supply CAC is rising.
REVEALS: Whether supply acquisition is becoming more or less efficient and whether the
company's supply growth is economically sustainable.

05. GEOGRAPHIC EXPANSION PLAYBOOK
QUESTION: "When you launched your second city, what specifically was different about
the approach compared to your first city? What was the playbook and what did you learn?
How does your cost per city to reach the liquidity threshold compare between city one
and city two?"
STRONG ANSWER: Describes a documented, repeatable launch playbook; second city reached
liquidity threshold faster and at lower cost than first city; names the 3–5 critical
steps in the playbook and explains why each one works.
WEAK ANSWER: "Our second city is going well"; cannot articulate the specific playbook
steps; cost per city is not tracked; describes each city launch as "unique" rather
than as an iteration of a scalable model.
REVEALS: Whether the company has turned geographic expansion into a repeatable capability
or whether each expansion is an improvised, expensive experiment.

06. OFF-PLATFORM TRANSACTION RATE MEASUREMENT
QUESTION: "How specifically do you measure or estimate the off-platform transaction rate?
What data do you use? And what contractual or product terms make it more expensive or
less convenient for supply and demand to transact off your platform?"
STRONG ANSWER: Describes a specific measurement methodology; has designed product
features that make on-platform transactions more convenient (escrow, invoicing, insurance,
quality guarantee); has contractual provisions requiring on-platform transactions for a
defined period after the initial match.
WEAK ANSWER: "We can't really measure it"; relies entirely on trust and marketplace
goodwill rather than product or contractual mechanisms; "our supply loves the platform
so they don't do this."
REVEALS: Whether the company is proactively managing disintermediation or depending on
goodwill that evaporates the moment a cheaper alternative appears.

07. SUPPLY CONCENTRATION ANALYSIS
QUESTION: "What percentage of your total GMV is generated by your top 10 suppliers?
Your top 3? Is there any single supplier that accounts for more than 10% of GMV?
What is your commercial relationship with your top 3 suppliers — are they on long-term
contracts or do they operate month-to-month?"
STRONG ANSWER: Top 10 suppliers represent below 25% of GMV; no single supplier above
8%; top suppliers are on 12+ month commercial agreements; describes a specific strategy
to reduce concentration as new supply is onboarded.
WEAK ANSWER: Top 5 suppliers are "a significant portion" without a specific number;
cannot confirm whether top suppliers have contractual commitments; "our top suppliers
are very loyal."
REVEALS: Whether the marketplace is structurally fragile to the exit of its most valuable
supply — and whether it has taken commercial steps to reduce that fragility.

08. DEMAND RETENTION BY COHORT
QUESTION: "For buyers who first transacted in each of the last six quarter-cohorts,
what percentage completed a second transaction within 90 days? A third within 180 days?
How does the repeat rate trend across cohorts — are newer cohorts retaining better or
worse than earlier cohorts?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific repeat rates for each cohort; newer cohorts retaining at the
same rate or better than earlier cohorts; retention rate above 40% at 90 days.
WEAK ANSWER: Provides a blended average repeat rate; "most buyers come back"; cannot
produce cohort-level data; cannot confirm whether newer cohorts are retaining differently.
REVEALS: Whether demand-side retention is improving (product is getting better at
creating habitual use) or deteriorating (the earliest buyers were most engaged, newer
ones less so — a signal of supply quality decline or competitor entry).

09. SECOND CITY LAUNCH TIMELINE AND CRITERIA
QUESTION: "By what specific criteria do you decide that you are ready to launch a new
geography? What metrics must your primary city hit before you allocate capital to a
second city? And at what point would you decide that a new city has failed and pull
the resources?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific criteria stated (e.g., primary city at positive contribution
margin, fill rate above 60%, supply retention above 80%); clear launch criteria;
equally clear criteria for exiting a city that is underperforming.
WEAK ANSWER: "We launch when we feel ready"; "we decide based on partner interest";
"we don't have a fixed exit criteria — we'd evaluate at the time."
REVEALS: Whether the company applies capital discipline to geographic expansion or
expands based on enthusiasm and investor expectations — the two most common causes of
geographic overextension.

10. CHICKEN-AND-EGG SOLUTION IN NEW MARKETS
QUESTION: "What is your specific supply seeding strategy for a new city? Which comes
first — supply or demand — and why? What is the minimum supply you need before you
activate demand acquisition? And how do you prevent early demand from having a bad
experience because supply is still thin?"
STRONG ANSWER: Describes a specific, tested supply-first or demand-first strategy with
the rationale for the sequencing; names the minimum supply threshold before demand
activation; has a strategy for managing demand expectations during the thin liquidity phase.
WEAK ANSWER: "We build supply and demand simultaneously"; "we reach out to suppliers
in the new city and start from there"; cannot describe how they prevent early bad
experiences from damaging the brand in a new geography.
REVEALS: Whether the company has a genuine, repeatable answer to the hardest problem
in marketplace building — or whether it is planning to solve it again from scratch in
every new city.

11. REGULATORY RISK ASSESSMENT
QUESTION: "Which specific regulatory changes would have the biggest impact on your
marketplace model? Have you received any correspondence from regulators about your
worker classification, your housing model, your financial facilitation, or your platform
content? What is your regulatory risk management strategy?"
STRONG ANSWER: Names specific regulatory risks with probability and impact assessments;
has taken legal advice; can describe the operational change required if the risk
materialises; has engaged proactively with relevant regulators.
WEAK ANSWER: "We're compliant with all applicable regulations"; "we monitor regulatory
developments"; cannot name a specific regulation that would materially change their model.
REVEALS: Whether the company's leadership understands the regulatory landscape for their
specific marketplace model and is managing it as a business risk or treating compliance
as a box-tick.

12. REVENUE SPLIT — TRANSACTION FEES VS SUBSCRIPTION VS DATA
QUESTION: "What is the breakdown of your revenue between transaction fees (take rate),
supply-side subscriptions, demand-side subscriptions, and any data or advertising
products? How has this mix changed over the last 12 months? And what is your strategy
for growing the non-transaction revenue components?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific percentage breakdown by revenue type; transaction fees are not
the only revenue source; non-transaction revenue (subscription, data) is growing faster
than transaction revenue as a percentage; describes a specific strategy for expanding
the subscription or data product layer.
WEAK ANSWER: "Almost all of our revenue is transaction fees"; "we plan to add
subscriptions in the future"; cannot describe the roadmap or timeline for non-transaction
revenue development.
REVEALS: Whether the company is building a defensible, diversified revenue model or
is entirely dependent on transaction volume growth — the latter creates existential
vulnerability to any GMV disruption.

GIG ECONOMY WORKER CLASSIFICATION (UK AND EU):

UK Worker Status — The Uber Ruling and Its Implications:
  The Supreme Court's 2021 Uber judgment established that platform workers who cannot
  set their own prices, cannot build a customer base, and operate within a system
  controlled by the platform are "workers" under UK employment law — not independent
  contractors.
  Worker status in the UK grants: national minimum wage, paid annual leave, protection
  against unlawful deduction from wages.
  Employee status (a further step beyond worker) grants: unfair dismissal protection,
  redundancy pay, sick pay.
  Current platform companies affected: delivery (Deliveroo — partially), private hire
  (Uber), cleaning (urban cleaning platforms), freelance (some elements of labour marketplaces).
  Alex flag: any UK labour marketplace where the platform controls pricing, work allocation,
  and customer relationships without giving workers genuine commercial independence should
  be assessed against the Uber worker criteria. If workers meet the Uber test, the
  current cost model does not include minimum wage and holiday pay — and the unit
  economics will change materially if worker status is reclassified.

EU Platform Work Directive (2024):
  The EU Platform Work Directive, formally adopted in 2024, establishes a rebuttable
  presumption of employment for platform workers across EU member states.
  Key provisions:
  — Workers are presumed to be employees unless the platform can demonstrate they are
    genuinely self-employed on at least 2 of 5 prescribed criteria.
  — Platforms must maintain transparent algorithmic management systems.
  — Workers must be informed about how algorithmic decisions affect their work.
  Member states have 2 years to transpose the Directive into national law.
  Alex flag: any marketplace with EU operations that uses algorithmic dispatch, pricing,
  or supply allocation and classifies workers as independent contractors should assess
  its exposure to the Platform Work Directive before Series A. The cost of reclassification
  (employer NI, paid leave, minimum wage compliance) can increase variable labour costs
  by 25–40%.

DIGITAL SERVICES ACT (DSA) — EU:
  The DSA came into force for Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) with >45M EU monthly
  active users in August 2023; for all other platforms from February 2024.
  Key requirements for marketplace platforms:
  — Trader verification: marketplaces must verify and display the identity of traders
    selling on the platform ("Know Your Business Customer").
  — Illegal content removal: platforms must have systems to detect and remove illegal
    product listings; failure triggers significant fines.
  — Algorithmic transparency: recommendation systems must be disclosed; users must have
    the option to use a non-profiling-based alternative.
  — Annual risk assessment: large platforms must conduct and publish annual risk assessments
    for systemic risks (illegal goods, fundamental rights, electoral integrity).
  Alex flag: any B2C marketplace with EU users has DSA obligations from February 2024.
  Trader verification is a compliance baseline. A marketplace that has not implemented
  KYBC (Know Your Business Customer) for EU sellers is in breach of DSA and exposed
  to fines of up to 6% of global annual turnover.

FINANCIAL MARKETPLACE REGULATION:
  Any marketplace facilitating lending, investment, or regulated financial products
  requires FCA authorisation. Specifically:
  — Loan-based crowdfunding platforms: FCA authorisation as a regulated activity.
  — Investment-based crowdfunding platforms: FCA authorisation under FSMA.
  — Buy now pay later facilitation: BNPL regulation (imminent in 2025–2026) will
    require FCA authorisation for platforms facilitating BNPL at checkout.
  PSD2 / Open Banking implications: marketplaces that initiate payments on behalf of
  buyers require PI (Payment Institution) authorisation or must partner with an
  authorised payment processor.
  Alex flag: any marketplace facilitating financial transactions — including BNPL at
  checkout, escrow services, or investment facilitation — must confirm its regulatory
  status before any revenue projection based on those products is credible.

PROPERTY MARKETPLACE REGULATION — MATERIAL INFORMATION:
  UK National Trading Standards Estate and Letting Agency Team (NTSELAT) guidance (2023)
  requires property portals and agents to disclose Material Information in three parts:
  Part A: Property type, tenure, price — mandatory at listing.
  Part B: Utilities, construction, flood risk — mandatory at listing.
  Part C: Restrictive covenants, rights of way, planning — required before sale.
  Non-compliance: Trading Standards enforcement; Advertising Standards Authority action.
  Alex flag: any property marketplace that does not enforce Material Information disclosure
  at the point of listing is in breach of NTSELAT guidance and liable to enforcement action.
  This is an operational requirement, not an optional feature.

FOOD DELIVERY — FOOD SAFETY AND ALLERGEN REGULATIONS:
  Marketplaces facilitating food delivery are subject to Food Safety Act 1990 and the
  Food Information to Consumers (FIC) Regulation 2021.
  Key obligations: allergen information must be displayed for all food listed on the
  platform; marketplaces may be co-liable with restaurants for allergen failures.
  Food Standards Agency enforcement: fixed penalty notices and prosecution for
  non-compliant food businesses listed on a platform.
  Alex flag: any food delivery marketplace must confirm it has an allergen information
  display system for every listed restaurant and a process for removing non-compliant
  listings. The Natasha's Law (2021) requirements are minimum compliance — not best practice.

ONLINE SAFETY ACT 2023 — UK CONSUMER PLATFORMS:
  The Online Safety Act 2023 (in force from 2024 under Ofcom's phased implementation)
  imposes new duties on user-to-user platforms and search services.
  Relevant to marketplaces: any marketplace where buyers and sellers can communicate
  is a user-to-user platform.
  Category 1 duties (largest platforms): illegal content duties, children's safety,
  transparency reporting.
  Category 2 duties (all in-scope platforms): systems and processes to identify and
  remove illegal content; risk assessments.
  Alex flag: consumer-facing marketplaces with user-to-user communication features must
  have conducted an Online Safety Act risk assessment and implemented proportionate
  systems for illegal content identification. Non-compliant platforms face Ofcom fines
  of up to 10% of global annual turnover.

TAX REPORTING — DAC7 (EU) AND HMRC (UK):
  DAC7 (EU Directive, in force from January 2023): requires digital platforms operating
  in the EU to report seller data (income earned through the platform, identity) to
  EU tax authorities annually.
  HMRC equivalent (from January 2024): UK platforms must report seller data to HMRC
  for sellers earning above £1,000/year on the platform.
  Affected platforms: all two-sided marketplaces where third-party sellers earn income,
  including goods marketplaces, services marketplaces, and accommodation platforms.
  Implementation cost: significant system development to collect, verify, and report
  seller tax identification data in the required format.
  Alex flag: any marketplace with EU or UK sellers must have DAC7 / HMRC reporting
  systems in place. A marketplace that has not implemented seller tax ID collection
  for EU sellers since January 2023 is in breach of DAC7 and exposed to enforcement
  action in each member state where it operates.

CONSUMER PROTECTION — B2C MARKETPLACE OBLIGATIONS:
  Consumer Rights Act 2015: consumers have the right to goods that are as described,
  of satisfactory quality, and fit for purpose. Marketplaces that present third-party
  goods under their own brand may share liability for product failures.
  Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013: 14-day right of return for goods purchased
  online; marketplaces must ensure sellers comply.
  CMA (Competition and Markets Authority): the CMA has taken enforcement action against
  marketplaces for fake reviews (2024 Digital Markets, Competition and Consumer Act
  powers) and for misleading pricing practices (pressure selling, countdown timers).
  Alex flag: any B2C marketplace using countdown timers, scarcity signals ("only 2 left"),
  or star ratings without robust review verification systems is exposed to CMA action
  under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumer Act 2024 — which gives the CMA
  direct enforcement powers without requiring court action.
