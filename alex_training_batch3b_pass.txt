PASS DEAL 021 — THE WINNER-TAKE-MOST LATE ENTRY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

PATTERN: Winner-Take-Most Late Entry
SECTOR: B2B Marketplace — Wholesale Trade / Independent Retail
STAGE: Series A
FOUNDER ARCHETYPE: Type 8 (Enabler — former VP Merchandising at a major UK grocery chain)

THE DEAL AS SUBMITTED:
A wholesale marketplace connecting independent UK retailers (convenience
stores, delis, farm shops) with 600+ small and emerging consumer brands.
Independent retailers currently buy from 15–20 different suppliers with
no centralisation, inconsistent credit terms, and no digital ordering.
The product consolidates purchasing into one dashboard: order from multiple
brands, unified invoice, 30-day credit terms on all suppliers. The founder
spent 12 years managing supplier relationships for a top-four UK grocer.
She knows the supply chain deeply. ARR: £340K from transaction fees (1.8%
take rate on GMV). GMV: £18.9M annualised. 1,400 active retailers. 620
active brands. NPS from retailers: 74. Growing 14% MoM. Raising £4M
Series A.

THE SIGNAL ALEX MUST CATCH:
Faire, the US-originated B2B wholesale marketplace, launched in the UK
in 2021 and had raised $1.5B by the time this company began its Series A
process. Faire operates in exactly this market — independent retailers,
emerging brands, consolidated ordering, net-60 credit terms — and has
already signed more than 700,000 retailers globally. The submission
does not name Faire in the competitive landscape section. It lists
"traditional wholesale distributors and trade show relationships" as
the competition. This omission is either a red flag about founder
awareness or a deliberate evasion. Faire is the market. The window
to build a competitive network closed when Faire's UK retailer count
crossed 100,000.

WHY THIS FOOLS JUNIOR ANALYSTS:
The founding team is credible. The product addresses a genuine friction
point that any independent retailer would recognise. The metrics look
healthy: 1,400 retailers, 620 brands, positive NPS, growing GMV.
Junior analysts are drawn to the clear problem, the domain expertise,
and the unit economics potential of marketplace businesses. They do not
ask who else is in the market or how many retailers the leader already has.

THE CORRECT ANALYSIS:
Step 1 — Identify the network effect structure. Wholesale marketplaces
exhibit strong cross-side network effects: more retailers attract more
brands, more brands attract more retailers. Once a marketplace reaches
critical mass on both sides, new entrants cannot offer equivalent
selection or liquidity. Faire has critical mass. This company has 1,400
retailers vs Faire's 700,000+ globally, tens of thousands in the UK.

Step 2 — Assess the defensibility window. In winner-take-most markets,
the competitive window closes when the leader reaches approximately
10x the challenger's scale on the supply or demand side. Faire's UK
retailer base is estimated at 40,000–60,000. This company has 1,400.
The ratio is 30–40x. The window has closed.

Step 3 — Model the capital requirement to compete. To reach parity with
Faire's UK network would require replicating their GMV subsidy programme
(Faire offers 60-day net terms with bad debt absorption), their brand
onboarding team, and their retailer acquisition spend. Faire's total
capital raised: over $1.5B. This company is raising £4M. The capital
asymmetry is not a gap — it is a wall.

Step 4 — Apply the product differentiation test. The submission claims
differentiation on "UK-first curation" and "better brand discovery tools."
These are feature differences, not structural advantages. Faire can
replicate UK curation with its existing brand onboarding team. Feature
parity in a network-effect market does not create competitive advantage —
the winner's advantage is the network itself, not the features.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS TO A YES:
A defensible niche that Faire structurally cannot serve: a specific vertical
(e.g. UK-only artisan food with provenance certification requirements that
Faire's global platform cannot accommodate) where the network effect is
local rather than global, and where regulatory or certification barriers
create a moat. Evidence of 5%+ retailer churn away from Faire toward this
platform, with documented reasons. These signals would indicate a surviving
niche rather than a head-on competition with a well-capitalised incumbent.

OUTCOME IF INVESTED:
Company grows to £800K–1.2M ARR over 18 months. Faire launches a UK-
specific curation feature. Faire's brand onboarding team begins targeting
this company's 620 brands directly with superior credit terms. Brand-side
churn accelerates. Retailer selection thins. NPS drops. The company pivots
to a vertical niche but is too thinly capitalised to dominate it. Distressed
sale or shutdown.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a marketplace submission omits a well-capitalised incumbent with
10x or greater network scale from its competitive landscape, this is the
Winner-Take-Most Late Entry pattern. Apply: always independently identify
the market leader in any marketplace or network business. If the leader
has 10x or greater scale and the submission does not name them, treat the
omission as either a strategic evasion or a founder awareness gap — both
are disqualifying signals. Do not invest without a credible structural
explanation for how the company survives at scale alongside the incumbent.

PASS DEAL 022 — THE CELEBRITY FOUNDER DISTRACTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

PATTERN: Celebrity Founder Distraction
SECTOR: EdTech / Future of Work
STAGE: Series A
FOUNDER ARCHETYPE: Type 1 (Domain Expert — former Director of Learning at a top-five global
                  consultancy, now a prominent public voice on the future of work)

THE DEAL AS SUBMITTED:
The founder is a well-known name in the talent and learning space: 180,000
LinkedIn followers, regular contributor to Harvard Business Review, keynote
speaker at Davos, SXSW, and the World Economic Forum. Her company builds
a B2B skills intelligence platform: employees self-assess competencies, the
system maps gaps against role requirements and recommends learning pathways.
The product has been in market 22 months. ARR: £680K. 11 enterprise
customers. NPS: 68. Average contract: £62K. The deck is exceptionally
well-produced. The founder's personal brand is woven into every section.
Press coverage from the FT, Wired, and Fast Company is cited. Raising
£5M Series A.

THE SIGNAL ALEX MUST CATCH:
The submission lists the founder's speaking engagements for the prior
12 months: 14 keynote appearances across 9 countries. An international
keynote requires 2–3 days of travel and preparation per engagement.
14 keynotes = approximately 28–42 days consumed. Additionally, the founder
wrote 3 long-form HBR articles (each typically requiring 20–30 hours of
research and drafting) and appeared in 11 podcast interviews. Total
estimated external commitment: 60–75 days of the prior year — equivalent
to 3 months of a working year. The submission also discloses that product
decisions "go through the founder" and that the sales process is "founder-
led for enterprise deals." A founder spending 3 months per year on brand
activities while also being the primary product decision-maker and the
primary enterprise salesperson is structurally overcommitted. The
£680K ARR in 22 months is not evidence of a working machine — it is
evidence of a talented individual doing everything slowly.

WHY THIS FOOLS JUNIOR ANALYSTS:
A founder with 180,000 LinkedIn followers and Harvard Business Review
bylines signals credibility and distribution. The press coverage implies
market validation. The keynote circuit signals that the market takes the
founder seriously. Junior analysts often treat personal brand as a proxy
for product-market fit. They confuse an audience with a customer base
and media coverage with commercial traction.

THE CORRECT ANALYSIS:
Step 1 — Separate brand traction from product traction. The founder
has significant distribution to an audience interested in the future of
work. That audience is not the same as enterprise HR buyers with active
procurement budgets. FT and Wired readers do not sign £62K SaaS contracts.

Step 2 — Calculate the founder's operational bandwidth. 14 keynotes +
3 HBR articles + 11 podcasts = minimum 60–75 days external in year one.
If the founder is also responsible for enterprise sales and product
decisions, and has been building for 22 months, the implied capacity
for company-building is approximately 60–65% of a full working year.

Step 3 — Assess ARR growth rate against operational capacity. £680K
ARR in 22 months = £31K ARR per month. For 11 enterprise customers, this
implies approximately 6 months average sales cycle and the founder closing
deals personally. If the founder is spending 35–40% of their time on
external brand activities, the counterfactual question is: how much ARR
would exist if that 35–40% were redirected to sales and product? The
brand may be accelerating deal sourcing but decelerating deal execution.

Step 4 — Identify the post-investment dependency risk. Series A capital
will be used to hire. But if the founder continues the keynote circuit
at scale — which the speaking agency will push for, and which the
founder clearly enjoys — the management layer underneath her will operate
without consistent founder attention. This is a culture and product
quality risk.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS TO A YES:
A credible COO or CRO hire in place before the Series A closes, with
a documented transition of enterprise sales and product decisions to
that hire. A written commitment from the founder to reduce speaking
engagements to 4 or fewer per year. Evidence of at least 3 enterprise
deals closed by someone other than the founder. These signals would
indicate the company is becoming a business rather than a personal brand
monetisation vehicle.

OUTCOME IF INVESTED:
Company raises the round. Founder continues the speaking circuit — the
invitations accelerate as the fundraising announcement generates press.
The VP Sales hired with Series A capital struggles to close deals without
the founder in the room. Enterprise pipeline stalls. The founder's time
is split further, not concentrated. ARR grows to £1.2M in 18 months —
respectable, but below the Series B threshold needed at this valuation.
The company becomes a sustainable lifestyle business for the founder,
not a venture-scale outcome.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder's submission documents more than 8 external speaking
engagements, media appearances, or published articles in the prior 12
months while also holding founder-led responsibility for sales and product
decisions, this is the Celebrity Founder Distraction pattern. Apply: require
documented evidence of management layer in place before investing. Calculate
external time commitment explicitly. If external commitments exceed 20% of
working days, the Series A is funding a company without a full-time CEO.

PASS DEAL 023 — THE SERVICES REVENUE MASQUERADE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

PATTERN: Services Revenue Masquerade
SECTOR: AI / Data Analytics — Financial Services Compliance
SECTOR: Series A
FOUNDER ARCHETYPE: Type 1 (Domain Expert — former Head of Financial Crime at a Tier 1 bank)

THE DEAL AS SUBMITTED:
A compliance intelligence platform for Tier 2 and Tier 3 banks and
building societies. The product ingests transaction data, applies ML
models to detect suspicious activity patterns, and generates SAR-ready
reports for the MLRO. The founder spent 14 years running financial crime
teams at Barclays. His personal network is the customer base. ARR: £1.4M.
9 customers. Average contract: £156K. Growing. NRR: 122%. Raising £6M
Series A. Gross margin stated as 68%. Product is described as "enterprise
SaaS with an AI layer."

THE SIGNAL ALEX MUST CATCH:
The submission describes three revenue components: "platform licence,"
"implementation and configuration," and "ongoing advisory." The deck
blends all three into a single ARR figure and a single gross margin
figure of 68%. But in a compliance technology product for regulated
banks, "implementation and configuration" means a team of ex-compliance
professionals spending 3–6 months mapping the bank's transaction data
schema, configuring alert thresholds, and training the MLRO team.
"Ongoing advisory" means the company provides a retained compliance
expert to sit alongside the software. These are services revenues with
gross margins of 20–35%, not 68%. The submission does not separate
software ARR from services ARR. If the blended margin is 68%, and
services-heavy compliance tech typically runs 20–35% services margins,
the implied software margin of 80–85% is only achievable if software
revenue is the majority of the ARR. The key question — what is the
software-only ARR and what is its gross margin in isolation — is not
answered anywhere in the submission.

WHY THIS FOOLS JUNIOR ANALYSTS:
NRR of 122% appears in the summary metrics and dominates the impression.
A financial crime domain expert founder is credible and well-networked.
The compliance use case has genuine urgency — banks face FCA enforcement
action for inadequate AML systems. The 68% gross margin seems acceptable
for enterprise software. Junior analysts do not decompose the revenue
into its constituent parts and do not apply different margin benchmarks
to services and software revenue.

THE CORRECT ANALYSIS:
Step 1 — Request the revenue breakdown. The first diagnostic in any
submission that uses "platform + implementation + advisory" language
is: what is the revenue split between recurring software licence and
professional services? If this is not in the submission, flag it
immediately as a required clarification before proceeding.

Step 2 — Apply the compliance tech services ratio test. In compliance
and regtech, implementation services for Tier 2/3 banks typically
represent 40–70% of total contract value in the first year, reducing
to 10–20% in year 2+. If the company has 9 customers and only 2–3 are
past year one, the services component of total ARR may be 55–65%.
On a blended basis: 40% software at 80% margin + 60% services at 30%
margin = blended margin of 50%, not 68%. The 68% margin may reflect
a presentation of partially deferred services revenue.

Step 3 — Assess scalability of the services component. A compliance
software business that requires 3–6 months of bespoke implementation
per customer cannot scale without proportionate headcount in the
implementation function. This is not a leverage model — it is a
consulting model with a software licence attached. At 9 customers and
£1.4M ARR, the company needs approximately 6–8 implementation
specialists. At 90 customers, it needs 60–80. That is not SaaS scaling.

Step 4 — Evaluate the NRR composition. NRR of 122% sounds strong.
But if expansion revenue is driven by additional advisory retainers
(adding a compliance consultant day rate) rather than software seat
expansion or module upsell, the NRR is services expansion, not software
product expansion. Services NRR does not compound the same way
software NRR does — it does not generate operating leverage.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS TO A YES:
Software-only ARR above £700K (50%+ of total) with documented gross
margin on software-only revenue above 75%. Implementation timelines
reducing as the product matures — evidence that the third customer
implemented in 8 weeks versus the first in 20 weeks. A credible path
to a fully self-serve or low-touch implementation model for smaller
bank customers. These signals would indicate a genuine software
business underneath the services wrapper.

OUTCOME IF INVESTED:
Company raises at a SaaS valuation multiple (8–10x ARR). Services
revenue is exposed during Series B due diligence. Series B investors
apply a blended multiple reflecting the services component — 4–5x
blended ARR. Down round. Alternatively: the company continues to
grow but margins do not expand because every new customer requires
the same services investment. Profitability is permanently deferred.
Founder eventually pivots to a pure consulting model.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a submission describes a SaaS product alongside "implementation,"
"configuration," or "advisory" revenue components without separating
their ARR and gross margin contributions, this is the Services Revenue
Masquerade pattern. Apply: require a revenue schedule that separates
recurring software licence from professional services revenue, with
individual gross margins for each. A blended margin above 60% is only
credible if software-only revenue is confirmed as the majority.

PASS DEAL 024 — THE ENTERPRISE ONLY TRAP
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

PATTERN: Enterprise Only Trap
SECTOR: GovTech / Public Sector SaaS
STAGE: Series A
FOUNDER ARCHETYPE: Type 1 (Domain Expert — former Director General at HM Treasury, 19 years
                  in public sector finance)

THE DEAL AS SUBMITTED:
A former senior civil servant built a financial controls and budget
transparency platform for local authorities and NHS Trusts. The product
gives finance directors a consolidated view of committed spend, actuals
vs budget by cost centre, and automated variance alerts — replacing
the Excel-based processes used by 90% of UK public sector finance teams.
ARR: £1.8M. 5 customers. Average contract: £360K. NRR: 108%. G-Cloud
12 listed. Customer 1: a London borough (£380K/year). Customer 2: an
NHS Integrated Care Board (£410K/year). Customer 3: a county council
(£290K/year). Customers 4–5: two further NHS Trusts. All 5 were closed
by the founder using personal relationships from her civil service career.
Raising £7M Series A. The product is excellent.

THE SIGNAL ALEX MUST CATCH:
The submission describes the TAM as "£2.4B — 418 local authorities,
236 NHS Trusts, and central government departments." But the product is
priced at £290K–410K ACV. At these price points, the product is only
viable for: large county and metropolitan councils (approximately 60
out of 418), NHS Integrated Care Boards and large acute Trusts (approximately
42 out of 236 NHS bodies), and selected central government departments.
The realistic customer universe at current pricing is 120–160 public sector
bodies globally — all in the UK, all subject to multi-year procurement
cycles, all requiring OJEU/Find-a-Tender compliance, and all constrained
by annual budget cycles that limit decision-making to one annual procurement
window. At 100% penetration of realistic customer universe at £350K ACV,
total revenue ceiling is £42–56M. That is not a venture-scale business
at a £28M pre-money valuation.

WHY THIS FOOLS JUNIOR ANALYSTS:
The founder's credentials are extraordinary. The ARR per customer is
impressive. NRR is positive. G-Cloud listing signals procurement-readiness.
The product clearly works — customers are paying £400K/year for it.
Junior analysts see strong metrics and a credible domain expert and do
not work backwards from the total customer count at the current price point.

THE CORRECT ANALYSIS:
Step 1 — Apply the customer universe cap. Not all of the stated 654 public
sector bodies can afford or justify £350K/year for budget management software.
Apply a realistic adoption ceiling: large local authorities (population >200K)
= approximately 60 UK bodies. NHS ICBs and acute trusts = approximately 42.
Government departments with devolved finance = approximately 15. Total
realistic universe: 117 bodies. At 350K ACV average: £40.9M revenue ceiling.

Step 2 — Model VC return requirement. At £28M pre-money, assuming 25%
dilution, the post-money is £35M. A 10x return requires an exit at £350M.
At £40.9M revenue ceiling and a 5x revenue exit multiple (generous for
public sector software), the company is worth approximately £200M at
maximum penetration. The return is 5.7x from a 7-year full market
penetration scenario. That is not a venture return.

Step 3 — Assess the path to a larger market. The submission describes
expansion into "European public sector" as the growth path. But EU public
procurement is governed by different frameworks (OJEU), different languages,
and different budget structures in each member state. This is not a growth
path — it is a 10-year project requiring €50M+ of localisation investment.
There is no credible path to 10x the current customer universe without
fundamentally changing the product or the price point.

Step 4 — Evaluate the mid-market path. Could the product be repriced for
smaller local authorities (population <100K) at £30–50K ACV? Potentially,
but that would require a product rebuild (the current product is built for
large finance teams with complex cost structures), a different implementation
model (the current model is highly bespoke), and a different sales motion.
The submission does not mention this path — the founder has not considered it.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS TO A YES:
A credible mid-market tier of the product at £40–60K ACV that can be
deployed in 4–6 weeks with minimal implementation — expanding the customer
universe from 117 to 400+ UK public sector bodies. Evidence that this tier
can be sold without the founder's personal relationships (i.e. via G-Cloud
inbound and framework agreements). The mid-market product revenue ceiling
would be £16–24M from the UK alone, with international expansion credible
at a lower localisation cost. Combined ceiling: £60–80M+. That is the
beginning of a venture case.

OUTCOME IF INVESTED:
Company closes 3 more enterprise customers in year one. Founder's
personal network is the primary pipeline — the network runs to approximately
30 relevant relationships. After year two, inbound pipeline slows. The
company has 8 enterprise customers, £2.9M ARR, and no clear path to 20+
customers without replicating the founder's network. Series B investors
identify the customer count ceiling. Bridge. The company is acquired by
a large public sector IT integrator (Capita, Sopra Steria) for 3–4x
revenue — a reasonable trade sale but not a venture outcome.

PATTERN LESSON:
When an enterprise product is priced at an ACV that limits the global
customer universe to fewer than 200 realistic buyers, this is the
Enterprise Only Trap pattern. Apply: calculate the revenue ceiling by
multiplying realistic customer universe by ACV, then check whether a
10x venture return is achievable from that ceiling at a credible exit
multiple. If the answer is no at maximum penetration, the opportunity
is a trade sale business, not a venture investment.

PASS DEAL 025 — THE PIVOT FATIGUE PATTERN
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

PATTERN: Pivot Fatigue
SECTOR: B2B SaaS — Vertical Software for Hospitality
STAGE: Series A
FOUNDER ARCHETYPE: Type 3 (Repeat Founder — prior company: a consumer app that failed to
                  scale. Root cause presented as "consumer acquisition cost." Not
                  a Type 4 — no specific structural analysis of what failed or why.)

THE DEAL AS SUBMITTED:
The team is now 26 months into their second company. Current product: an
operations management platform for independent hotel groups — front-desk
task management, housekeeping scheduling, and maintenance ticketing in
one mobile interface. ARR: £290K. 18 hotel group customers (2–15 hotels
each). Average contract: £16K. The product has been live in its current
form for 8 months. NPS: 71. Growing 9% MoM. The founder is articulate,
the deck is well-constructed, and the hospitality pain point is real.
Raising £3M Series A.

THE SIGNAL ALEX MUST CATCH:
The submission's "Company History" section discloses: (1) The company
launched in month 1 as a B2C consumer app for hotel guests (digital
concierge). (2) In month 8, after acquiring 400 guest users across 3
hotels with no revenue, the team pivoted to B2B — selling the concierge
app to hotels as a guest experience tool (£3K/year). (3) In month 16,
after 6 hotel customers and £18K ARR, the team pivoted again — the hotel
customers asked for internal operations tools, so the team built a new
product from scratch and discontinued the guest-facing app. The current
product (month 16–26) is version 3. The company is pitching Series A
momentum on 8 months of data from a product that is itself the result
of two prior failures. The three versions of the product have consumed
22 months of the 26 available. Each pivot destroyed the prior customer
base and reset ARR to zero.

WHY THIS FOOLS JUNIOR ANALYSTS:
The current product metrics are real: £290K ARR, 18 customers, 9% MoM
growth, NPS 71. Presented in isolation, these metrics look like an early-
stage company gaining traction. The pivot history is disclosed but framed
as "learning and iteration" — language that normalises the pattern. Junior
analysts are taught that successful founders iterate. They do not distinguish
between product iteration (improving the same product based on user feedback)
and company pivoting (abandoning the product and customer base entirely and
starting again).

THE CORRECT ANALYSIS:
Step 1 — Apply the pivot pattern test. Three products in 26 months:
B2C concierge (months 1–8, abandoned); B2B guest experience (months 8–16,
abandoned); B2B operations management (months 16–26, current). Two full
pivots in 26 months is a rate of one pivot per 13 months. Each pivot has
consumed the prior customer base. This is not iteration — it is serial
restarts with the same team and a depleting capital base.

Step 2 — Assess residual runway and capital efficiency. The company raised
seed capital at month 1. After 26 months and 2 pivots, they are raising
Series A on 8 months of product data. Calculate implied capital consumed
per month of productive output: if the seed was £500K–800K, approximately
£350K–560K was consumed building and abandoning products 1 and 2. The
efficient capital deployed against the product actually being pitched is
the seed balance minus the pivot cost — which may represent only 30–40%
of the seed round.

Step 3 — Evaluate conviction in the current direction. The third product
emerged from customer feedback during the second product phase — hotel
customers asked for operations tools. This origin story is reassuring
(customer-driven) but also concerning: the team did not identify the
operations problem independently; they were told about it by customers
who were already using a product they built. This is a reactive finding,
not a conviction-based insight. Will the founder pivot again if the next
set of customers asks for something different?

Step 4 — Assess team attrition. Two pivots typically produce engineering
team churn — engineers join for a product vision and leave when the
vision is abandoned. The submission lists 6 team members but does not
disclose the team at founding or the attrition rate. Request current
vs original team composition.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS TO A YES:
24 months of uninterrupted execution on the current product (not 8),
with ARR growth demonstrating compounding across at least 4 sequential
cohorts. Evidence of zero further pivots despite customer requests for
adjacent features. A founding team with no changes since month 16. These
signals would indicate the founder has found conviction, not just a third
direction to explore.

OUTCOME IF INVESTED:
Company grows to £600–800K ARR over 12 months. The hospitality market
is fragmented and the sales motion is slow (independent hotels make
purchasing decisions annually). The founder identifies an adjacent

destabilise the business if acquisition slows even modestly.

£4M Series A at implied £15M+ post-money.

differentiation evaporates. The company has no product, data, or switching
cost advantage — only a price advantage that any competitor can replicate.

what was the sales channel and approximate sales cost? This calculation

Step 3 — Estimate CAC from disclosed data. The company has a sales team
(implied by the team section: "VP Sales + 2 Account Executives"). London-
market fully loaded cost for this team: approximately £420K/year. If this
team closed 28 customers in 12 months, sales-attributed CAC = £420K / 28
= £15,000 per customer. At £57K ACV, that is a 3.2-month CAC payback —
excellent. But if the marketing budget (content, events, paid search)
added another £200K, total CAC rises to £22,143 and payback to 4.7 months —
still acceptable. The problem is this cannot be confirmed because the
founder doesn't disclose it. The refusal to disclose, combined with the
non-standard gross margin treatment, is the signal.

Step 4 — Apply the pattern recognition test. Non-standard COGS allocation
+ refusal to disclose CAC + strong headline revenue growth is a specific
combination. This pattern typically indicates one of two things: (a) the
founder genuinely does not track unit economics and is operationally unsophisticated
about the business she is running; or (b) the founder tracks them and does
not disclose them because they are unfavourable. Both scenarios are
disqualifying at Series A. A sophisticated operator founder who built a
profitable prior company should understand COGS allocation.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS TO A YES:
A restated gross margin using standard COGS allocation (infrastructure
and data costs in COGS, not R&D), confirmed above 55%. A customer
acquisition schedule showing CAC by channel with payback below 12 months.
An explanation from the founder of why the prior company's profitability-
first discipline was replaced with a growth-first model, with a credible
strategic rationale. These signals would indicate financial transparency,
not evasion.

OUTCOME IF INVESTED:
Series A closes. Financial due diligence during the Series B reveals true
gross margin of 41% and actual blended CAC of £28,000 (payback: 5.9
months — still acceptable, but not Series A metrics). The Series B
investors apply a lower revenue multiple reflecting the corrected margin.
The valuation step-up is smaller than modelled. Existing investors are
diluted more than expected. Trust between founder and board is damaged
by the disclosure pattern. The company is viable but the relationship
dynamics impair its execution.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a submission presents gross margin with a non-standard footnote
allocating operational costs to R&D, and simultaneously declines to
disclose CAC at £1M+ ARR, this is the Unit Economics Denial pattern.
Apply: reconstruct COGS using standard allocation before accepting any
gross margin figure. Require a customer acquisition schedule for every
customer to date before accepting that CAC is not tracked. A founder
who cannot or will not provide unit economics at Series A is either
unsophisticated or evasive — both are disqualifying signals for a
capital-intensive growth phase.
