SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = Consumer / DTC):
— Primary paying customer is an individual person spending their own money, not a
  business spending a budget allocation — even if the product has an enterprise version
— Revenue model includes subscription charged to personal payment methods, in-app
  purchase, advertising against consumer attention, or direct sale of physical goods
  to an end consumer via the company's own channel (website, app, direct mail)
— Retention is measured in engagement terms (DAU, MAU, session frequency, D30 retention)
  rather than contractual terms (NRR, logo churn, renewal rate)
— Growth is driven by organic word-of-mouth, social referral, viral loops, or paid
  acquisition on consumer channels (Meta, TikTok, Google, Apple Search Ads) — not
  a sales team, account executives, or enterprise procurement

CRITICAL DISTINCTION — CONSUMER vs B2B vs PROSUMER:
A consumer product is one where the user pays personally and the decision to purchase
is not approved by a business. Duolingo is consumer. Rosetta Stone for enterprise
language training is B2B SaaS.
A prosumer product (used personally but expensed to a business) should be assessed
on both frameworks — consumer engagement metrics AND B2B retention metrics.
Test: if the company stopped all paid advertising tomorrow, would it gain new users
from word-of-mouth and organic discovery? If yes — consumer moat potentially present.
If no — it is a paid media business wearing a consumer product's clothes.

THE MOST IMPORTANT CONSUMER METRIC:
Organic share of new user acquisition in the last 90 days. A consumer product with
less than 30% organic acquisition is a paid media business. It does not have a moat —
it has a paid channel. The moment CAC exceeds LTV, the business stops growing.
This single metric determines more about the long-term viability of a consumer business
than any other data point in the submission.

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A consumer app that earns primarily from B2B advertising partnerships (brand deals,
  sponsored content, employer job postings) is partially B2B in its revenue model —
  apply both consumer engagement and B2B revenue benchmarks
— A direct-to-consumer physical brand that sells 40%+ of revenue through retail
  partnerships (Amazon, Boots, ASOS) is not a true DTC company — retail margin,
  retail returns, and shelf placement risk apply; the SaaS Library's distribution
  risk framework applies to the retail channel
— A social media app that earns revenue from influencer subscriptions or creator
  tips is a marketplace (apply Marketplace Library) layered on a consumer engagement
  product (apply Consumer Library) — assess both

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. CONSUMER MOBILE APP
Signals: product is delivered primarily via iOS or Android app; revenue is in-app
purchase, subscription through the app store (taking Apple/Google 15–30% cut), or
advertising; submission references DAU/MAU ratio, retention curves, session length,
or app store ratings; key growth levers are App Store Optimisation (ASO), paid
user acquisition (Meta, TikTok, Google UAC), and viral sharing mechanics; competitive
set includes dominant category apps (Duolingo, Calm, Headspace, Strava) or specific
vertical alternatives.
Business model: freemium (free tier with paid subscription) — typical conversion
to paid is 2–8%; pure subscription (£3–£15/month); or advertising-funded (£0.50–£5
CPM depending on demographic quality).
Key risk: App store algorithm changes (ASO) can reduce organic discovery overnight.
Apple and Google together take 15–30% of in-app subscription revenue (reduced to 15%
after year 1 on both platforms). A company with 100% app store distribution has no
direct relationship with its users — Apple or Google can, in theory, remove the app.

2. CONSUMER SUBSCRIPTION (DIGITAL)
Signals: product is digital content or a digital service delivered on a recurring
subscription basis; consumer pays monthly or annually; submission references subscriber
count, monthly churn rate, annual renewal rate, and average revenue per subscriber;
competitive set includes streaming services, newsletter platforms, online learning
platforms, or category-specific subscription services; churn is the primary operational
metric — not NRR (which applies to B2B).
Business model: direct subscription (£3–£30/month depending on content depth).
Key risk: subscription fatigue — the average UK consumer has 3–5 active digital
subscriptions; adding a new subscription requires displacing an existing one. Churn
in consumer digital subscriptions is structurally higher than B2B — annual renewal
rates of 65–75% are typical; 80%+ is excellent; below 55% is structural failure.

3. DIRECT-TO-CONSUMER PHYSICAL
Signals: company manufactures or sources physical products and sells them directly
to consumers via its own website, app, or direct mail without a retail intermediary;
revenue is product sale; key metrics are gross margin after COGS and returns, repeat
purchase rate, and average order value; primary growth channels are paid social (Meta,
TikTok), influencer marketing, and email/SMS retention; competitive set includes
category-specific DTC brands (Gymshark, Huel, Graze, Bloom & Wild) or retail incumbents.
Business model: product revenue at 50–75% gross margin (before marketing); first-purchase
CAC must be recovered within 2–3 orders for most categories.
Key risk: returns are existential for DTC physical — fashion return rates of 25–40%
can compress gross margin from 55% to 35% after returns processing, shipping, and
restocking. Any gross margin figure that does not account for returns is misleading.

4. CONSUMER MARKETPLACE
Signals: platform connects consumer buyers with individual sellers or service providers;
transaction value is paid through the platform; the company takes a fee; key metrics
are GMV, take rate, buyer repeat rate, and seller retention. Apply Marketplace Library
benchmarks alongside these consumer-specific benchmarks, specifically: buyer NPS above
50 for a healthy consumer marketplace; seller retention above 75% monthly.
Note: consumer marketplaces primarily earn from the supply side (seller fees) while
keeping demand (buyer) access free. Any marketplace charging buyers a fee should
demonstrate that demand is inelastic to the fee — i.e., buyer retention does not
decline when the fee is introduced.

5. CONSUMER FINTECH
Signals: financial product sold directly to individual consumers — current accounts,
savings, investments, insurance, credit, or payments; revenue is interchange, interest
margin, AUM fee, or subscription; submission references active accounts, monthly active
users, and revenue per active user; regulatory licence required (FCA EMI, PI, investment
management, consumer credit); competitive set includes Monzo, Revolut, Freetrade,
Plum, or Hargreaves Lansdown digital. Apply Fintech Library benchmarks alongside
Consumer Library benchmarks — both sets apply.
Key risk: consumer fintech faces both consumer retention risk (users disengage from
apps they find no daily utility in) and regulatory risk (FCA Consumer Duty, BNPL
regulation). A consumer fintech app with low daily active engagement is not providing
the utility that justifies its regulatory cost base.

6. CONSUMER HEALTH AND WELLNESS
Signals: product improves or tracks health, fitness, mental wellbeing, nutrition, or
sleep; sold directly to consumers without a clinician prescribing or recommending it;
key metrics are D30/D90 retention (health behaviour change requires sustained engagement
to be credible), subscription renewal rate, and NPS; competitive set includes Whoop,
Noom, Headspace, Second Nature, Calm, or category-specific alternatives.
Apply Healthtech Library benchmarks for D2C Health (sub-category 7 of Healthtech
Library) alongside these Consumer Library benchmarks. If the product makes clinical
claims, MHRA registration may apply — verify before assessing.
Key risk: habit formation is the product. A consumer health app that cannot demonstrate
sustained engagement beyond Day 90 is not changing health behaviour — it is providing
a temporary novelty. Clinical evidence is rarely required for wellness positioning but
is required if the company is making medical claims to premium buyers.

7. GAMING AND ENTERTAINMENT
Signals: product is a game, interactive entertainment platform, or digital entertainment
service; revenue is in-app purchase (IAP), subscription, advertising, or live events;
key metrics are DAU, average revenue per daily active user (ARPDAU), payer conversion
rate (% of players who spend money), and average revenue per paying user (ARPPU);
competitive set includes mobile gaming studios (King, Supercell, Jam City), esports
platforms, or interactive entertainment platforms.
Business model: freemium game — payer conversion rate 2–5%; ARPPU £10–£50/month
for healthy IAP games. Advertising-funded — ARPDAU £0.02–£0.10 depending on ad format
and demographic. Subscription gaming — £5–£15/month.
Key risk: mobile gaming is a hits business — the top 1% of games generate 90%+ of
revenue. A studio with one successful game must demonstrate whether it has a systematic
process for game development or is dependent on repeating a specific creative formula.

SECTION B — STAGE-SPECIFIC METRIC BENCHMARKS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Format: STRONG (top quartile) | ACCEPTABLE (median) | CONCERN (flag) | FAIL (disqualifying)

NOTE: Consumer benchmarks are engagement-first, monetisation-second. A consumer product
with excellent retention but early monetisation is more investable than a consumer
product with high revenue and poor retention. Retention predicts LTV; LTV predicts
whether the acquisition cost is recoverable. Always assess retention before revenue.

  Best-in-class benchmarks by type:
  Social / entertainment:  Sessions per day >2.0 | Session length >8 min

  Health / fitness:        Sessions per week >3 | Session length >10 min
  Finance:                 Sessions per month >8 | Session length >3 min

ACQUISITION BENCHMARKS — ORGANIC VS PAID SPLIT:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Organic share of new user acquisition (last 90 days):
  STRONG >60% organic | ACCEPTABLE 30–60% | CONCERN 15–30% | FAIL <15%

This is the single most important consumer moat metric. Organic acquisition includes:
direct search, App Store Optimisation (ASO), word-of-mouth referral, press coverage,
and viral mechanics. Paid acquisition includes: Meta/Instagram ads, TikTok, Google UAC,
Apple Search Ads, influencer paid partnerships.

Viral coefficient (K-factor: average new users invited per active user):
  STRONG >0.5 | ACCEPTABLE 0.2–0.5 | CONCERN 0.05–0.2 | FAIL <0.05
  Note: K-factor above 1.0 means the product is genuinely viral — each user generates
  more than one new user. K-factor above 0.5 provides a meaningful organic boost to
  paid acquisition. K-factor below 0.1 means the product has no material word-of-mouth.

CAC by channel (cost to acquire one new paying user):
  Meta / Instagram:         STRONG <£8 | ACCEPTABLE £8–20 | CONCERN £20–40 | FAIL >£40
  TikTok:                   STRONG <£5 | ACCEPTABLE £5–15 | CONCERN £15–30 | FAIL >£30
  Google UAC:               STRONG <£10 | ACCEPTABLE £10–25 | CONCERN £25–50 | FAIL >£50
  Apple Search Ads (ASA):   STRONG <£6 | ACCEPTABLE £6–18 | CONCERN £18–35 | FAIL >£35
  Organic (ASO / SEO):      STRONG <£2 | ACCEPTABLE £2–5 | CONCERN >£5
  Note: CAC benchmarks above are for non-subscription consumer apps. For physical DTC
  or financial products, CAC is materially higher (see DTC benchmarks below).

CAC trend (blended CAC change over last 6 months):
  STRONG: declining or stable | CONCERN: rising >20% over 6 months
  FAIL: rising >50% over 6 months with no structural explanation

LTV / CAC ratio:
  STRONG >4x | ACCEPTABLE 2.5–4x | CONCERN 1.5–2.5x | FAIL <1.5x
  Note: LTV for consumer subscriptions = ARPU ÷ Monthly Churn Rate.
  A £5/month subscription with 5% monthly churn has LTV = £100.
  At £15 CAC that is 6.7x LTV/CAC — excellent. At £40 CAC that is 2.5x — marginal.

MONETISATION BENCHMARKS — SUBSCRIPTION AND FREEMIUM:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Free-to-paid conversion rate (% of registered free users who convert to paid):
  STRONG >8% | ACCEPTABLE 3–8% | CONCERN 1–3% | FAIL <1%
  Context: Spotify (historical): ~26% conversion (premium brand + extensive free tier).
  Consumer apps general: 3–5% is typical. Below 2% suggests the value proposition
  for paying is not compelling enough relative to the free tier.

Monthly subscription churn (% of paying subscribers who cancel in a given month):
  STRONG <3% | ACCEPTABLE 3–6% | CONCERN 6–10% | FAIL >10%
  Note: 5% monthly churn = 46% annual churn = only 54% of subscribers renew each year.
  That means the company must replace nearly half its subscriber base each year just to
  stay flat. The first alarm is monthly churn above 6%.

Annual subscription renewal rate (% of annual subscribers who renew):
  STRONG >80% | ACCEPTABLE 65–80% | CONCERN 50–65% | FAIL <50%

ARPU benchmarks (average revenue per monthly active user):
  Consumer app subscription:    STRONG >£8/month | ACCEPTABLE £4–8 | CONCERN <£4
  Consumer fintech (active):    STRONG >£8/month | ACCEPTABLE £3–8 | CONCERN <£3
  Health / wellness:            STRONG >£12/month | ACCEPTABLE £6–12 | CONCERN <£6
  Gaming (all users, blended):  STRONG >£2/month | ACCEPTABLE £0.50–2 | CONCERN <£0.50

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Gross margin after COGS (before marketing spend):
  STRONG >60% | ACCEPTABLE 45–60% | CONCERN 30–45% | FAIL <30%
  Note: this is pre-returns. The post-returns gross margin is always lower.

Return rate by category:
  Fashion / apparel:       STRONG <15% | ACCEPTABLE 15–25% | CONCERN 25–35% | FAIL >35%
  Beauty / wellness:       STRONG <5% | ACCEPTABLE 5–10% | CONCERN >12%

  Homeware / gifts:        STRONG <8% | ACCEPTABLE 8–15% | CONCERN >20%

  STRONG >50% | ACCEPTABLE 35–50% | CONCERN 20–35% | FAIL <20%
  Note: any DTC physical company presenting gross margin without accounting for returns

Contribution margin (gross margin minus paid acquisition and fulfilment cost):
  STRONG >25% | ACCEPTABLE 10–25% | CONCERN 0–10% | FAIL <0%
  Note: a DTC brand with 50% gross margin but 40% marketing spend has a 10% contribution
  margin — barely enough to cover fixed costs. Contribution margin below 0% means
  the company loses money on every order at current acquisition spend.

CAC for physical DTC (cost to acquire a first-time buyer):
  Apparel / fashion:       STRONG <£25 | ACCEPTABLE £25–55 | CONCERN £55–90 | FAIL >£90
  Beauty / personal care:  STRONG <£20 | ACCEPTABLE £20–45 | CONCERN £45–70 | FAIL >£70
  Food / FMCG:             STRONG <£15 | ACCEPTABLE £15–35 | CONCERN £35–60 | FAIL >£60
  Health / supplements:    STRONG <£25 | ACCEPTABLE £25–55 | CONCERN £55–80 | FAIL >£80

Repeat purchase rate (% of first-time buyers who purchase again within 90 days):
  STRONG >45% | ACCEPTABLE 25–45% | CONCERN 10–25% | FAIL <10%
  Note: DTC physical economics require repeat purchase to be profitable. At 25% gross
  margin, the first order likely does not recover CAC. Repeat purchase 2 and 3 are
  where profit is made. A DTC brand with 10% repeat purchase rate in 90 days will never
  generate LTV sufficient to justify paid acquisition above £20 CAC.

Average order value (AOV) and orders per customer per year:
  STRONG: AOV × orders/year generates LTV >4x CAC within 24 months
  FAIL: AOV × orders/year generates LTV <2x CAC even at maximum purchase frequency

CAC payback period (for physical DTC):
  STRONG <2 orders | ACCEPTABLE 2–4 orders | CONCERN 4–7 orders | FAIL >7 orders (or never)

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. DAU/MAU BELOW 15%
SIGNAL: Daily active users as a percentage of monthly active users is below 15%.
The company may present MAU as its primary engagement metric without disclosing DAU.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A consumer product with DAU/MAU below 15% is used by fewer than 1 in
6 of its "monthly active" users on any given day. This is not a product that has
become a daily habit. For most consumer businesses — app, subscription, health, finance
— daily habitual use is the precondition for high LTV. A user who opens an app twice
a month is at high risk of cancellation. Low DAU/MAU is a leading indicator of high
churn, not current churn — the retention collapse often follows 90–120 days later.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What is your DAU/MAU ratio? Can you show the DAU/MAU trend over
the last 6 months? Is DAU/MAU stable, improving, or declining?"

02. D30 RETENTION BELOW 10%
SIGNAL: Fewer than 10% of users who registered or downloaded the app are still active
30 days later. Company may report strong download or sign-up numbers without disclosing
the retention curve.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: D30 retention below 10% means 90%+ of users abandon the product within
the first month. This is not a retention problem — it is a fundamental product-market
fit failure. The product does not provide enough recurring value to justify returning.
No amount of acquisition scale can compensate for this: adding more users to a leaking
bucket does not make the bucket full.
VERIFICATION: Request the Day-7, Day-14, and Day-30 retention rates from first cohorts
and most recent cohorts. If the company cannot produce retention curves, they are not
managing the business to consumer product standards.

03. 90%+ OF GROWTH FROM PAID ACQUISITION
SIGNAL: The overwhelming majority of new user acquisition in the last 90 days is from
paid channels — Meta, TikTok, Google, Apple Search Ads. Organic acquisition (ASO,
word-of-mouth, press, referral) represents less than 10% of new users.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A consumer business with 90%+ paid acquisition is not a product business
with a marketing channel — it is a paid media arbitrage business. It continues to grow
as long as the economics of paid acquisition are positive. When Meta changes its
algorithm, when iOS changes its privacy rules (as happened with ATT in 2021), or when
CAC rises as the targetable audience saturates, the business stops. There is no
underlying product moat — no word-of-mouth, no organic retention loop, no viral mechanic.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your new users in the last 90 days came from
organic sources — ASO, direct, word-of-mouth, or referral? How has this percentage
changed over the last 12 months?"

04. RISING CAC WITH FLAT OR DECLINING RETENTION
SIGNAL: Customer acquisition cost has increased by more than 25% in the last 6 months
while D30 retention is flat or declining. Revenue growth continues because acquisition
volume is being maintained — but at higher cost and with the same or worse LTV.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: This is the Paid Acquisition Trap in early formation. The company is
spending more to acquire users of the same or lower quality. LTV/CAC is deteriorating.
The company is moving backwards on the most important unit economics ratio while
appearing to grow. If not corrected, the unit economics will become negative before
the team notices — because the delay between acquisition and LTV realisation means the
damage from today's cohort does not appear in the P&L for 3–6 months.
VERIFICATION: Calculate LTV/CAC for each of the last 4 quarterly cohorts. If the ratio
is declining, ask: "What is causing the CAC increase? Is it channel saturation, bid
competition, or audience exhaustion? What is your plan to restore LTV/CAC above 3x?"

05. APP STORE RATING BELOW 4.0
SIGNAL: App Store or Google Play rating is below 4.0. Company does not disclose the
rating in the submission. Recent review trends (last 90 days) show ratings declining.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: App store rating is the most visible consumer trust signal for any
mobile-first product. A rating below 4.0 reduces organic conversion rate from app store
discovery by 30–50% (users are less likely to download an app rated below 4.0). More
importantly, a low or declining rating signals that current users are having a poor
enough experience to leave a negative review — which is a high-friction action. Users
who leave a 1-star review are not marginal dissatisfied users; they are actively harmful
to acquisition.
VERIFICATION: Check the app store listing directly during diligence. Ask: "What is
your current App Store and Google Play rating? What are the most common complaints
in recent reviews? What have you done in the last 90 days to address them?"

06. SINGLE ACQUISITION CHANNEL DEPENDENCY
SIGNAL: More than 60% of paid user acquisition is through a single channel — typically
Meta (Facebook/Instagram), TikTok, or Google. No meaningful acquisition from a second
paid channel. Organic acquisition below 20%.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Single-channel dependency is the consumer equivalent of banking partner
concentration in Fintech. A Meta algorithm change, a privacy regulation shift (Apple
ATT reduced Meta's targeting effectiveness by 30–40% in 2021), or a platform policy
update can cut the primary acquisition channel immediately. A company that rebuilt its
entire CAC model on Meta post-ATT is now building on TikTok — which faces its own
regulatory risk in the UK and US. A consumer company with no channel diversification
has existential acquisition fragility.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your new users came from each acquisition channel
in the last 90 days? What is your acquisition plan if your primary paid channel's
effectiveness declined by 50% tomorrow?"

07. SEASONAL DEMAND MASKING STRUCTURAL RETENTION PROBLEM
SIGNAL: Company shows strong MoM growth in Q1 (January health resolutions, Valentine's
gifting, New Year productivity) or Q4 (Christmas gifting, Black Friday DTC) but does
not disclose what happens to those users or customers in the months following the
seasonal peak.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Consumer products with a strong seasonal demand spike can present 3-month
growth metrics that look compelling while hiding a structural churn problem. A health
app that acquires 100K users in January and retains 8K by April has a 92% churn rate
dressed as Q1 growth. The seasonal acquisition cohort is the weakest cohort — driven
by a resolution mindset, not genuine habitual need.
VERIFICATION: Request cohort retention for the January and Q4 acquisition cohorts
specifically. Ask: "What percentage of users acquired in January are still active in
April? How does that compare to the retention rate of users acquired in October?"

08. DTC PHYSICAL: GROSS MARGIN BELOW 40% AFTER RETURNS
SIGNAL: DTC physical company presents gross margin of 50–60% but the figure is
calculated before returns, return shipping, restocking, and damaged goods write-offs.
Post-returns gross margin is below 40%.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A DTC physical business with post-returns gross margin below 40% has
insufficient margin to fund paid acquisition, fixed overhead, and generate profit
simultaneously. At 35% post-returns gross margin with a CAC of £30 and average order
value of £50, the unit economics are: £50 × 35% = £17.50 gross profit on order 1,
minus £30 CAC = negative £12.50 contribution on first order. If the average customer
only orders twice in their lifetime, the business is permanently loss-making on a
unit economics basis.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What is your gross margin after accounting for returns, return
shipping, and damaged goods write-offs? What is your return rate by product category?
Have you included the cost of processing returns in your COGS?"

09. FREE-TO-PAID CONVERSION BELOW 2% FOR FREEMIUM
SIGNAL: Consumer product uses a freemium model and the conversion rate from registered
free users to any paid tier is below 2%. Company reports total registered users and
paying users separately, but the ratio reveals a sub-2% conversion rate.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: A freemium conversion rate below 2% means the product's paid value
proposition is not compelling relative to the free tier. Either the free tier is too
generous (giving away the core value for free), or the paid features are not worth
the price, or the user base fundamentally does not perceive enough value to pay.
A consumer business with 2 million registered users and 30,000 paying subscribers
has a 1.5% conversion rate — this looks impressive until you recognise that 98.5% of
the company's acquired users are not generating revenue.
VERIFICATION: Calculate the conversion rate from the submission data if it is not
explicitly stated. Ask: "What is your free-to-paid conversion rate? What are the most
common reasons free users give for not upgrading? What specific features or events
trigger a paid conversion?"

10. NO VIRAL OR REFERRAL MECHANISM
SIGNAL: Product has no feature, incentive, or mechanics that encourage existing users
to invite new users. Sharing requires the user to leave the product and use a third-party
communication channel. No referral programme exists. No social sharing component is
embedded in the core product experience.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Virality and referral are the primary long-term moats for consumer
products. A consumer product with zero viral mechanic is entirely dependent on paid
acquisition and organic discovery to grow. Without a referral or sharing mechanism,
the product cannot compound its user base — every new user must be individually acquired
from outside the existing user community.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Does your product have any feature that encourages or enables users
to invite or share with other users? What is your referral rate — what percentage of
new users are referred by existing users? What would it take to build a viral loop
into the core product experience?"

11. CONSUMER DATA PRIVACY COMPLIANCE GAP — UNDER-18 USERS
SIGNAL: Product has a material number of users under 18 — or its target market includes
under-18s — but the company has not explicitly designed for age-appropriate experience
under the UK Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code), nor has it implemented
age verification, parental consent, or content restrictions required for underage users.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The UK Age Appropriate Design Code (in force since September 2021)
requires any product likely to be accessed by under-18s to implement privacy-by-default
settings, restrict profiling, limit data collection, and prevent features that could harm
children's wellbeing. A consumer app in gaming, social, health, or education that has
not implemented the Code is exposed to ICO enforcement action. The ICO has levied
significant fines (TikTok: £12.7M, 2023) specifically for failures to protect children's
data. Additionally, US COPPA (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act) applies to
products accessible to US users under 13.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your users are under 18? Have you conducted
a Children's Code audit? What specific design choices have you made to comply with
the Code?"

12. INFLUENCER DEPENDENCY FOR CUSTOMER ACQUISITION
SIGNAL: More than 30% of new customer acquisition in the last 90 days traces back to
2–3 key influencer relationships. The company's marketing budget is heavily allocated
to influencer fee payments. No evidence of acquisition scaling beyond the influencer
channel without degrading economics.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Influencer marketing is not a scalable acquisition channel — it is a
series of individual high-risk relationships. An influencer who changes their positioning,
joins a competitor's campaign, suffers a reputational event, or simply stops posting
about the product removes a material portion of customer acquisition overnight. A DTC
or consumer app brand with influencer-concentrated acquisition has not built a durable
marketing channel — it has rented one.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What percentage of your customer acquisition over the last 90 days
is attributable to influencer partnerships? If your top 3 influencer relationships ended
tomorrow, what would happen to your acquisition volume and CAC?"

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE RETENTION CLIFF
DESCRIPTION: A consumer app launches with strong PR coverage, App Store featuring, or
a successful paid acquisition campaign. Downloads are impressive — 200K in the first
60 days. Seed investors are excited by the growth numbers. 90 days later, investigation
reveals: D30 retention is 7%. Of the 200K who downloaded, 186,000 have not opened the
app in the past 30 days. Daily active users are 8,000 — the product's entire active
user base fits in a large theatre. The 8,000 active users like the product. But there
is no evidence that the 192,000 inactive users would ever return. The product has not
formed a habit. Each new paid acquisition campaign generates a spike of downloads
followed by the same 7% survival rate. The product is running water into a broken bucket.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Submissions reports total downloads or registered users
rather than monthly or daily active users; D30 retention not disclosed; DAU/MAU ratio
below 15%; the product's core value is delivered in a single session without a reason
to return; the acquisition strategy has never changed because changing it would expose
that the problem is retention, not acquisition.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any consumer app where D30 retention is not disclosed or is below 10%,
and where growth metrics are presented in terms of total registered users or total
downloads rather than active users.

2. THE PAID ACQUISITION TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A consumer company builds a growth model based entirely on paid acquisition.
At £10 CAC and a £25 LTV the economics work at early scale. The company raises a
Series A to scale acquisition. As spend increases, the most efficient audience segments
are exhausted first — the lowest-hanging fruit disappears. CAC rises to £18, then £25.
LTV is unchanged because the product has not improved. At £25 CAC and £25 LTV,
contribution margin is zero. The company is still growing (more spend = more users)
but each new user is worth exactly what they cost. At £30 CAC and £25 LTV, the company
is destroying value with every acquisition. The Series A capital is being burned to
acquire users who will never collectively generate a return on the acquisition spend.
Revenue growth looks healthy in the pitch deck. The unit economics spreadsheet
tells a completely different story.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: CAC has increased more than 40% in the last 12 months; organic
acquisition represents less than 20% of new users; the company's growth plan for the
next 12 months is to increase paid acquisition budget proportionally with revenue; there
is no product-led growth strategy; the founding team describes competition for paid
audiences as "manageable" without acknowledging that every competitor in the category
is also bidding on the same audiences.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any consumer company where organic acquisition is below 20% AND CAC has
been rising over the last 3 quarters AND there is no product change planned to improve
the organic acquisition rate.

3. THE NOVELTY EFFECT
DESCRIPTION: A consumer app launches with a genuinely new feature — a creative AI tool,
a social mechanic, a novel health tracking capability. Early engagement metrics are
spectacular: D7 retention of 45%, session length 12 minutes, DAU/MAU above 60%. Investors
see the metrics and immediately recognise them as best-in-class. They invest at a
premium valuation based on engagement metrics that appear category-defining. 6 months
later, the D7 retention for new cohorts is 18%. Session length has dropped to 4 minutes.
The original cohort's engagement has declined from 60% DAU/MAU to 20%. The novelty wore
off. The core product mechanic was interesting to experience once or twice but provided
no reason to return habitually. The investors funded what appeared to be a habit-forming
product but was actually a novelty that generated impressive engagement for 60–90 days.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: D7 and D30 retention for the earliest cohorts (acquired in the
first 3 months) is significantly higher than D7 and D30 retention for more recent
cohorts; the product's core mechanic is novel rather than useful (entertainment value
rather than practical utility); no specific habit loop exists in the product (there is
no reason to open it at a predictable time or in response to a predictable trigger);
the founding team uses engagement metrics from the first 30 days of operation to
represent long-term retention.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any consumer app that launched within the last 18 months and is presenting
retention metrics without cohort-level data showing whether the earliest cohorts are
still engaged at the same rate as the most recently acquired cohorts.

4. THE PHYSICAL DTC MARGIN SQUEEZE
DESCRIPTION: A DTC physical brand achieves impressive top-line revenue growth —
£3M in Year 1, £8M in Year 2. The gross margin appears healthy at 52%. But when
returns (28% return rate in fashion), return shipping (£3 per return), and inventory
write-offs (8% of production never sold at full price) are accounted for, the actual
gross margin is 33%. With a £40 CAC and an average order value of £60, the first
order generates £60 × 33% = £19.80 gross profit minus £40 CAC = negative £20.20
contribution margin on first order. The company depends entirely on repeat orders
to reach profitability. But 65% of customers never purchase a second time. The company
is permanently loss-making at the unit level. Revenue grows because new customer
acquisition is increasing, but the unit economics are worse with every pound spent
on acquisition. The investors who backed the brand on the strength of 52% gross margin
have a business that is structurally loss-making at every scale.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Gross margin disclosed without returns adjustment; return rate
not disclosed; repeat purchase rate below 30% in 90 days; CAC is high relative to AOV
(CAC above 60% of AOV means the first order cannot recover acquisition cost at any
reasonable gross margin); DTC brand is in a high-return-rate category (fashion,
apparel) without a specific return reduction strategy; contribution margin per order
not tracked or not disclosed.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any DTC physical company where the gross margin figure in the submission
does not explicitly state that returns, return shipping, and write-offs are included.
Always ask for the post-returns gross margin — it is always lower than the headline figure.

5. THE PLATFORM ALGORITHM CHANGE
DESCRIPTION: A consumer company builds its acquisition model on organic distribution
through a single platform's algorithm — TikTok organic, Instagram Reels, or Google
SEO. The content flywheel is working: the company's posts or pages generate thousands
of organic impressions per day, driving thousands of free user acquisitions. The team
and investors credit the company's traction to "strong organic growth" and allocate
minimal budget to diversifying acquisition. The platform changes its algorithm. TikTok
deprioritises certain content formats. Instagram reduces Reels reach for commercial
accounts. Google changes its SEO ranking for health content (Medic Update pattern).
Organic acquisition drops 70% in 30 days. The company has no paid acquisition capability
to replace it (no tested creative, no audience data, no attribution model). It takes 3–6
months to rebuild. In that time, paid CAC is 3x its theoretical efficient rate because
the team is learning while spending. The company's growth story ends without a single
competitive threat.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: "Organic growth" is attributed primarily to a specific platform's
algorithm rather than word-of-mouth or referral (organic via an algorithm is not the
same as genuine virality — it can disappear overnight); the team cannot articulate what
specifically drives the platform's algorithm in their favour; no diversified acquisition
strategy has been tested; the founding team has never spent on paid acquisition and
cannot describe their paid unit economics.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any consumer company where "organic" acquisition is primarily algorithmic
distribution from a single platform rather than genuine word-of-mouth referral.

6. THE INFLUENCER DEPENDENCY
DESCRIPTION: A DTC brand or consumer app launches with a compelling influencer
marketing strategy — 3 key creators with a combined audience of 5M followers produce
authentic content about the product. Sales spike with each post. The brand reaches
£2M ARR on the strength of influencer-driven awareness. Investors see the traction and
invest in scaling influencer partnerships. The company signs 20 more influencers.
The incremental influencers have smaller, less engaged audiences — the returns from
influencer 4–20 are a fraction of influencer 1–3. Meanwhile, one of the original three
influencers switches to a competitor in return for a brand deal (equity stake + fee).
The second exits to start their own competing brand. The third suffers a reputational
event and their content becomes toxic to associate with. In 12 months, the original
three-creator acquisition engine is gone. The 20 replacement influencers generate 30%
of the acquisition volume at 200% of the cost. The brand is spending more on influencer
fees than it is generating from influencer-attributable sales.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Top 3 influencers account for more than 40% of influencer-
attributable revenue; influencers are not contracted with exclusivity clauses; no
owned media or CRM strategy exists to re-engage influencer-acquired customers without
the influencer; the founding team describes influencer relationships as "partnerships"
with personal components that are not governed by commercial contracts; no
performance-based component in influencer compensation (flat fee rather than % of sales).
WHEN TO APPLY: Any DTC brand or consumer app where influencer marketing accounts for
more than 30% of customer acquisition and fewer than 5 influencers account for more
than 50% of that acquisition.

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. D30 AND D90 RETENTION BY ACQUISITION COHORT
QUESTION: "Can you show me the Day-30 and Day-90 retention curves for each quarterly
acquisition cohort over the last 12 months? Are the retention rates for more recently
acquired cohorts higher or lower than earlier cohorts? And for cohorts that are 12+
months old, what percentage of those users are still active today?"
STRONG ANSWER: Produces cohort retention curves; recent cohorts retain at the same
rate or better than early cohorts (product improving); oldest cohorts show that a
meaningful percentage of users have been active for 12+ months (long-tail engagement).
WEAK ANSWER: Reports a blended average D30 retention without cohort breakdown;
cannot confirm whether retention is improving or declining across cohorts; defines
"active" broadly (e.g., opened app once in 30 days rather than completed a meaningful
session).
REVEALS: Whether the product is genuinely forming habits across a growing user base
or whether impressive headline metrics are masking cohort-level retention deterioration.

02. ORGANIC VS PAID ACQUISITION SPLIT (LAST 90 DAYS)
QUESTION: "Of all new users acquired in the last 90 days, what percentage came from
each channel: organic App Store discovery, word-of-mouth referral, press/content, paid
social (Meta, TikTok, Google, ASA), and influencer partnerships? How has the organic
percentage changed over the last 12 months?"
STRONG ANSWER: At least 40% organic over the last 90 days; the organic share has been
stable or growing as the company scales; can describe the specific drivers of organic
acquisition (what content, features, or user behaviours generate referral).
WEAK ANSWER: "Most of our growth is organic" without a specific percentage; cannot
separate word-of-mouth from algorithmic organic; organic percentage has been declining
as the company has increased paid acquisition spend.
REVEALS: Whether the company has a durable acquisition moat or is dependent on paid
media economics that will deteriorate as the market saturates.

03. VIRAL COEFFICIENT (K-FACTOR)
QUESTION: "What is your viral coefficient — for every 100 active users, how many new
users do they bring to the product? What specific mechanics drive that referral —
do you have a referral programme, a social sharing feature, or organic word-of-mouth?
How has the K-factor changed over the last 6 months?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific K-factor above 0.2 with a named mechanic driving it; K-factor
has been stable or improving; can describe the specific user behaviour that triggers
a referral (e.g., "when a user completes a milestone they share it, and 8% of recipients
click through and register").
WEAK ANSWER: "We have strong word-of-mouth" without a specific K-factor; referral is
described anecdotally; no specific product mechanic drives sharing; "people just love
it and tell their friends."
REVEALS: Whether organic growth is a managed, measurable product property or a vague
description of goodwill that cannot be scaled.

04. CAC TREND OVER LAST 6 MONTHS (BY CHANNEL)
QUESTION: "What is your blended CAC by channel for each of the last 6 months? Which
channels have had the largest CAC increase? What is the cause — audience saturation,
increased competition, or creative fatigue? And what is your plan to address rising
CAC in each affected channel?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific CAC by channel for 6 months; identifies channels with rising
CAC and the specific cause; has a plan (new creative angles, new audience segments,
new channels) being actively tested; overall blended CAC is stable or declining as
organic share grows.
WEAK ANSWER: "CAC has been pretty stable"; provides a single blended figure; cannot
break down by channel; "we haven't seen significant CAC inflation."
REVEALS: Whether the company understands its acquisition economics at the channel level
and is managing them proactively, or whether it is unaware of CAC trends that are
quietly destroying unit economics.

05. LTV BY ACQUISITION CHANNEL
QUESTION: "Do users acquired from different channels have different LTV — do Meta users
retain better or worse than TikTok users or organic users? What is the LTV breakdown
by channel? And how does that affect how you allocate acquisition budget?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has calculated LTV by acquisition channel; organic users have higher LTV
than paid users (typical); reallocates budget towards highest LTV/CAC channels; monitors
LTV by channel quarterly.
WEAK ANSWER: "We haven't segmented LTV by channel yet"; assumes LTV is the same across
channels; allocates budget based on raw CAC without factoring in LTV differences.
REVEALS: Whether the company is optimising its acquisition spend on a unit-economics
basis or is optimising for top-of-funnel volume without understanding the downstream value
of each channel's users.

06. APP STORE RATING AND REVIEW TREND
QUESTION: "What is your current App Store and Google Play rating? How many reviews do
you have? What is the trend — rising, declining, or stable — over the last 90 days?
What are the most common negative themes in recent 1-star and 2-star reviews, and
what have you done to address them in the product?"
STRONG ANSWER: Rating 4.5+; trend is stable or rising; can articulate the most common
negative themes and describe specific product changes made in response; has an active
review response strategy.
WEAK ANSWER: Rating 3.8–4.0 and declining; cannot identify common complaint themes;
"most users love the product, the negative reviews are outliers"; no active review
management strategy.
REVEALS: Whether the company is managing its most visible quality signal or is ignoring
the feedback of its most vocal dissatisfied users — who also happen to be the users most
likely to influence organic acquisition decisions for prospective users.

07. SUBSCRIPTION CHURN BY COHORT YEAR
QUESTION: "For paying subscribers who started in each of the last three years, what
is the 6-month, 12-month, and 24-month retention rate? Are your most recently acquired
subscribers churning faster or slower than your earliest subscribers?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific retention rates for each cohort year; 12-month retention above
65% for all cohorts; recent cohorts retaining at the same rate or better (product
improving); describes the specific moments in the subscriber journey where churn is
highest and what has been done to address those moments.
WEAK ANSWER: Provides a single monthly churn rate; "subscriber retention is strong";
cannot produce cohort-level data; defines churn as monthly cancellations without cohort-
level analysis of when in the subscription lifecycle churn happens.
REVEALS: Whether subscription retention is improving as the product matures or whether
newer subscribers are churning faster — which is a sign of declining product quality
or audience saturation in the highest-value user segments.

08. REVENUE PER DAILY ACTIVE USER TREND
QUESTION: "What is your average revenue per daily active user (Revenue ÷ DAU) for each
of the last four quarters? Is this metric rising, falling, or stable? What specific
product changes have increased or decreased revenue per DAU?"
STRONG ANSWER: Revenue per DAU is stable or rising; specific product changes (new
subscription tier, higher ARPU plan, additional IAP options) have improved the metric;
can explain whether improvement is volume-driven (more DAU) or yield-driven (more
revenue per DAU).
WEAK ANSWER: Does not track revenue per DAU; "ARPU has been improving" without DAU
context; revenue growth attributed to user growth without analysis of whether each
user is generating more value over time.
REVEALS: Whether the company is building deeper monetisation of its existing engaged
user base or simply acquiring new users to maintain revenue growth.

09. PLATFORM DEPENDENCY RISK
QUESTION: "If Meta's advertising effectiveness declined by 50% — as happened with
Apple's ATT changes in 2021 — what would happen to your acquisition volume and CAC?
What is your 6-month plan for that scenario? And for organic acquisition: if your
primary organic channel's algorithm changed unfavourably, what percentage of organic
acquisition would be affected?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has modelled a Meta ATT-equivalent scenario; has active test campaigns
on at least 2 alternative paid channels; organic acquisition is diversified across
ASO, content SEO, and genuine word-of-mouth — not solely one platform's algorithm.
WEAK ANSWER: "We have a good relationship with Meta"; "we haven't really modelled that
scenario"; describes organic acquisition as "strong" without acknowledging that
algorithm-dependent organic can disappear overnight.
REVEALS: Whether the company has built a resilient acquisition model or has a single
point of failure in its growth engine.

10. PHYSICAL DTC: RETURN RATE AND GROSS MARGIN AFTER RETURNS
QUESTION: "What is your return rate by product category, and what is your gross margin
after accounting for returns, return shipping, and write-offs on damaged returned goods?
How does your post-returns gross margin compare to your headline gross margin? And what
specific actions are you taking to reduce returns?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific return rate by category; post-returns gross margin explicitly
calculated and disclosed; has a returns reduction programme (better size guidance,
improved product descriptions, virtual try-on, personalisation) with measurable impact.
WEAK ANSWER: Presents gross margin without confirming returns are included; "our return
rate is below industry average" without a specific figure; "we include returns in COGS"
without confirming the specific adjustment made.
REVEALS: Whether the company's unit economics are viable at scale or whether the
headline gross margin is concealing a return rate that makes the business structurally
loss-making on contribution margin.

11. USER AGE DISTRIBUTION AND COPPA / CHILDREN'S CODE COMPLIANCE
QUESTION: "What percentage of your users are under 18? Under 13? Have you conducted
an audit of your product against the ICO's Age Appropriate Design Code? What specific
design choices have you made for younger users — privacy by default, no profiling,
content restrictions? If you have US users under 13, how do you comply with COPPA?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has data on user age distribution; has conducted a Children's Code
audit (or age verification makes under-18 users genuinely rare); can describe specific
design decisions made for compliance; COPPA compliance programme in place if US users
under 13 exist.
WEAK ANSWER: "We don't specifically target under-18s"; "users have to agree to our
Terms of Service saying they're 18+"; cannot confirm whether a Children's Code audit
has been conducted; does not know the age distribution of its user base.
REVEALS: Whether the company has addressed one of the highest-risk regulatory areas
for consumer products — the ICO has publicly stated that the Children's Code is an
enforcement priority and has already levied significant fines.

12. TOP 3 FEATURES DRIVING HIGHEST RETENTION
QUESTION: "Which 3 product features or experiences are most strongly correlated with
users who stay active for 90+ days? How do you know — what data supports this? And
what is your product roadmap to make those features more central to the core experience
for new users?"
STRONG ANSWER: Identifies 3 specific features with cohort-level data showing that users
who engage with them in their first 7 days retain at 2x or higher the rate of users
who don't; product roadmap actively focuses on getting new users to these features faster
(reducing time-to-value).
WEAK ANSWER: "Users who engage deeply retain better"; describes retention-driving features
anecdotally without data; "we're working on improving the onboarding experience" without
a specific feature-level hypothesis.
REVEALS: Whether the team understands what makes their product sticky at a feature level —
which is the foundation of any intelligent retention improvement strategy.

UK GDPR AND CONSUMER DATA:

UK GDPR (retained after Brexit, supplemented by Data Protection Act 2018) applies to
all consumer products processing personal data of UK users. Key requirements for
consumer companies:
— Lawful basis: consumer products typically rely on consent or legitimate interests for
  non-essential data processing. Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and
  unambiguous. Pre-ticked boxes and bundled consent are not valid.
— Data minimisation: only collect data necessary for the stated purpose. Consumer apps
  that collect location, contacts, or behavioural data beyond what the core product
  requires are exposed to ICO enforcement.
— Privacy by design: privacy protections must be built into the product from the
  start, not bolted on after complaints.
— Right to erasure: consumers can request deletion of their personal data. Consumer
  apps must have a clear, accessible deletion process.
— Personalisation and profiling: using personal data to profile users for advertising
  requires explicit consent. Legitimate interests is rarely a valid basis for advertising
  profiling.
Alex flag: consumer apps that monetise via third-party advertising without explicit
  consent for profiling are operating in violation of UK GDPR. The ICO has signalled
  that adtech enforcement is a priority. Any consumer company with advertising-based
  monetisation must have a consent management platform (CMP) implemented correctly.

AGE APPROPRIATE DESIGN CODE (UK CHILDREN'S CODE):

The ICO's Age Appropriate Design Code (in force from September 2021) applies to
all online services likely to be accessed by children under 18 in the UK.
15 standards including:
— Best interests of the child: design decisions must prioritise children's wellbeing.
— Privacy settings: highest privacy settings by default for users likely to be children.
— No profiling: do not profile children based on their personal data unless strictly necessary.
— No nudge techniques: no design features that encourage children to provide more data
  or stay on the platform longer than is in their best interests.
— Geolocation: do not track children's precise location by default.
— Connected toys and devices: devices that could be used by children must comply.
Who must comply: any UK service "likely to be accessed by children" — which includes any
  service without robust age verification. The burden of proof is on the service provider
  to demonstrate it is not likely to be used by children, not on the ICO to prove it is.
ICO enforcement: TikTok (£12.7M, 2023), YouTube (US FTC settlement £170M). ICO has
  stated the Code is a priority enforcement area.
Alex flag: any consumer app in gaming, social, health, education, or entertainment that
  has not conducted a Children's Code audit is at material risk of ICO enforcement action.
  "Our terms say users must be 18+" is not sufficient compliance.

COPPA (US — CHILDREN'S ONLINE PRIVACY PROTECTION ACT):

Applies to any online service directed to US children under 13, or any general audience
service with actual knowledge of users under 13.
Key requirements: verifiable parental consent before collecting personal data from
under-13 users; no targeted advertising to under-13 users; data deletion on parental
request.
FTC enforcement: substantial civil penalties (up to $51,744 per violation per day).
Alex flag: any UK consumer company with US users — particularly apps in gaming, education,
  health, or social — that does not have a COPPA compliance programme is operating with
  significant US legal exposure. The FTC has pursued enforcement actions against UK
  companies that operate in the US market.

ONLINE SAFETY ACT 2023 (UK):

The Online Safety Act creates new duties for user-to-user services and search services.
For consumer platforms:
— Category 1 (Very Large Platforms, designated by Ofcom): additional duties including
  safety by design, transparency reports, risk assessments.
— All in-scope services: illegal content duties (must remove illegal content rapidly);
  children's safety duties (services likely to be accessed by children must comply with
  additional safety requirements); transparency about terms of service enforcement.
Timeline: Ofcom has published codes of practice; compliance expected from 2025–2026.
Alex flag: any consumer platform with user-generated content or user-to-user communication
  must complete an Online Safety Act risk assessment and implement proportionate systems
  for illegal content identification. Non-compliance: fines up to 10% of global annual
  turnover, or £18M, whichever is higher.

CONSUMER PROTECTION — CMA ENFORCEMENT:

Digital Markets, Competition and Consumer Act 2024 (in force 2025):
  The DMCCA 2024 significantly strengthens the CMA's enforcement powers for consumer
  protection. Key provisions relevant to consumer companies:
— Fake and misleading reviews: platforms must have systems to detect and remove fake
  reviews. Paying for positive reviews or suppressing negative reviews is prohibited.
  The CMA can now act directly (without a court order) and impose fines up to 10% of
  global annual turnover.
— Drip pricing: showing a headline price with mandatory additional charges revealed
  later in the checkout process is prohibited. All mandatory charges must be included
  in the headline price.
— Subscription traps: automatic renewal without clear consumer consent; making it
  harder to cancel than to subscribe; failing to remind consumers before a free trial
  converts to paid. The CMA has already taken action against Amazon Prime, Apple, and
  streaming services.
— Pressure-selling: countdown timers, artificial scarcity claims, and "other people are
  viewing this" claims that are false or misleading.
Alex flag: any consumer company using countdown timers, fake scarcity indicators, or
  misleading pricing in its subscription or checkout flow is exposed to direct CMA
  enforcement under the DMCCA 2024.

ADVERTISING REGULATION — ASA AND INFLUENCER MARKETING:

ASA (Advertising Standards Authority) and CAP (Committee of Advertising Practice):
  All advertising to UK consumers must comply with CAP codes. Key rules for consumer
  companies:
— Influencer disclosure: any paid partnership, gifted product, or affiliate arrangement
  with an influencer must be clearly disclosed with #ad, #sponsored, or equivalent.
  The ASA and CMA have pursued enforcement action against influencers and brands for
  non-disclosed paid promotions.
— Health claims: any health or wellness claim in advertising must be substantiated.
  Claims that a product treats, prevents, or cures a condition require evidence; general
  wellness claims must not mislead.
— Targeting restrictions: ads for gambling, alcohol, HFSS food (high fat, sugar, salt)
  and similar categories must not be targeted to under-18s.
Alex flag: any consumer company using influencer marketing without a clear disclosure
  compliance policy is exposed to ASA action. The ASA has dramatically increased
  enforcement of influencer disclosure rules since 2022 — "the rules apply to micro-
  influencers too" is explicit ASA guidance.

SUBSCRIPTION CONTRACT REGULATION (UK — 2024–2026):

The UK Government has introduced new regulations on subscription contracts (under the
DMCCA 2024 and preceding consultation) including:
— Pre-contract information: consumers must be clearly informed before entering a
  subscription of: the price, the length of the minimum commitment, and how to cancel.
— Cooling-off periods: consumers have the right to cancel within 14 days of starting
  a subscription and receive a full refund.
— Renewal reminders: companies must remind consumers before each annual renewal that
  renewal is approaching and that they can cancel.
— Cancellation: must be as easy to cancel as to subscribe — no "cancel by phone only"
  or buried cancellation options.
Alex flag: any consumer subscription company that has not audited its subscription
  onboarding, renewal reminder, and cancellation flows against the new subscription
  contract regulations is exposed to CMA action. This applies to all digital subscription
  products, not just media and entertainment.

APP STORE REGULATION — DIGITAL MARKETS ACT (EU DMA):

The EU Digital Markets Act (in force from March 2024) designates major app store
operators (Apple App Store, Google Play) as gatekeepers and imposes new obligations:
— Sideloading: Apple must allow alternative app distribution on iOS in the EU.
— Alternative payment systems: app developers can use alternative payment processors
  for in-app purchases in the EU, potentially avoiding Apple/Google's 15–30% fee.
— Interoperability: third-party apps can request interoperability with dominant platforms.
UK equivalent: the CMA's Digital Markets regime under DMCCA 2024 will designate
  Strategic Market Status (SMS) companies with similar obligations (expected 2025–2026).
Implication for consumer apps: EU-based consumers can (from 2024) potentially access
  apps outside the App Store. The developer's in-app purchase commission model may
  change for EU users. UK changes expected to follow within 1–2 years.
Alex flag: any consumer app company building its monetisation model entirely around
  in-app subscription through Apple/Google should model the impact of commission
  reduction (from 30% to potentially 10–15%) and alternative payment processing on
  both unit economics and technical infrastructure.

PHYSICAL DTC — PACKAGING, RETURNS, AND DISTANCE SELLING:

Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013: consumers purchasing online have a 14-day right
  of return on most goods (no reason required). The seller must accept returns and
  refund including standard delivery costs. Exceptions: perishables, personalised goods,
  hygiene products once opened.
Extended producer responsibility (EPR): from 2025, UK producers and importers of
  packaging must pay fees based on the packaging they place on the market. DTC brands
  with significant packaging volumes must register and report.
Plastic packaging tax: UK plastic packaging tax (April 2022) applies to plastic
  packaging with less than 30% recycled content — £210.82 per tonne as of April 2024.
Alex flag: any DTC physical brand that has not assessed its EPR obligations and
  plastic packaging tax liability is carrying an unbudgeted cost. For high-volume DTC
  brands, these costs are material.
