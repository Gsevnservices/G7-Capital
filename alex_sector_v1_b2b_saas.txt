SECTION A — SECTOR IDENTIFICATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

PRIMARY CLASSIFICATION SIGNALS (any one = B2B SaaS):
— Subscription revenue model with monthly or annual recurring contracts
— Business entities as primary paying customers (not individuals paying personally)
— Software delivered via browser, mobile app, or API with no physical product component
— ACV explicitly stated or inferable from pricing page or customer count vs ARR

MISCLASSIFICATION WARNINGS:
— A company that sells to businesses but charges per-transaction is a marketplace, not SaaS
— A company with a subscription but significant services revenue is a hybrid — separate the streams
— A company where AI inference cost is the primary COGS may have SaaS pricing but not SaaS economics

SUB-CATEGORY IDENTIFICATION — SIGNALS FROM SUBMISSION:

1. HORIZONTAL SaaS
Signals: product works across all industries with no customisation; named competitive set includes
category leaders (Salesforce, HubSpot, Slack, Notion, Asana, Zoom); buyer is a functional
role (Head of Sales, Head of Marketing) not an industry-specific title; pricing is per-seat
or per-user without industry premium.
TAM: typically >£1B. Competition: high. GTM differentiation is the primary moat test.
Key risk: cannot win on product alone — distribution and brand must differentiate.

2. VERTICAL SaaS
Signals: product requires industry-specific data structures, workflows, or regulatory
knowledge; buyer title is industry-specific (Head of Underwriting, Practice Manager,
Contracts Director); pricing includes industry-specific modules; competitive set is
legacy on-premise software or spreadsheets rather than other SaaS companies.
TAM: £50M–£500M typical. Competition: low from SaaS peers. Switching cost: HIGH.
Key risk: TAM ceiling reached before venture returns materialise. Apply Enterprise Only
Trap test (PASS Deal 024) if realistic customer count is below 500.

3. DEVELOPER TOOLS / INFRASTRUCTURE
Signals: primary buyer is an engineer or engineering manager; product is consumed via
CLI, SDK, or API before a sales conversation begins; pricing includes usage-based component
(API calls, compute hours, seats); GitHub, npm, Homebrew, or marketplace listed as
distribution channel; free tier or open-source version exists.
TAM: variable. PLG motion is the indicator — if no free tier exists, verify why.
Key risk: Acqui-Hire Trap (PASS Deal 012). Check M&A interest from Datadog, Cloudflare,
GitHub, or Hashicorp before assessing as standalone investment.

4. AI-NATIVE SaaS
Signals: AI model inference is a direct COGS line item; product output requires a
foundation model (GPT, Claude, Gemini, or proprietary); product capability changes
materially with each model generation; gross margin stated above 65% requires COGS
decomposition to confirm inference costs are correctly allocated.
TAM: large but contested. Moat question is primary: proprietary data, workflow lock-in,
or switching cost — one of three must be present or PASS.
Key risk: foundation model provider changes pricing, deprecates model, or releases
competing product. Every AI-native SaaS carries this platform dependency risk.

5. WORKFLOW AUTOMATION
Signals: product replaces a specific manual process that is measurable in hours or FTEs;
ROI is quantifiable (e.g. "reduces X hours of manual work" or "replaces Y headcount");
buyer is operations, finance, legal, or compliance — not product or engineering;
pricing is often per-workflow or per-document rather than per-seat.
TAM: determined by the size of the addressable workflow. Apply Framework 1 (Business
Model Decomposition) to verify the ROI claim is real and the buyer can measure it.
Key risk: Services Revenue Masquerade (PASS Deal 023). Separate software licence from
implementation and configuration revenue before accepting gross margin.

SECTION C — SECTOR-SPECIFIC RED FLAGS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. BOOKED NOT RECOGNISED ARR
SIGNAL: Submission states ARR that includes multi-year contracts booked upfront;
no distinction between recognised revenue and total contract value.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: ARR is overstated; growth rate is partly an accounting choice.
VERIFICATION: Request MRR schedule month-by-month for the last 12 months.
If MRR does not compound consistently with stated ARR, the figure is TCV not ARR.

02. NRR INCLUDING NEW LOGOS
SIGNAL: Company defines NRR to include new customers acquired in the period.
TRUE NRR measures only what existing customers from a prior period do.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: NRR is inflated. A company with 80% true NRR can report 110%+
NRR by including new ARR from the cohort period. Obscures churn.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Is your NRR calculated on the same set of customers at start
and end of the measurement period, excluding any customer added during the period?"

03. BLENDED GROSS MARGIN HIDING SERVICES
SIGNAL: Submission presents a single gross margin figure for a product that includes
implementation, professional services, or managed services revenue.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: True software gross margin may be 20–30 points higher or lower than
the blended figure. Masks whether the software business is viable standalone.
VERIFICATION: Require disaggregated gross margin: software licence only vs services.
Software margin below 65% in a SaaS product is a structural concern.

04. LOGO CHURN DENOMINATOR MANIPULATION
SIGNAL: Annual logo churn stated as a percentage of current customer count, not
starting customer count. A company that grew from 20 to 40 customers and lost 4
reports 10% churn (4/40) when the real rate is 20% (4/20).
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Churn rate is understated by the ratio of growth. High-growth companies
can mask significant absolute churn behind a small percentage denominator.
VERIFICATION: Request absolute numbers: customers at start of period, gross new adds,
churned, ending count. Calculate: churned / starting count = true logo churn rate.

05. CAC EXCLUDING FOUNDER SALES TIME
SIGNAL: CAC is calculated on sales team salaries only; founder is primary or sole closer
and their time is not allocated to CAC. Common in founder-led sales at seed stage.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: True CAC is understated. When the founder is replaced by a sales team,
actual CAC will be 2–4x the stated figure. LTV/CAC ratio is overstated.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "Of your last 20 customer closings, how many required the founder
personally in the sales process?" If >50%, founder time must be allocated to CAC.

06. ANNUAL PREPAY MASKING MONTHLY CHURN
SIGNAL: Company reports low monthly churn but 80%+ of customers are on annual prepay
contracts. Monthly churn data covers only the few customers on monthly billing.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: True retention signal is unknown until the first annual renewal cycle.
First renewal churn — which occurs 12 months after customer acquisition — may be
30–50% in consumer-facing or low-ACV SaaS. Annual prepay creates a false 12-month
retention illusion.
VERIFICATION: Request first-renewal retention: of all customers whose first annual
contract reached renewal, what percentage renewed at the same or higher value?

07. USAGE-BASED PRICING VOLATILE REVENUE
SIGNAL: Company uses usage-based pricing (per API call, per compute hour, per document)
and reports ARR by annualising the most recent month's usage. ARR fluctuates with
customer usage cycles.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: ARR is not a committed revenue figure — it is a trailing usage extrapolation.
Annualising a peak usage month overstates committed revenue.
VERIFICATION: Request committed ARR (contractually obligated minimum) vs usage-run-rate ARR.
Difference between the two reveals revenue predictability.

08. ENTERPRISE PIPELINE THAT IS ALL PILOTS
SIGNAL: Submission lists 5+ enterprise logos as "in pipeline" or "evaluating."
Investigation reveals all are unpaid pilots or POCs with no commercial terms agreed.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: Reference Customer Illusion (PASS Deal 013). Pilot logos are not pipeline —
they are evaluation relationships with no commitment. A company with 10 enterprise pilots
and 0 closed contracts has no commercial traction.
VERIFICATION: For each named enterprise account, ask: "Is there a signed commercial
agreement or purchase order in place? What is the annual value?"

09. CUSTOMER SUCCESS STAFF RATIO ALARM
SIGNAL: Company has >£2M ARR and no dedicated customer success hire; all CS is handled
by the founders or shared with the sales team.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: NRR and retention figures at current scale may not hold as the customer
base grows. CS capacity is the primary limiter of NRR above 10 customers. A company
adding 5+ customers per month without a CS hire will experience expansion revenue
compression within 6 months.
VERIFICATION: At what customer count did the company hire its first CS person?
If the first CS hire has not happened above 20 customers, flag the risk.

10. SALES CYCLE LENGTHENING TREND
SIGNAL: Submission discloses average sales cycle duration and the current pipeline
has deals that have been open longer than the stated average.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Sales cycle is lengthening — either the market is becoming more cautious,
the ICP is shifting upmarket without a corresponding process change, or the product is
not compelling enough to close quickly without extensive evaluation.
VERIFICATION: Ask: "What was your average sales cycle 12 months ago vs today?"
A lengthening trend at a growth company is a meaningful slowdown signal.

11. BURN MULTIPLE ABOVE 4X WITH DECLINING GROWTH
SIGNAL: Burn multiple (net burn / net new ARR) is above 4x for two consecutive quarters
while YoY growth rate is declining.
SEVERITY: HIGH
WHAT IT MEANS: The company is spending an increasing amount to generate a declining
amount of new revenue. This is the CAC Spiral failure pattern in early form.
Companies with burn multiple above 4x and declining growth almost never raise
a subsequent round at a flat or up valuation.
VERIFICATION: Calculate burn multiple from the submission data. If not provided,
estimate from headcount + office costs vs new ARR added.

12. COHORT REVENUE CONCENTRATION IN YEAR 1
SIGNAL: More than 60% of current ARR was generated by customers signed in the most
recent 12 months. Older cohorts represent a small and shrinking share of ARR.
SEVERITY: MEDIUM
WHAT IT MEANS: Either the business is genuinely hypergrowth (positive) or older cohorts
are churning or contracting and being replaced by new cohort acquisition (negative).
Must distinguish between the two.
VERIFICATION: Request ARR waterfall by cohort year. If cohort year -2 and -3 represent
less than 20% combined of current ARR in a company with 30%+ YoY growth, ask for
cohort-level NRR to confirm cohort health.

SECTION D — HISTORICAL FAILURE PATTERNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. THE CHURN CLIFF
DESCRIPTION: A B2B SaaS company with excellent early retention metrics hits its first
mass annual renewal cycle (month 12–18) and experiences 25–40% logo churn in a 90-day
window. The prior retention metrics were all based on cohorts that had not yet reached
renewal. The churn cliff was not visible until it arrived.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Average contract age below 10 months across the customer base;
NRR reported from a cohort of customers all acquired in the same 6-month window;
Customer Success team sized for acquisition, not retention; no documented renewal
management process or QBR cadence.
WHEN TO APPLY: Series A companies where 70%+ of customers were acquired in the last
12 months. Any company where the stated logo churn is based on fewer than two full
annual renewal cycles.

2. THE ENTERPRISE TRAP
DESCRIPTION: A company wins 2–3 large enterprise accounts that each represent £200K+
ARR. The CS, implementation, and product teams are consumed by the requirements of these
accounts. SMB customers receive no support, churn at 30–40% annually, and cease to be
a growth engine. The company becomes a de facto services business for 3 clients.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: One customer >25% of ARR; CS team spending >50% of capacity on
one or two accounts; product roadmap driven by enterprise-specific requirements; SMB
logo churn above 20% annually while enterprise NRR is reported as 140%+.
WHEN TO APPLY: Companies with a mixed SMB/enterprise customer base where enterprise
ACV is 10x+ higher than SMB ACV and the sales motion has shifted entirely to enterprise.

3. THE FEATURE FACTORY
DESCRIPTION: A company ships new features every 2–4 weeks in response to customer
requests. The roadmap is entirely reactive. Core product experience degrades because
no engineering time is allocated to improving the fundamental value proposition.
NRR begins declining 6–9 months before the team notices, because expansion ARR from
new features does not offset the quiet non-renewal of customers who never found
the core product compelling.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Product roadmap contains more than 70% customer-requested
features vs founder-initiated product vision; release velocity is high but DAU/MAU
ratio is declining; NRR expansion is driven by upsell modules, not deeper usage
of the core product; time-to-value for new customers increasing quarter-on-quarter.
WHEN TO APPLY: Series A and B companies where the product team has grown above 8 engineers.

4. THE PLG-TO-ENTERPRISE TRANSITION FAILURE
DESCRIPTION: A company with a successful product-led growth motion (free tier → paid
self-serve) attempts to move upmarket to enterprise. It hires an enterprise sales team
but the product has no enterprise-grade features (SSO, RBAC, audit logs, SLA guarantees,
procurement-compatible contracts). Enterprise deals stall in security review. The sales
team burns through enterprise pipeline without closing. PLG self-serve growth continues
but the enterprise investment destroys burn efficiency.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Enterprise sales team hired before enterprise product features
shipped; no enterprise-tier pricing available; sales cycle on enterprise deals exceeding
9 months with no signed contracts after 12 months of enterprise motion; Magic Number
below 0.3 while burn multiple rises above 3x.
WHEN TO APPLY: Developer tool or horizontal SaaS companies attempting upmarket move
with £1–5M ARR and a primarily self-serve customer base.

5. THE PRICING MODEL FLIP
DESCRIPTION: A company achieves Series A traction on per-seat pricing with good NRR,
then switches to usage-based pricing to signal enterprise market ambition. Existing
customers on per-seat contracts continue to generate predictable ARR. New customers
on usage-based contracts generate volatile revenue that is difficult to model. NRR
from usage-based cohorts is lower in year 1 than per-seat cohorts were. The Series A
NRR that secured the funding is not reproducible under the new pricing model.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: Pricing model change announced mid-funding cycle; CFO presenting
"annualised run rate" on usage data rather than contracted ARR; new cohort NRR below
105% in first 12 months; salespeople requesting exceptions to return to per-seat pricing
for specific deals.
WHEN TO APPLY: Any company between Series A and Series B that has changed its
primary pricing model within the last 18 months.

6. THE CAC SPIRAL
DESCRIPTION: Early customers were acquired cheaply via founder network, inbound
content, or viral referral. As these channels saturate, the company increases paid
acquisition spend to maintain growth targets. CAC rises 30–50% year-on-year while
LTV is unchanged. Burn multiple rises from 2x to 5x. Revenue growth continues to
look healthy in absolute terms but the unit economics of new cohorts are fundamentally
worse than the cohorts that justified the investment.
EARLY WARNING SIGNALS: CAC has increased more than 30% in the last 12 months while
LTV/CAC ratio has declined below 3x; paid acquisition is above 50% of gross new ARR
acquisition cost; blended CAC is rising while organic and referral channel volume is flat
or declining; payback period on new cohorts exceeds 24 months.
WHEN TO APPLY: Series A and B companies where paid acquisition is a material
customer acquisition channel and growth rate is being maintained above 60% YoY.

SECTION F — KEY QUESTIONS FOR FOUNDER MEETING
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

01. NRR COMPOSITION
QUESTION: "Can you break down your NRR into three components: seat or usage expansion
from existing customers, new modules or products sold to existing customers, and
offset for any contraction or churn? What is each component as a percentage of your NRR?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific percentages for each component; expansion from core product
usage is the largest driver (not add-on module sales); contraction is below 5%.
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot separate components; describes NRR as "customers buying more
because they love the product" without quantification.
REVEALS: Whether NRR is driven by genuine product value expansion or by upsell
motion that could decline when the upsell pipeline is exhausted.

02. CAC BY CHANNEL
QUESTION: "What is your customer acquisition cost broken down by channel — outbound,
inbound content, paid acquisition, partner or referral, and founder-led? And how has
your blended CAC changed over the last four quarters?"
STRONG ANSWER: Precise CAC by channel; identifies the highest-ROI channel; confirms
blended CAC has been stable or declining as the team has scaled.
WEAK ANSWER: Provides a single blended number; cannot distinguish channel economics;
describes acquisition anecdotally rather than by cost.
REVEALS: Whether growth is economically sustainable and whether the current team can
continue acquiring customers at scale without the founder.

03. SALES CYCLE TREND
QUESTION: "What was your average sales cycle from first outbound or inbound contact to
signed contract 18 months ago, and what is it today? What accounts for the change?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific number for both periods; if lengthening, provides a specific
structural explanation (upmarket shift, new procurement requirements, category uncertainty).
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot recall the 18-month-ago figure; describes it as "roughly the same."
REVEALS: Whether competitive dynamics or deal quality are changing. A lengthening
cycle at constant team size means falling sales capacity per head.

04. LOGO CHURN BY COHORT YEAR
QUESTION: "For customers you acquired in each of the last three years, what percentage
reached their first annual renewal and renewed? And what percentage reached their second
renewal and renewed? Can you give me absolute numbers?"
STRONG ANSWER: Cohort data available; first-renewal rate above 85%; second-renewal rate
above 90% (retention typically improves after year 2 as customers are embedded).
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot separate by cohort; describes overall churn; has not calculated
cohort-level renewal rates.
REVEALS: Whether the Churn Cliff failure pattern is forming in a cohort that has not
yet reached renewal. First renewal is the highest-risk moment in SaaS retention.

05. COMPETITIVE WIN/LOSS RATE
QUESTION: "In competitive deals — where you know the prospect evaluated at least one
other vendor — what is your win rate? And which competitor do you lose to most often
and why, in the customer's words?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific win rate (e.g. 62% in competitive evaluations); names the
primary competitor they lose to; can quote what customers said about why they chose
the competitor; describes what they are doing to address it.
WEAK ANSWER: "We win most competitive deals." Cannot quantify. Describes competitors
as "legacy tools" rather than naming the real comparison.
REVEALS: Whether competitive moat is real or assumed. A founder who cannot describe
why they lose competitive deals has not done the loss analysis.

06. TIME TO VALUE
QUESTION: "How long does it take from contract signature to the customer achieving
their first measurable outcome using your product? And how has that changed in the
last 12 months?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific number (e.g. "23 days to first report generated"); trend is
decreasing as onboarding has been systematised; has a dedicated onboarding motion.
WEAK ANSWER: "It depends on the customer." Cannot give a median figure. Does not track
time-to-value as a metric.
REVEALS: CS capacity and product maturity. Long or worsening time-to-value predicts
first-renewal churn — customers who never fully onboard almost never renew.

07. NET NEW ARR vs EXPANSION ARR SPLIT
QUESTION: "In the last 12 months, what percentage of your ARR growth came from new
logos versus expansion in existing accounts? How has that ratio changed over time?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific split (e.g. 60% new logo, 40% expansion); expansion percentage
is growing over time as the customer base matures; can project what expansion ARR
becomes as a share of growth at Series B scale.
WEAK ANSWER: Reports total ARR growth without distinguishing source; expansion is
described but not measured.
REVEALS: NRR quality and business maturity. A company where expansion ARR is growing
as a share of total growth has a compounding revenue engine. One entirely dependent
on new logos needs perpetual acquisition investment.

08. IMPLEMENTATION FAILURE RATE
QUESTION: "Of all the customers who have signed contracts in the last 24 months, how
many failed to fully implement the product and subsequently churned or did not renew?"
STRONG ANSWER: Specific number and percentage; has a root cause analysis for each
failed implementation; has changed the implementation process as a result.
WEAK ANSWER: "It occasionally happens." Cannot quantify. Describes implementation
failure as the customer's fault rather than a process gap.
REVEALS: Implementation failure is the single largest predictor of first-renewal churn
in enterprise SaaS. A company that cannot quantify implementation failure rate does not
understand its own churn risk.

09. KEY CUSTOMER CONCENTRATION
QUESTION: "What percentage of your ARR is accounted for by your three largest customers?
And for each of the top three, when does their contract come up for renewal and who is
the primary relationship holder on our side?"
STRONG ANSWER: Top three are below 30% combined; each has multiple stakeholders engaged;
relationship is held institutionally not by a single individual.
WEAK ANSWER: Top three exceed 40% combined; primary relationship is the founder or one
account manager; renewal dates cluster in the same quarter.
REVEALS: Revenue fragility and the Key Person Dependency pattern from Wrong Call 026.
A renewal cluster in one quarter combined with concentrated ARR is a liquidity risk.

10. PRICING MODEL EVOLUTION
QUESTION: "How has your pricing model changed since you launched? Are you charging more
or less per customer than you were 18 months ago, and why?"
STRONG ANSWER: ACV has increased as the company has moved upmarket; has evidence that
customers accept the higher price without extended negotiation; gross margin has improved.
WEAK ANSWER: Pricing has declined to close deals; offering significant discounts on
standard pricing; has not increased prices despite growth in product value.
REVEALS: Pricing confidence and product value conviction. Companies that discount
to close deals have not yet demonstrated that their product is must-have.

11. PRODUCT ROADMAP PRIORITISATION
QUESTION: "How do you decide what goes on the product roadmap? What percentage of
your last three quarters' engineering output was customer-requested versus product-vision-driven?"
STRONG ANSWER: Has a clear framework — prioritises by impact on retention or expansion
revenue, not by customer request volume; can name 2–3 features shipped that no customer
asked for but that drove measurable retention improvement.
WEAK ANSWER: "We build what customers ask for." Roadmap is customer-request driven
with no independent product vision. Cannot name a proactive product bet.
REVEALS: Whether the Feature Factory failure pattern is forming. A 100% reactive
roadmap indicates the product team is executing for existing customers, not building
the future product that will win new ones.

12. CUSTOMER SUCCESS EFFICIENCY
QUESTION: "How many customers is each customer success manager responsible for today,
and what is your target CSM-to-customer ratio at Series B ARR? How do you plan to
get from here to there without NRR declining?"
STRONG ANSWER: Current ratio is clear (e.g. 1 CSM per 40 customers at £15K ACV);
target ratio is defined and benchmarked; plan for scale includes automated onboarding
and health scoring.
WEAK ANSWER: Cannot state current ratio; describes CS as "highly personalised" without
acknowledging the scaling challenge; has not modelled CS headcount requirements.
REVEALS: Whether the company has a scalable CS model or is building a relationship-
intensive business that cannot improve margins as it grows.

DATA PRIVACY — GDPR (UK and EU):

Applicability threshold: Any B2B SaaS company that processes personal data of
EU or UK data subjects on behalf of enterprise customers is a Data Processor under
GDPR. This applies from the first enterprise customer, not at a revenue threshold.

What compliance requires at each stage:
Pre-seed / Seed: Privacy Policy and Cookie Policy on website; Data Processing Agreement
(DPA) template available for enterprise customers who request it; no need for a DPO
unless processing data at scale or processing special category data.
Series A: DPA signed with every enterprise customer; record of processing activities
maintained; data residency documented (which region data is stored in); breach
notification process in place; vendor DPAs signed with all sub-processors (AWS, GCP,
Stripe, etc.).
Series B and beyond: UK and EU Data Protection Officer required if processing >250
employees' personal data or sensitive categories; regular GDPR audits; data retention
schedule enforced; penetration testing of personal data systems.

When non-compliance becomes material: At the first enterprise procurement process —
typically Series A stage — enterprise buyers will request a completed security
questionnaire. If the company cannot demonstrate basic GDPR compliance (DPA template,
data residency documentation, breach process), deals will stall or fail.
Material risk: ICO fines up to £17.5M or 4% of global annual turnover for UK GDPR
breaches. Enterprise contracts increasingly include audit rights and indemnity clauses
for data breaches.

Specific questions to ask: "Do you have a signed DPA with each enterprise customer?
Where is customer data stored and can you certify it is not transferred outside the UK/EU?
Have you experienced any personal data breach in the last 24 months?"

AI REGULATION — EU AI Act (effective 2026):

High-risk AI classifications relevant to B2B SaaS:
Recruitment and HR screening tools (automated CV screening, candidate scoring) — High Risk.
Credit scoring and financial risk assessment tools — High Risk.
Biometric identification systems — Prohibited in most public contexts.
Any AI tool that makes or significantly influences decisions about individuals in
employment, education, credit, or critical infrastructure — High Risk.

Low-risk AI (most B2B SaaS applications): Text generation, document summarisation,
code assistance, customer service chatbots, sales intelligence, marketing copy.
These require transparency disclosures (users must know AI is involved) but no
conformity assessment or registration.

Timeline for B2B SaaS compliance:
August 2026: Prohibited practices ban effective. Any AI system that manipulates
users or exploits vulnerabilities becomes illegal.
August 2027: High-risk AI systems must be registered, audited, and meet technical
documentation requirements.
Implication: B2B SaaS companies with high-risk AI applications must begin compliance
preparation by mid-2025 to meet the 2027 deadline. A company selling AI-driven
HR screening or credit tools that has not begun EU AI Act preparation is carrying
a regulatory risk that could delay or prevent EU market entry.

SECTOR-SPECIFIC COMPLIANCE — REGULATED INDUSTRY INHERITANCE:

When a B2B SaaS company sells into a regulated industry, it inherits compliance
obligations from its customers:

Financial Services (FCA-regulated customers):
The SaaS vendor becomes an "outsourced service provider" under FCA SYSC rules.
Enterprise banks and asset managers must conduct operational resilience assessments
of all critical third-party suppliers. This requires: SOC 2 Type II report; penetration
test within the last 12 months; documented business continuity plan; right-to-audit clause.
Timeline: Enterprise financial services deals will not close without SOC 2 at Series A.

Healthcare (NHS or regulated health data):
DSPT (Data Security and Protection Toolkit) required for any supplier handling NHS data.
Cyber Essentials Plus required as minimum. Clinical data processing requires NHS DPIA.
Timeline: No NHS enterprise deal proceeds without DSPT accreditation. Minimum 3-month
process to obtain.

Government / Public Sector:
G-Cloud 14 framework listing required for NHS and most UK government procurement.
Cyber Essentials (not Plus) is minimum for G-Cloud listing. Cyber Essentials Plus
required for contracts involving sensitive government data.
Timeline: G-Cloud application process takes 4–8 weeks. Without listing, public sector
direct award is not possible above £10,000.

CERTIFICATION TIMELINE — WHEN ENTERPRISE EXPECTS EACH:

Seed stage: Privacy Policy + DPA template = minimum viable for any enterprise sales.
Series A: SOC 2 Type I (first audit, faster) at minimum. ISO 27001 if selling to
European enterprise. Cyber Essentials for UK public sector pipeline.
Series B: SOC 2 Type II (annual recurring audit). ISO 27001 full certification.
Penetration test documented and shared with enterprise security teams on request.

SOC 2 Type I cost and timeline: £15,000–£40,000; 3–4 months to complete.
SOC 2 Type II cost and timeline: £20,000–£60,000 annually; first audit takes 6 months
(requires 6 months of operating history under audit controls).
ISO 27001 cost and timeline: £25,000–£80,000; 9–12 months to certification.

Flag to firm: If a Series A company selling to financial services, healthcare, or
government has not begun SOC 2 preparation, every enterprise deal in the pipeline
is at risk of stalling at security review. Ask: "What is your current certification
status and what is your timeline for SOC 2 Type II?"
