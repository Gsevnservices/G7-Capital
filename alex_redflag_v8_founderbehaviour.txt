ALEX — RED FLAG LIBRARY
CATEGORY 8: FOUNDER BEHAVIOUR RED FLAGS
Version 1.0. Built: April 2026.
15 flags. Apply mechanically to every deal submission.
These flags are observable from a submission or public sources
without a meeting.
Two analysts applying these rules reach the same conclusion.
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION RULE:
For every deal submission, Alex checks all 15 flags before completing
the screening note. Flags are not discretionary — if the signal is
present, the flag is raised. Flags are then weighed in aggregate.
One CRITICAL flag = automatic deep investigation required.
Two or more HIGH flags = note this in the Risk Assessment section.
Founder behaviour flags are the most difficult category to apply
consistently because the instinct is to dismiss behavioural signals
as subjective. They are not. Every flag in this category describes
an observable, documentable signal — a numeric inconsistency in the
deck, a LinkedIn post, a Companies House filing, a definition missing
from a metrics table — not an impression or a feeling. Apply these
flags to what is written and what is publicly available. Never apply
them to what was said in a meeting or what Alex infers from tone.
The purpose of this category is not to psychoanalyse founders.
It is to document the observable signals that predict whether the
person asking for capital will use it in the way they describe.

FLAG FB01: INCONSISTENT NARRATIVE ACROSS MATERIALS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The founding story, the problem statement, or the key metrics are
described differently in different sections of the submission or
across different public sources. Observable by comparing: (1) the
company description in the submission deck versus the company
description on the company website; (2) the founding year stated
in the deck versus the incorporation date on Companies House;
(3) the ARR or customer count figure cited in the executive summary
versus the figure cited in the traction section of the same deck;
(4) the problem framing in the deck versus the problem framing in
the most recent press coverage or investor update. A single minor
discrepancy is noise. Two or more material inconsistencies — where
the divergence changes the nature of the claim — are a flag.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Narrative inconsistency is the foundational signal that a company's
materials have been assembled from multiple sources without coherent
authorship — which is itself informative — or that the company's
story has evolved to fit different audiences without the underlying
facts being updated consistently. The three failure modes: (1) the
company has been pitching different stories to different investor
types — a technical story to deep-tech investors, a commercial story
to growth investors — and the materials reflect this without the
founder noticing the contradictions; (2) the metrics were updated
in one section of the deck but not all sections, producing stale
data in one place and current data in another — which means the
metrics themselves may not be reliably tracked; (3) the founding
story has been retroactively adjusted to better fit the current
narrative — a common phenomenon where the "aha moment" moves, the
founding team composition changes in the retelling, or the timeline
compresses. None of these patterns is necessarily malicious, but
all of them indicate that the company's materials cannot be taken
at face value — every material claim requires independent verification.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A key financial metric — ARR, customer count, revenue, or funding
raised — is stated at materially different levels in different
sections of the same submission document, with no explanation
for the discrepancy. A company that cannot maintain consistent
financial data within a single document is a company that either
does not have reliable data or has presented data that has been
selectively chosen for different audiences.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The inconsistency is in a secondary narrative detail —
the founding story has been updated to reflect a more accurate
timeline, the problem statement has evolved as the market
understanding has improved — and the core financial and
operational metrics are consistent throughout.
(b) The inconsistency is explained by a specific known event:
the company rebranded, pivoted, or restated figures following
an accounting change, and the different descriptions reflect
pre- and post- event states.
(c) The discrepancy is in public sources (press, LinkedIn) that
predate the current materials by more than 12 months — narratives
legitimately evolve over time and historic public statements are
not always updated to reflect current positioning.

VERIFICATION:
Cross-reference: (1) founding date in deck vs Companies House
incorporation date; (2) ARR/revenue figures in summary vs traction
section vs financial appendix; (3) customer count in the problem
statement vs the traction slide vs any press releases. Document
each inconsistency with the specific discrepancy and the source.
Ask: "Your deck states [figure A] in the executive summary and
[figure B] in the traction section — which is the current number
and what does the other figure refer to?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When two or more material metrics are stated at different levels
in different sections of the submission, document each inconsistency
and request a reconciliation before advancing — decks with internal
metric inconsistencies reliably produce data quality problems in
diligence and indicate that the financial reporting is not reliable
enough to base an investment decision on.

FLAG FB02: METRICS PRESENTED WITHOUT STANDARD DEFINITIONS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
ARR, NRR, DAU, MAU, GMV, or other commonly-defined industry metrics
are cited in the submission without stating the definition used —
allowing for non-standard calculation that would inflate the headline
number relative to the industry standard. Observable from any metrics
table or traction slide that presents a metric without a footnote,
appendix, or inline definition specifying exactly what is included
in the calculation. The flag triggers specifically when the metric
is one where non-standard definitions are common: ARR that may include
one-time professional services; NRR calculated on an ending rather
than starting cohort; DAU that counts any app open rather than a
qualifying action; GMV that includes cancelled or refunded transactions.

WHAT IT MEANS:
The presentation of undefined metrics is how a company communicates
a number that appears to be one thing and is actually another.
The most common manipulations, each observable from context clues:
(1) ARR that includes professional services or one-time fees — detectable
when the ARR figure is cited alongside a revenue figure that is lower
than the ARR would imply, suggesting the ARR contains non-recurring
components; (2) NRR calculated on ending cohort rather than starting
cohort — detectable when the NRR figure is higher than the company's
gross retention and expansion metrics would independently suggest;
(3) GMV presented for a marketplace where the company retains only
a small take rate — the £50M GMV company with a 5% take rate has
£2.5M revenue, not £50M, and the distinction matters acutely for
valuation multiples; (4) MAU that counts bot traffic, trial users,
or single-action sessions — detectable when the MAU figure implies
an engagement rate that is implausible for the category. A company
that presents metrics without definitions either does not know the
standard definitions (concerning) or knows them and prefers not
to disclose which definition they are using (disqualifying).

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company has previously provided different definitions of the
same metric to different investors — observable when two investor
updates from different periods define ARR or NRR differently —
indicating that the metric definition is being adjusted to produce
the most favourable headline in each context.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company provides definitions when asked and the definitions
align with standard industry practice — the omission from the
deck was an editing decision, not a concealment strategy.
(b) The metric in question has a company-specific definition
that is disclosed and explained — e.g. "our ARR includes only
committed recurring contract value, excluding all professional
services and one-time fees" — and the definition is more
conservative than the industry standard.
(c) The metric is not a headline commercial metric — the undefined
term is a technical product metric (session length, feature
adoption rate) where no standard industry definition exists.

VERIFICATION:
For every metric cited without a definition, ask: "How do you
define [metric]? Specifically: (1) what is included and excluded;
(2) what is the calculation methodology; (3) is this definition
consistent with how you have calculated this metric historically?"
Then cross-check the defined metric against other metrics in the
deck to test internal consistency — a correctly-defined ARR
implies a specific range of customer count at the stated ACV;
a correctly-defined NRR implies a specific gross retention rate.
Internal consistency is the verification, not just the definition.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a traction slide presents ARR, NRR, or GMV without definitions,
request the metric definitions and then cross-check the defined
figures against other disclosed metrics for internal consistency
before accepting the headline numbers — non-standard metric
definitions are the most common source of materially overstated
traction in early-stage submissions.

FLAG FB03: SELECTIVE DISCLOSURE OF TIME PERIODS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Growth metrics in the submission cite a specific time period that
is materially shorter or differently bounded than the standard
reporting period — and the non-standard period has the effect
of producing a more favourable growth rate than the standard period
would show. Observable when: (1) a deck cites "last 6 months" growth
rather than trailing 12 months, and the last 6 months include a
period of unusually high growth that is not representative of the
full year; (2) growth is cited "since our last funding round" rather
than since a calendar start date — allowing the company to choose
a low starting point; (3) a company with declining growth cites
only the period before the deceleration began; (4) a company cites
"since product launch" when the launch date was chosen to coincide
with an unusually strong period.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Selective time period disclosure is one of the most reliable signals
of deliberate metric management. The mechanism: any business has
some period in its history — a product launch, a viral moment,
a partnership announcement, a seasonal spike — where growth was
above average. By selecting that period as the baseline or endpoint
for a growth claim, a founder can cite a growth rate that is true
for that window but unrepresentative of the underlying business
trajectory. The tell is always in the specificity of the period
chosen: "last 6 months" when all other metrics are reported annually;
"since we launched our enterprise product" when the launch date
is seven months ago and the preceding period showed declining growth.
A business with genuine, consistent growth has no reason to select
a non-standard reporting period — it presents trailing twelve months
because the trailing twelve months is its best evidence.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company cites a growth rate for a cherry-picked period AND
refuses to provide trailing twelve month growth data when asked —
the refusal to provide the standard metric after a specific request
is a disqualifying behaviour signal, not merely a presentation issue.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The non-standard period has a specific, disclosed reason that
is unrelated to growth optimisation — "since our product launch"
is appropriate when the product was launched in the reporting period
and there are no prior periods of meaningful revenue to include.
(b) The company provides both the period cited and the trailing
twelve month comparison, noting that the shorter period is provided
for a specific operational reason.
(c) The metric in question is genuinely only measurable from a
specific start point — a marketplace's GMV since launch is only
meaningful since launch if the product did not exist before.

VERIFICATION:
For every growth metric that uses a non-standard time period,
calculate the same metric on a trailing twelve month basis using
the data available in the submission. Ask: "What is your trailing
twelve month growth rate for [metric]? Can you provide quarterly
data for the last 8 quarters so we can see the full growth trend?"
The response to a request for trailing twelve month data — whether
it is provided promptly and without comment, or whether it triggers
hesitation, alternative figures, or deflection — is itself informative.

PATTERN LESSON:
When growth metrics are cited for non-standard periods that produce
a more favourable result than trailing twelve months would, calculate
the implied trailing twelve month rate from the available data and
note the divergence — selectively bounded growth claims are the
most common technique for presenting a decelerating business as
a growing one without making a technically false statement.

FLAG FB04: COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE SYSTEMATICALLY UNDERSTATED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
Every competitor mentioned in the deck or submission is described
as significantly inferior across all dimensions — slower, more
expensive, worse product, shrinking customer base — with no
acknowledgment of any competitor strength. Observable from the
competitive landscape slide or section where the standard 2×2
matrix places the company in the top-right quadrant with all
competitors in the bottom-left, or where each named competitor
is described only through its weaknesses. The flag triggers when
the competitive assessment is uniformly negative across all
competitors and when no competitor is credited with a single
genuine strength.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A founder who cannot name a competitor's genuine strength does not
understand the competitive landscape, has not done serious competitive
analysis, or has chosen to present a misleading competitive picture.
In any market with more than £10M of capital deployed, some competitors
are good at something. A B2B SaaS market with three funded competitors
will have at least one with better sales execution, at least one
with a deeper product in a specific vertical, and at least one with
stronger brand recognition among a specific buyer persona. A founder
who cannot articulate what each competitor does well has either
not talked to enough customers — who will be able to tell them —
or is not presenting honestly. The downstream consequence: a founder
who does not understand the competitive landscape will be surprised
by it. They will lose deals to competitors they described as inferior
and will struggle to explain the losses. They will hire a sales team
that does not know how to handle competitive objections because
no one told them to expect any. They will price against a market
they have not correctly mapped. The competitive assessment is not
just a slide — it is a diagnostic of how the founder thinks about
the market they are operating in.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The submission claims there is no significant competition in a
market where a 5-minute Google search identifies multiple funded
competitors — this is not a nuanced competitive assessment but
a factual inaccuracy that must be explained before any further
engagement. See also Flag FB13.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The founder explicitly acknowledges specific competitor strengths
in the submission and explains how the company's positioning either
avoids direct competition on those dimensions or counters them —
a genuinely differentiated analysis acknowledges and then addresses
competitive strength.
(b) The company competes in a market segment that the named competitors
have not entered — the competitive landscape is segmented, and the
founder can articulate why.
(c) The competitive analysis covers only the current state and notes
that the landscape is evolving — an honest assessment of a fast-
moving market may legitimately place the company ahead of current
competitors while acknowledging that the competitive picture will
change.

VERIFICATION:
Run a 5-minute competitive research exercise before reviewing the
company's competitive claims: search for the top 3 competitors
in the space, identify their product reviews, their customer
testimonials, and their fundraising trajectory on Crunchbase.
Then compare what the company says about each competitor to what
is publicly known. Specifically note any competitor strength that
the company's analysis omits. Ask: "What does [competitor name]
do well? Where have you lost deals to them and why?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When the competitive landscape slide credits no competitor with
a single genuine strength, run an independent 5-minute search
on each named competitor before accepting the framing — a company
that has not correctly mapped competitive strengths will be unable
to articulate a credible competitive response when asked in diligence,
and the gap between the submitted narrative and external reality
is itself a signal about market understanding.

FLAG FB05: PRIOR INVESTOR RELATIONSHIPS NOT DISCLOSED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Prior investors or angels are not listed on the cap table or
mentioned in the deck despite being discoverable through public
records. Observable by cross-referencing the disclosed cap table
against: (1) Companies House shareholder register, which lists
all shareholders holding more than 25% (PSC register) and, in
the annual confirmation statement, all shareholders by name;
(2) Crunchbase or LinkedIn investor announcements from prior
fundraising rounds; (3) press coverage of prior funding events
that named investors who do not appear on the current cap table.
The flag triggers when a named investor in a prior funding
announcement does not appear on the current cap table and
the discrepancy is not explained.

WHAT IT MEANS:
An investor who has been removed from the cap table since a prior
public announcement is there for one of three reasons: (1) they
were bought out — the company or other investors purchased their
stake — and the buyout is not disclosed; (2) there was a dispute
that resulted in equity transfer — a non-disclosed legal settlement;
(3) the prior announcement was inaccurate — the investor did not
actually invest and was named in error, which is itself a misleading
practice. None of these explanations is benign from a diligence
perspective. An undisclosed buyout means the company spent capital
to repurchase equity rather than deploy it in the business — where
did the capital come from? An undisclosed legal settlement means
the company has had a shareholder dispute that produced litigation
or a negotiated exit — the nature of the dispute is relevant to
how the company handles investor relationships. The additional
concern: a cap table that does not match public records is a cap
table that may have other inaccuracies not detectable from public
sources. If one investor is missing, others may be as well.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A prior investor is absent from the current cap table AND the
company cannot provide a clear, documented explanation for the
absence — an equity transfer agreement, a buyout confirmation,
or a correction to the prior announcement. The absence of an
explanation for a missing shareholder is a due diligence failure
that must be resolved before any further engagement.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The missing investor is explained by a documented, arm's-length
buyout at a disclosed price with disclosed funding — the company
can show where the buyout capital came from and that it was
properly approved by the board.
(b) The prior funding announcement was genuinely in error —
the named investor did not complete the investment — and this
is verifiable through the investor's own public records or
confirmation.
(c) The investor transferred their stake to a connected entity
that does appear on the cap table — a fund to a fund vehicle,
or an individual to a holding company — and the transfer is
documented.

VERIFICATION:
Run a Companies House confirmation statement check for all UK-
incorporated companies — the most recent confirmation statement
lists all shareholders. Cross-reference against Crunchbase for
all announced investors. Cross-reference against any press coverage
naming investors. Identify any investor present in public records
who is absent from the submitted cap table. Ask: "Your cap table
does not include [named investor] who was named in [source] as
an investor. Can you explain why they are not on the current cap table?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a Companies House or Crunchbase search identifies prior
investors not present on the submitted cap table, request a
documented explanation before advancing — a cap table with missing
shareholders has a provenance problem that must be resolved before
legal due diligence can produce a clean title opinion.

FLAG FB06: FUNDRAISING URGENCY MANUFACTURED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The submission or accompanying communications create artificial
closing urgency — "closing in 2 weeks," "term sheet from [named firm]
expiring," "allocation filling fast" — without providing verifiable
evidence. Observable from any written communication, deck footnote,
or submission note that uses time-bounded pressure language without
an attached document confirming the stated condition: a countersigned
term sheet, a round closing confirmation, or a written oversubscription
statement from the lead investor.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Manufactured fundraising urgency is a negotiating tactic — it is
designed to compress the investor's diligence timeline and extract
a commitment before the investor has gathered sufficient evidence
to make a decision. The tactic is informative about the founder
because it trades short-term process advantage for long-term
relationship damage: professional investors recognise manufactured
urgency because they see it regularly, and it consistently produces
the opposite of its intended effect — investors slow down rather
than speed up, and become more sceptical of all other claims in
the materials. The deeper signal: a founder who uses artificial
urgency is a founder who believes their business cannot attract
capital on the merits alone — and the self-assessment embedded
in that belief is relevant data. Genuinely competitive rounds
generate real urgency from real oversubscription — and that urgency
is verifiable through the lead investor. A verifiable term sheet
expiry is not manufactured urgency. An unverifiable claim about
one is.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder claims a term sheet from a named investor as urgency
leverage, the named investor is a real institutional firm, and when
contacted (directly or through a network reference) the firm either
denies the term sheet or indicates it is not on the terms described.
This is not a deal process tactic — it is a misrepresentation.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The urgency is real and verifiable — a countersigned term sheet
with an expiry date is shared upon request, and the timeline is
genuine rather than manufactured.
(b) The company is genuinely oversubscribed and provides a
round status update from the lead investor — not the founder —
confirming limited remaining allocation.
(c) The urgency reflects a genuine operational timeline — a
government grant match-funding deadline, a customer contract
requiring balance sheet confirmation, a partnership closing date —
that is documentable and not under the founder's control.

VERIFICATION:
Request evidence for any urgency claim before adjusting diligence
pace: (1) for a term sheet claim, request the countersigned term
sheet with the stated expiry date; (2) for a closing claim, request
a round status update from the lead investor; (3) for an allocation
claim, request written confirmation from the lead that the round
is oversubscribed. If evidence is not provided within 48 hours,
proceed at normal pace. Ask: "Can you share the term sheet you
mentioned so we can calibrate our timeline accordingly?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When fundraising urgency is claimed without verifiable evidence,
continue at normal diligence pace and request the supporting
document — manufactured urgency that is called out and unsubstantiated
typically disappears without affecting the deal, and the founder's
response to the request for evidence is itself informative about
how they handle pressure and factual challenges.

FLAG FB07: FOUNDER PUBLICLY CRITICAL OF PRIOR INVESTORS OR EMPLOYERS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
Public statements by the founder — on LinkedIn, Twitter/X, in
interviews, podcasts, or published articles — contain significant
criticism of prior investors, former employers, co-founders, or
team members by name or in a way that makes them identifiable.
Observable by searching the founder's name on LinkedIn and Twitter/X
and reviewing the last 24 months of public posts and long-form
content. The flag triggers for criticism that is: (1) directed at
a specific named or identifiable individual or firm; (2) describes
a grievance about professional conduct, competence, or character;
(3) was posted publicly rather than shared privately.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Founders who are publicly critical of prior investors, employers,
or colleagues are providing direct evidence about how they handle
professional disappointment, disagreement, and conflict. The
investor-specific version of this flag is the most directly relevant:
a founder who has publicly criticised prior investors is a founder
who, when the relationship with the current investor becomes difficult
(as it inevitably will at some point in the company's life), may
respond the same way. The employer criticism version is related:
every successful founder has worked somewhere they did not enjoy
or left under difficult circumstances. The founder who discusses
these experiences publicly, with named criticism of former colleagues,
has not developed the professional judgement to distinguish between
what is useful to share and what is damaging to themselves and
others. This judgement deficit is the same one that produces bad
decisions about customer communication, team management, and
regulatory relationships. The flag is not about protecting
the feelings of prior investors or employers. It is about
documenting that the founder's judgement in a category directly
related to the investor relationship is demonstrably limited.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder has made public statements that are potentially
defamatory, that have resulted in legal proceedings, or that
name a specific investor by name in a way that constitutes
a public allegation of misconduct without documented evidence.
These are not professional misjudgements — they are legal risks
that transfer to any company the founder leads.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The public criticism is of an institution or a system —
a critique of the VC model, a criticism of an industry practice,
a public commentary on a policy issue — rather than a personal
criticism of a named individual or firm's conduct.
(b) The criticism is historical (more than 3 years old) and
the founder has demonstrably built constructive public relationships
with investors and professional contacts since — the historic
statement is not predictive of current behaviour.
(c) The criticism refers to a well-documented public event —
a company collapse, a published fraud investigation, a publicly
known governance failure — where the criticism is factual and
contextually appropriate.

VERIFICATION:
Search: founder full name + "LinkedIn", founder full name + "Twitter",
founder full name + interview/podcast. Review the last 24 months
of public content. Specifically look for: (1) posts naming
prior investors or employers critically; (2) threads describing
professional conflicts; (3) interview quotes about prior
relationships. Document specific statements found, not impressions.

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder has published significant public criticism of prior
investors or employers, note this in the Risk Assessment and reference
check directly with one of the individuals or firms named — the
subject of the criticism is often the most informative reference
check available and their account of events is a direct test of
whether the founder's public narrative is accurate.

FLAG FB08: DECK LANGUAGE SHIFTS BETWEEN SECTIONS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The language, vocabulary, and analytical sophistication vary
significantly between different sections of the submission deck —
the market sizing section uses institutional investment terminology
and advanced financial modelling that is inconsistent with the
writing style and conceptual level of the problem/solution section,
or vice versa. Observable from a close reading of the submission
for: (1) vocabulary shifts (casual language in the product section,
technical finance language in the financial model); (2) formatting
inconsistency (professional graphic design in some slides, amateur
formatting in others); (3) conceptual sophistication gaps (a
complex bottoms-up market sizing methodology in the TAM slide,
a vague problem statement with no supporting evidence in the
problem slide). The flag strengthens when the founder cannot
fluently discuss the technical or financial content of the
sections that appear authored by someone else.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A deck with inconsistent authorship signals that the founders
have assembled materials from multiple sources — hired consultants,
investor templates, borrowed slides from peers — without fully
internalising the content. This is not a presentation quality
issue. It is a signal about whether the founder can credibly
represent the full depth of their business plan. The specific
risk: an investor who discovers mid-diligence that the founder
cannot explain the assumptions behind their financial model,
or cannot discuss the methodology behind their market sizing,
loses confidence in the accuracy of every other claim in the
materials — because the claims were produced by someone with
more expertise than the founder demonstrably has. The second
signal: a founder who has used external support to produce
a deck they cannot defend is a founder who will use the same
approach when producing investor updates, board materials,
and customer proposals — and the quality of those materials will
decline when external support is not available.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder confirms in written Q&A that specific sections
of the deck were produced by a third party (investment bank,
consultant, pitch deck service) and cannot explain the assumptions
underlying the financial model or the methodology behind the
market sizing — these are the two sections of the deck that
are most predictive of the founder's planning quality, and
inability to defend them after submission is a fundamental
credibility failure.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The language inconsistency is in the design and visual
formatting rather than the substantive content — a founder who
hired a designer to make the deck look professional has not
misrepresented their analytical capability.
(b) The deck clearly attributes specific sections to co-founders
or advisors — a CTO-authored technical architecture section in
a different voice to the CEO-authored go-to-market section is
appropriate and transparent.
(c) The founder can fluently explain and defend all sections
of the deck when asked — the inconsistency was in presentation
style, not in the depth of understanding.

VERIFICATION:
In written Q&A or clarifying question responses, ask the founder
to explain the methodology behind the market sizing and the
key assumptions in the financial model in their own words.
The response should demonstrate that they understand and can
defend the content — not just recite the slide. Ask: "Walk me
through your market sizing methodology — how did you arrive
at the SAM figure?" and "What are the 3 biggest assumptions
in your financial model and how sensitive are the projections
to each one?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When the deck shows significant language or sophistication
inconsistency between sections, ask the founder to explain
the financial model assumptions and market sizing methodology
in writing before advancing — a founder who cannot defend the
analytical content of their own deck in plain language has not
produced the plan themselves, and the quality of execution will
reflect the quality of thinking, not the quality of the consultants.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG FB09: FINANCIAL PROJECTIONS WITH NO ASSUMPTIONS STATED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
A detailed financial model — revenue projections, headcount plan,
unit economics forecast — is provided in the submission but no
assumptions are stated for any of the key growth drivers. Observable
from any financial appendix or model that shows revenue projections
without specifying: (1) the assumed new customer acquisition rate
and how it changes over time; (2) the assumed ACV or price per
customer and whether it changes; (3) the assumed churn rate;
(4) the assumed hiring timeline and the revenue per head ratio
the model implies; (5) the assumed S&M efficiency or CAC payback
period. A financial model with outputs but no inputs is a projection
without a plan.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Financial projections without stated assumptions cannot be evaluated,
challenged, or updated. They are not a plan — they are a target.
The distinction matters: a plan specifies the inputs (hire 3 AEs
per quarter at £120K OTE, each generating £600K ARR in year 2 at
a 12-month ramp) and derives the output from those inputs. A target
specifies the output (£5M ARR by end of year 2) and leaves the
inputs unstated. The investor who receives a target cannot test
whether the assumptions are realistic — because there are no
assumptions to test. The founder who built a target rather than
a plan either does not know what assumptions are required to build
the model (which means the projections are not credible) or knows
but has chosen not to state them (which means they do not want
the assumptions scrutinised). The practical consequence: a company
that cannot articulate its growth assumptions before investment
will not have a clear operational plan post-investment, and will
not be able to explain variance to the plan when actuals diverge
from projection — because there was no plan, only a target.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The financial model shows a >5× revenue increase in year 2 with
no stated assumptions for the growth drivers, and when asked,
the founder cannot identify the specific operational activities
that will produce the revenue step-up — the model is aspirational,
not operational.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The assumptions are available in a separate financial model
file that was not included in the submission deck — common when
founders keep assumptions in a working spreadsheet that they
share in the data room rather than in the deck.
(b) The projections are presented only as an order-of-magnitude
range with explicit acknowledgment that they are illustrative —
a £5M–£10M ARR range by end of year 2, noted as illustrative,
is more honest than an unexplained £7.3M projection.
(c) The company is at a very early stage where a detailed bottom-up
model is not possible — pre-product-market fit companies
legitimately cannot model customer acquisition at the same
specificity as a company with 50 customers and 18 months of
cohort data.

VERIFICATION:
Request the financial model in spreadsheet form with all
assumptions visible. The model should allow Alex to: (1) change
the assumed churn rate and see the revenue impact; (2) change
the assumed AE quota attainment and see the ARR impact; (3) change
the assumed S&M spend and see the CAC payback impact. A model
that is hard-coded rather than assumption-driven is not a
financial model — it is a table of numbers. Ask: "Can you share
the financial model in editable format with assumptions visible?
What is your assumed monthly new customer acquisition rate in
year 1, year 2, and year 3?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a financial model provides revenue projections without stated
assumptions, request the working model in editable format before
evaluating the financial plan — a model with hidden or absent
assumptions cannot be stress-tested, and a founder who cannot
explain the operational levers behind their revenue projection
has not planned the business, only described a desired outcome.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG FB10: RESPONSE TO HARD QUESTIONS IS DEFLECTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
In written Q&A, clarifying question responses, or email exchanges
with the firm, the founder consistently redirects difficult questions
rather than answering them directly. Observable from written responses
that: (1) restate the question and then pivot to a different topic
without addressing the original question; (2) answer a related
but easier question than the one asked; (3) provide extensive
context about why the question is less important than it seems,
without addressing the substance; (4) respond to a specific metric
question by citing a different metric that is more favourable;
(5) promise to follow up with information that is not subsequently
provided. The flag requires a pattern — two or more instances of
deflection in response to different difficult questions.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Deflection in written responses is a high-quality behaviour signal
because it is documentable. Unlike impressions from a verbal meeting,
a written response that deflects a question leaves evidence. The
failure mode the deflection predicts: a founder who deflects hard
questions from an investor during fundraising will deflect hard
questions from the board during governance, and will deflect hard
operational realities from themselves during execution — the same
cognitive pattern operates in all three contexts. The specific
risk to the investor: information that would have been disclosed
during diligence — and might have changed the investment decision —
is withheld through deflection. Post-investment, the same
information surfaces as a surprise in a board meeting, at which
point the investor's recourse is limited. Founders who deflect
are not necessarily hiding something catastrophic. They are often
protecting themselves from a conversation they find uncomfortable.
But the protection comes at the cost of the investor's ability to
make an informed decision, and the pattern reliably produces
information asymmetry that benefits the founder and disadvantages
the investor.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder deflects a direct question about a specific financial
metric — customer churn rate, burn rate, runway, NRR — that is
material to the investment decision and for which the founder
demonstrably has the answer, as evidenced by the same data being
referenced elsewhere in the materials. Deflection on a question
the founder has the answer to is not a communication style issue.
It is a disclosure choice.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The deflection is on a single question that the founder
subsequently answers directly when the question is restated more
specifically — a misunderstanding rather than a pattern.
(b) The founder acknowledges the question directly, explains why
they cannot provide the full answer at this stage (e.g. commercially
sensitive data, ongoing legal process), and offers a specific
alternative that partially addresses the question.
(c) The question is genuinely outside the founder's current
knowledge and they respond with "I don't know but will find out"
and then provide the answer — intellectual honesty about uncertainty
is not deflection.

VERIFICATION:
In the clarifying questions flow, ask two or three questions that
are directly challenging — a question about churn if the deck
does not disclose it, a question about a prior pivot or failure
if the timeline implies one, a question about a gap in the use
of proceeds. Evaluate the responses specifically: (1) did the
founder answer the question asked; (2) did they provide a specific
data point or a narrative; (3) did they promise follow-up data
that was subsequently provided. Document each question and the
nature of the response for the Risk Assessment section.

PATTERN LESSON:
When two or more written responses to direct questions deflect
rather than answer, note this pattern explicitly in the Risk
Assessment and request the specific unanswered data points as
a precondition for advancing — deflection in due diligence is
reliably predictive of information withholding post-investment,
and the questions that were deflected are usually the most
important ones to have answered before capital is committed.

FLAG FB11: PRIOR COMPANY LEGAL DISPUTES NOT DISCLOSED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Publicly available records — Companies House filings, court records
accessible through the Cause List or BAILII, news coverage, or
creditors' voluntary liquidation notices — reveal legal disputes,
CCJs (County Court Judgements), insolvency proceedings, or material
regulatory actions at prior companies associated with the founder,
that the founder has not proactively disclosed in the submission.
Observable by searching: (1) the founder's name in Companies House
for all current and past directorships, then checking each dissolved
company for liquidation circumstances; (2) the founder's name
combined with "lawsuit", "CCJ", "dispute", or "tribunal" in
public search; (3) the founder's name on BAILII for any court
proceedings naming them as a party.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Prior legal disputes are not automatically disqualifying — every
founder who has built more than one company is likely to have
encountered commercial disputes, employment tribunal claims,
or supplier disagreements at some point. The flag triggers on
non-disclosure rather than on the existence of a dispute. A founder
who has experienced a prior business failure involving a legal
dispute and proactively discloses it — explaining what happened,
what they learned, and what they would do differently — demonstrates
the self-awareness and transparency that experienced investors
value. A founder who has the same history and does not disclose
it is either unaware that the information is publicly available
(unlikely at the data room stage) or has made a decision not to
disclose something that a reasonable investor would want to know.
The specific risks: (1) a prior CCJ indicates that the founder
failed to pay a creditor — which is relevant to how they manage
cash and obligations; (2) a prior insolvency involving director
conduct is relevant to governance standards; (3) a prior employment
tribunal involving discrimination or harassment is directly relevant
to the company culture the founder will create.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
A Companies House search reveals that a prior company associated
with the founder was subject to a Compulsory Liquidation (as
opposed to a voluntary liquidation) with documented creditor
losses — and the founder has not disclosed this in the submission.
Compulsory liquidation suggests the company continued trading while
insolvent — which is a directorial conduct issue, not simply a
business failure.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The legal dispute is historical (more than 5 years ago), was
disclosed proactively by the founder, and the founder can provide
a clear account of what happened and what was learned.
(b) The dispute is a commercial arbitration that was resolved
in the company's favour or settled without admission of liability
— disclosed proactively and documented.
(c) The dissolved company was a dormant vehicle or a failed startup
that was wound down cleanly through a members' voluntary liquidation
with no outstanding creditor claims.

VERIFICATION:
Search Companies House for all directorships associated with the
founder's full name. For each dissolved company, check: (1) the
dissolution circumstances (voluntary vs compulsory); (2) the final
accounts filed; (3) any liquidator's report filed as part of the
dissolution. Search BAILII and public court records for any
proceedings naming the founder individually. Ask: "Have you
been involved in any legal disputes, regulatory actions, or
insolvency proceedings at any prior company in any capacity?
Is there anything in your background that you think we should
know about?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a Companies House search reveals prior directorships at
dissolved companies without prior proactive disclosure from
the founder, investigate the dissolution circumstances of each
before advancing — non-disclosure of a publicly accessible
legal or insolvency record is the clearest indicator that the
founder has decided what information to share with investors,
which is a fundamentally different relationship than one founded
on full disclosure.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG FB12: CUSTOMER REFERENCE COACHING
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
Written testimonials, reference customer quotes, or case studies
provided by the company use identical or near-identical language,
emphasis, or superlatives across multiple customers — suggesting
that reference customers were briefed on what to say rather than
speaking independently. Observable from any customer testimonials
section where multiple quotes use the same key phrases ("transformed
our workflow", "10× productivity", "game changer"), the same
structure (problem → solution → outcome), or the same numerical
claims applied to different customers in different verticals.
Also observable when a reference customer, when contacted for an
independent reference check, uses the same framing as the written
testimonial with no variation.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Customer references are the most valuable diligence signal available
at the screening stage — they are independent third parties with
no economic reason to misrepresent their experience. A customer
who has been coached on what to say is not an independent signal.
They are a managed signal, which is functionally the same as no
signal. The coaching may range from providing key talking points
("make sure you mention the time savings and the integration with
Salesforce") to more active preparation ("we have a call before
you speak to the investor to make sure you're comfortable with
the questions"). Both forms of coaching produce reference calls
that confirm the founder's narrative rather than independently
validate it. The tell at the screening stage — before reference
calls are possible — is in the written testimonials. Genuine
customer testimonials are specific, idiosyncratic, and slightly
imperfect — they reference details particular to that customer's
implementation, use the customer's own vocabulary, and often
include a qualification or a suggestion alongside the praise.
Coached testimonials are uniformly positive, use marketing language,
and describe the product in terms that match the company's own
pitch materials.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The company provides multiple written testimonials that are
structurally identical and use the same key phrases, AND when
independently contacted, the reference customers confirm they
were provided with talking points or a template before speaking
to investors. Managed references that misrepresent themselves
as independent are a disclosure failure.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The testimonials are in the customer's own words with
individual specificity — each references their particular use
case, their specific team, and their particular outcome — and
the language variation across customers reflects genuine
independent authorship.
(b) The company discloses upfront that testimonials were submitted
in response to a structured request (e.g. a survey with specific
questions) — structured collection produces some linguistic
similarity and is transparent.
(c) The written testimonials are supplemented by unsolicited
evidence of customer satisfaction: unprompted LinkedIn recommendations,
public product reviews on G2 or Capterra, or case studies produced
by the customer's own marketing team.

VERIFICATION:
Compare written testimonials across customers for: (1) identical
key phrases; (2) identical structure (problem-solution-outcome);
(3) identical numerical claims. Note any testimonials that use
language directly from the company's marketing materials. During
reference calls, ask customers open-ended questions first —
"How would you describe the product to a colleague?" — before
asking the structured questions, to elicit unprepared language.
Compare the unprepared response to the written testimonial.

PATTERN LESSON:
When written testimonials from multiple customers use identical
language or structure, conduct reference calls with open-ended
questions before structured ones — the divergence between
a customer's unprepared description and their coached testimonial
is the most reliable signal of whether the reference is independent
or managed.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
FLAG FB13: FOUNDER DESCRIBES COMPETITION AS "NO COMPETITION"
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The founder explicitly states or strongly implies that there is
no meaningful competition in their market, in a market where:
(1) a 5-minute Crunchbase search identifies funded competitors;
(2) the product category described has established incumbents;
or (3) the customer problem addressed is being solved — by any
means, including manual processes or adjacent software — by a
significant proportion of potential customers. Observable from
any competitive analysis section that identifies zero named
competitors, uses language like "we are creating a new category
with no existing solution," or dismisses all alternatives as
"not truly competitive." See also Flag FB04 (systematic
understatement), which is a related but distinct pattern.

WHAT IT MEANS:
"No competition" in a real market is not a competitive advantage —
it is a market signal. The three explanations for a genuinely
empty competitive landscape are: (1) the market does not exist —
no one has built a solution because there is no viable business
model, insufficient demand, or a regulatory barrier that makes
the category unattractive; (2) the company is in a very early
market where category creation is possible but where the absence
of competition is evidence of risk, not of opportunity; (3) the
founder has not looked. In a functioning startup ecosystem, every
market that has attracted one funded company will attract others.
The founder who claims no competition has either defined their
market so narrowly that competitors who would take their customers
are excluded from the analysis, or has not conducted a meaningful
competitive assessment. Both failures predict the same operational
outcome: the company will encounter competitors in the field
that they did not anticipate, will not have prepared their sales
team to address competitive objections, and will lose deals they
did not know they were at risk of losing.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder claims no competition in a market where a 5-minute
search identifies a well-funded competitor (>£5M raised) with
a directly overlapping product, and the founder cannot explain
why that competitor does not compete with them. This is not
a framing issue — it is a factual error about the market the
company is operating in.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The company is genuinely creating a new category where no
prior commercial solution existed — the claim of no competition
is supported by the absence of results in a thorough search and
by customer testimony that they previously had no tool for the
problem.
(b) The "no competition" claim is limited to a specific sub-segment
that the company has carved out — "no competition for [specific
vertical + specific buyer persona]" — with competitors acknowledged
in the adjacent segments.
(c) The founder distinguishes between direct competition (no
other product does this exact thing) and indirect competition
(customers currently use [alternative] which is not designed
for this purpose) — an honest and useful distinction if clearly
articulated.

VERIFICATION:
Before reviewing the founder's competitive claims, conduct an
independent 5-minute search: Crunchbase for companies in the
same category with raised capital, Google for "[problem] software"
and "[category] platform," and Product Hunt for recent launches
in the space. Then compare the search results to the companies
listed as competitors. Ask: "I found [named company] through
a search — where do they fit in your competitive landscape
and how do you differentiate against them specifically?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder claims no meaningful competition, run an independent
search before accepting the claim — a competitive landscape that
looks empty from the submission but contains funded players
discoverable in 5 minutes is the clearest available signal that
the competitive analysis has not been conducted at a standard
sufficient for the investment decision.

FLAG FB14: SALARY ABOVE MARKET FOR STAGE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: MEDIUM

SIGNAL:
The founder is paying themselves a salary that is materially above
the market rate for the company's current stage and capital position.
Observable from: (1) the use of proceeds section showing a salary
line for founder/CEO that exceeds £100K at seed stage pre-product-
market fit; (2) the financial model showing a founder salary line
that is growing in line with or faster than the company's revenue;
(3) a direct statement by the founder of their current compensation
in the submission or Q&A that exceeds the following stage benchmarks:
pre-seed: £40–70K; seed: £60–90K; Series A: £90–130K; Series B:
£130–200K. These are London/UK benchmarks for 2026. Adjust ±20%
for significantly higher or lower cost-of-living markets.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Founder salary above market for stage is a direct capital allocation
decision — the company is spending investor money to pay the founder
a salary that is above what the stage justifies. The failure modes:
(1) capital depletion: a £150K founder salary at seed stage costs
the company £50–90K more per year than a market-rate salary,
which at a £1M seed round represents 5–9% of raised capital spent
on above-market founder compensation before a single product decision
is made; (2) incentive misalignment: a founder who has de-risked
their personal income relative to the stage has reduced the urgency
that produces strong execution — the company needs to succeed for
the founder to succeed financially, and above-market salary partially
decouples those two things; (3) team morale: a founder who pays
themselves £150K while the first engineers are on £80K has set
a compensation tone that experienced employees notice and that
produces recruiting and retention friction in the subsequent period.
The above-market salary is also informative about the founder's
relationship to capital: they are treating the raised capital as
income replacement rather than as fuel for growth, which predicts
slower burn discipline throughout the business.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The founder is paying themselves a salary above the Series A
benchmark (>£130K) on a pre-seed or seed raise, AND the use
of proceeds shows that salary costs represent more than 30%
of the total raise — the investor's capital is being deployed
primarily to fund founder income, not to fund the business.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The founder has meaningful personal financial obligations
(mortgage, family) and is taking a salary at the lower end of
the market range for stage — this is a reasonable need for
a sustainable personal baseline, not extraction.
(b) The company has raised a Series A or beyond and the founder
salary is in the market range for that stage — above-market salary
is a seed and pre-seed signal, not a late-stage one.
(c) The founder is a technical founder whose salary replaces
a contract engineering cost that the company would otherwise
pay to a third party — a CTO at £100K seed stage who is the
sole engineer is saving the company more than the salary costs.

VERIFICATION:
Request: the use of proceeds breakdown showing all personnel costs
including founder salaries. Compare each founder salary to the
stage benchmark above. Calculate founder salary as a percentage
of the total raise. Ask: "What salary are the founders taking
and how did you determine that level? Has it changed since the
last funding round?"

PATTERN LESSON:
When a founder salary is above the stage benchmark, compare it
as a percentage of the total raise and include this in the
financial analysis — above-market founder compensation at early
stage is a capital efficiency flag, not a personal judgement,
and it compounds: a founder who starts above market typically
increases their salary faster than the business grows.

FLAG FB15: TIMELINE INCONSISTENCIES IN COMPANY HISTORY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CATEGORY: Founder Behaviour
SEVERITY: HIGH

SIGNAL:
The dates in the submission — founding date, first customer date,
first revenue date, prior funding dates — do not form a coherent
chronological narrative when cross-referenced against each other
and against public records. Observable by: (1) comparing the
incorporation date on Companies House against the founding date
stated in the deck; (2) comparing the stated first revenue date
against the product launch date — revenue cannot precede launch;
(3) comparing the stated funding dates against Crunchbase or press
coverage of announced rounds; (4) comparing the stated team join
dates against LinkedIn profile dates for key team members;
(5) checking whether the stated customer acquisition date for
"flagship" customers precedes the stated product launch date.
The flag triggers when two or more dates are materially inconsistent
and the inconsistency cannot be explained by a straightforward
definition difference.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Timeline inconsistencies are the most precisely verifiable form
of narrative inaccuracy — every date can in principle be verified
against an external record. A company that presents inconsistent
dates has either made careless errors in its materials or has
adjusted dates to support a more favourable narrative. Both are
informative. The most common patterns of date adjustment and what
they signal: (1) founding date earlier than incorporation — common
when founders want to appear more experienced; the delta between
"when we started working on this" and "when the company was
incorporated" is legitimate in principle but must be disclosed as
such; (2) first customer before product launch — sometimes explained
by a design partner or beta user, but requires explanation to be
credible; (3) funding dates inconsistent with Crunchbase — often
explained by the difference between close and announcement dates,
but a discrepancy of more than 6 months requires explanation;
(4) team member join dates inconsistent with LinkedIn — the most
common source of unintentional inconsistency, because LinkedIn
profiles are maintained by the individual and not always updated
to reflect current roles accurately. Each inconsistency by itself
is manageable. A pattern of two or more inconsistencies is a flag
about the reliability of the materials as a whole.

DISQUALIFYING WHEN:
The stated "first revenue" date precedes the company's
Companies House incorporation date — a company cannot have
revenue before it legally exists — and the founder cannot
explain the discrepancy with documentation. This is not a
narrative inconsistency. It is a factual impossibility.

MANAGEABLE WHEN:
(a) The inconsistencies are all explained by a single specific
factor — a pivot that redefined "founding" from the original
entity to the current one, an acquisition that brought in a
prior customer relationship, a pre-incorporation trading period
that preceded formal registration — and the explanation is
consistent with the documentary evidence.
(b) The timeline inconsistency is in a public data source
(Crunchbase, LinkedIn) rather than in the company's own
materials — third-party data is less reliable than primary
documents and inconsistencies between third-party sources
and primary documents are lower severity.
(c) The dates are internally consistent within the submission
but inconsistent with a single third-party source — a single
external inconsistency requires explanation but is not a pattern.

VERIFICATION:
Build a timeline table: incorporation date (Companies House),
founding date (deck), first product (deck), first customer (deck),
first revenue (deck), each funding round (deck vs Crunchbase),
key team join dates (deck vs LinkedIn). Flag every date that
is inconsistent across sources. For each inconsistency ask:
"Your deck states [date A] for [event] but [source] shows [date B].
Can you explain the difference?" The response — whether it
provides a clear factual explanation or a defensive narrative —
is itself informative.

PATTERN LESSON:
When two or more dates in the submission are inconsistent with
public records, build a full timeline table comparing deck dates
to Companies House, Crunchbase, and LinkedIn before advancing —
a company whose materials contain multiple verifiable date
errors has not maintained sufficient document discipline to
support the level of due diligence that institutional investment
requires.
